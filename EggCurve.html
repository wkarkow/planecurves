Egg Curve

A collection of plane curves with a single axis of symmetry and an egg-like (ovoid or pyriform) silhouette.

Double egg (polar):
\[ r = a\cos^{2}\theta \]
Hortsch egg (polar):
\[ r = a\,e^{\cos 2\theta}\cos^{2}\theta \]
Granville egg (parametric):
\[ x = b + r\cos t,\quad y = \frac{a r\sin t}{b + r\cos t} \]
Rosillo egg (Cartesian):
\[ y = \pm(b-x)\sqrt{\frac{a^{2}-x^{2}}{c-x}} \]
Bastelein eggs (Cartesian, 4 forms):
\[ y = \pm\frac{2}{3}\sqrt{\frac{1-x^{2}}{1+3ax}},\quad y = \pm\frac{2}{3}\sqrt{(1-x^{2})(1-3ax)},\quad y = \pm\frac{2}{3}\sqrt{(1-x^{2})e^{-3ax}},\quad y = \pm\frac{2}{3}\sqrt{(1-x^{2})\frac{1-3ax}{1+3ax}} \]
Jacobs egg (Cartesian):
\[ y = \pm\sqrt{\frac{1-x^{2}}{a\,b^{x}}} \]
Szego egg (Cartesian):
\[ y = \pm\sqrt{e^{2x-2}-x^{2}-0.02} \]
Descartes egg (implicit):
\[ x^{3}+y^{3}+0.06=3xy \]
Maclaurin egg (Cartesian):
\[ y = \pm\sqrt{\frac{x^{2}(3-x)-0.01}{1+x}} \]
Bernoulli egg (Cartesian):
\[ y = \pm\sqrt{\frac{\sqrt{8x^{2}+0.96}-(2x^{2}+1)}{2}} \]
deSluze egg (Cartesian):
\[ y = \pm\sqrt{\frac{-x^{3}+1.5x^{2}-0.04}{x+0.5}} \]
Sillke egg (Cartesian):
\[ y = \pm\sqrt{\bigl|\sin x + 0.1\sin 2x\bigr|} \]
Blaschke 1 egg (Cartesian):
\[ y = \pm\sqrt{x\bigl(\sqrt{a}-\sqrt{x}\bigr)} \]
Blaschke 2 egg (Cartesian):
\[ y = \pm\sqrt{\dfrac{6.4 - e(x-3)^{2}}{2x}} \]
Skovgaard egg (Cartesian):
\[ y = \pm\sqrt{3\sqrt{2x+1}-2x-3} \]
Babois egg (Cartesian):
\[ y = \pm\sqrt{\dfrac{1}{\varphi}\bigl(0.1 - \bigl(\sqrt{x} - \dfrac{1}{\varphi}\bigr)^{2}\bigr)},\quad \varphi = \dfrac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2} \]
Bouma egg (Cartesian):
\[ y = \pm\sqrt{2^{x}-x^{2}} \]
Neyt egg (Cartesian):
\[ y = \pm\sqrt{x - \dfrac{\cosh x}{e}} \]
Yamamoto egg (Cartesian):
\[ y = \pm\dfrac{1}{2.8}\sqrt{-\ln\bigl(1 + e^{-x} - e^{-4x^{2}}\bigr)} \]

History and Applications

Inventing “egg” curves has long been a cottage industry in recreational mathematics. The collection implemented here is not exhaustive; it gathers classical forms that receive a mild “eggifying” tweak together with a large number of hobbyist constructions that have appeared on mathematical websites, often accompanied by construction notes and dated e-mail contributions. The principal living archives for these shapes are Robert Ferréol’s MathCurve1 and Jürgen Köller’s Mathematische Basteleien.2

The double-egg polar form \(r=a\cos^{2}\theta\) appears on MathCurve with a geometric construction and links to related two-lobed eggs; it is already present in the work of Villalpando (1606) and Münger (1894). A century later Granville (1908/1929) obtained an egg-shaped quartic as the hyperbolism of a circle; the same site records the Euclidean construction (point, line, circle, perpendicular) that yields a modern-looking profile. Nicolas Rosillo’s 2009 locus—built from a circle and two marked points on a diameter—produces a rational quartic that can be tuned into a clean egg silhouette. Köller’s page “Egg Curves and Ovals” collects the Bastelein family (ellipse modulated by a linear, reciprocal or exponential factor), the Jacobs exponential-weight egg, the Szegő form obtained by adding a small constant to an open exponential curve, and a long list of reader contributions (Sillke, Blaschke, Skovgaard, Babois, Bouma, Neyt, Yamamoto3). Several of the entries are simply classical curves—the folium of Descartes, the trisectrix of Maclaurin, the lemniscate of Bernoulli, the conchoid of de Sluze—given a constant offset that closes an otherwise asymptotic or looped figure into a compact oval.

The classical double-egg polar form coincides with the magnetic field lines of a dipole; the orthogonal trajectories are the curves of the companion polar equation.1 No engineering applications have been identified for the remaining recreational forms. A separate scientific literature continues to study the geometry of real avian eggs, producing “universal” formulae intended for biological and poultry-industry use.4 The recreational constructions and the biological models share the same practical spirit: capture a believable egg silhouette with the simplest possible algebraic expression.
