Nielsen's Spiral

Parametric plot of the cosine and sine integrals.

Nielsen's Spiral parametric equations:
\[ x = a\cdot\operatorname{ci}(t)=\gamma+\ln t+\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\cos u-1}{u}\,du \]
\[ y = a\cdot\operatorname{si}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\sin u}{u}\,du-\frac{\pi}{2} \]

History and Applications

Nielsen’s spiral1,2, sometimes called the sici spiral (from sine and cosine integrals), arises from the late-19th / early-20th-century study of special functions defined by integrals, particularly the sine and cosine integrals. These functions had already appeared naturally in problems involving oscillatory behavior with slowly decaying amplitude, such as diffraction, wave propagation, and asymptotic analysis of integrals. The spiral itself is most commonly attributed to Niels Nielsen3 (1865–1931), a Danish mathematician known for his work on special functions and integral representations. Nielsen’s broader research program was focused on understanding how integral-defined functions behave globally, especially their convergence, asymptotics, and geometric structure when interpreted parametrically. By plotting the sine and cosine integrals, one obtains a curve whose geometry directly reflects the accumulation of oscillatory contributions weighted by \(1/t\). The spiral emerges naturally as the cumulative geometry of the sine and cosine integrals when viewed as a trajectory in a plane. Its curvature increases linearly with the parameter and its arc length grows logarithmically, properties that were of direct interest in asymptotic analysis. The direction of the tangent advances essentially linearly with the parameter and the curve winds infinitely many times while converging toward a limiting point. The decay of the integrands as \(1/t\) produces slow convergence and long-range memory, which is visible in the spiral’s gradual tightening. The constant \(\gamma \approx 0.5772156649\) is Euler’s constant (the Euler–Mascheroni constant).

Real-life applications in engineering and the natural sciences are documented in acoustic design. The spiral serves as a profile function for horns in loudspeaker systems, where its geometry supports favorable impedance matching, moderate beaming, and acceptable radiation patterns.4 It also provides an intuitive geometric picture of the sine and cosine integrals, which engineers and physicists encounter as \(\int \sin(t)/t\,dt\) and \(\int \cos(t)/t\,dt\) in signal processing, diffraction theory, and transient response analysis.
