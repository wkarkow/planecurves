Apollonian Cubic

The isoptic cubic (focal of van Rees); locus of points from which two fixed segments subtend equal visual angles, manifesting as one or two branches depending on parameters.

Apollonian Cubic implicit equation:
\[ (x - 2a)(x^{2} + y^{2}) + b^{2} (\cos(2\alpha) \, x + \sin(2\alpha) \, y) = 0 \]
Apollonian Cubic polar equation:
\[ r = \frac{a}{\cos\theta} \pm \sqrt{\frac{a^{2} - b^{2} \cos\theta \cdot \cos(\theta - 2\alpha)}{\cos\theta}} \quad (a \geq 0,\, b > 0,\, 0 \leq \alpha \leq \pi/2) \]

History and Applications

The Apollonian cubic1, also known as the isoptic cubic or focal of van Rees, is the locus of points P from which two given line segments AB and CD subtend equal angles. This geometric property was first explored by Hendrik van Rees, a Dutch mathematician active in the early 19th century, in his 1829 work on foci of conic sections. Independently, Jacob Steiner, a Swiss mathematician renowned for his contributions to projective geometry (1805–1858), formulated a similar problem in 1852 without knowledge of van Rees’ earlier results. The equivalence between their approaches was noted by Francisco Gomes Teixeira, a Portuguese mathematician (1851–1933), in 1915. The curve gained popularity in the 19th century, attracting studies from mathematicians such as Henri Brocard (known for Brocard points), Jules Henri Poincaré’s teacher Charles Chasles, Germinal Pierre Dandelin (famous for Dandelin spheres in conics), Gaston Darboux (noted for differential geometry), and George Salmon (author of influential treatises on conic sections and algebraic geometry). In modern times, a group at the University of Crete led by Paris Pamfilos has advanced research on these cubics, dubbing them "isoptic cubics" and developing the Isoptikon tool for interactive visualization given two segments.

The curve can manifest as one or two branches depending on parameters; it is elliptical when a² > 3b², forms an oval or branch under other conditions, and degenerates to a rational strophoid when a = 0. Its isoptic nature—defining points where the visual angle to a fixed segment remains constant—finds application in optics, such as modeling loci for equal angular subtension in vision geometry or lens design2.

Real-life applications in engineering and natural sciences are primarily found in optics. The isoptic property supports design of systems where constant visual angle or equal subtended angles are required, such as certain lens or mirror configurations and vision-related geometric modeling. No broader engineering uses outside this optical context were identified.
