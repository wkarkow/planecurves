Ramphoid Curve

A plane algebraic quartic with a ramphoid (beak-like, second-kind) cusp at the origin.

Ramphoid Curve parametric equations:
\[ x = a\cdot t^{4},\qquad y = a(t^{2}+t^{3}) \]
Generalized parametric equations:
\[ x = a\cdot t^{b},\qquad y = a(t^{c}+t^{d}) \]

History and Applications

The ramphoid curve1,2, also known as the ramphoid cusp, is a plane algebraic curve featuring a cuspidal point of the second kind. In this type of cusp the two branches of the curve lie on the same side of the common tangent line at the singular point, which distinguishes it from ordinary cusps like the semicubical parabola. Leonhard Euler proposed the curve in 1744 in a letter to Gabriel Cramer dated October 20, 1744. Euler presented it to demonstrate that such cusps could exist in algebraic curves, contrary to Cramer’s earlier belief that they could not. This led Cramer to include the concept in his 1750 treatise Introduction à l'analyse des lignes courbes algébriques, crediting Euler to illustrate “beak-like reversal” in quartics. The 1744–1745 correspondence resolved tensions between geometric intuition and algebraic analysis, with Euler noting how equations reveal singularities geometry might miss. In 1750, Cramer debated Jean d'Alembert on “inflection points” in quartics, where tangents intersect four times, defending their algebraic reality via series expansions and noting “invisible” inflections at origins visible only through calculation. Cramer's approach shifted from 1744 geometric refutations to 1750 algebraic proofs in the era's debate on visualizing versus computing singularities in plane curves. “Ramphoid” means “bird’s beak,” from the Greek ramphos and was coined in 1809 by the French mathematician Louis-Benjamin Francoeur, who also named cusps of the first kind “keratoid.”

In algebraic geometry the ramphoid cusp serves as the simplest example of a second-order cusp. Real-life applications in engineering or the natural sciences for this specific curve are not documented. Its value remains theoretical, primarily in the study of singular points of plane algebraic curves.

AutoLISP Code

(defun C:RAMPHOID (/ basept xaxisangle cd sa sb sm om os divcount cosangle sinangle x0 y0 plineents segcount baseinfo paramstep
                     divpt xspan restartflag id iq dcl opt kw mode dpr dct dpt span eq_display xc yc a1 a2 x1 y1 x2 y2 fa fb fc fd fe)
  (setq id "Ramphoid Curve"
        iq "x=a·tᵇ, y=a·(tᶜ+tᵈ)"
        dcl nil
        opt nil
        kw 0
        mode 'param
        dpr "# of divisions to x-span of division point"
        dct 500
        dpt '(5.0 0.0)
        span nil
        kw (ca id iq dcl opt kw mode dpr dct dpt span)
        fa (get_real 2 "a (scale)" 1.0)
        fb (get_real 2 "b (x exponent)" 4.0)
        fc (get_real 0 "c (y first exponent)" 2.0)
        fd (get_real 2 "d (y second exponent)" 3.0)
        fe (get_keyword 0 "Positive Negative" "Positive or Negative t?" "Positive")
        eq_display (strcat "\na=" (rtos fa 2 4) ", b=" (rtos fb 2 4) ", c=" (rtos fc 2 4) ", d=" (rtos fd 2 4))
        xc '(* fa (alt_expt (eval a2) fb nil))
        yc '(* fa (+ (alt_expt (eval a2) fc nil) (alt_expt (eval a2) fd nil)))
        paramstep (/ (alt_expt (/ xspan fa) (/ 1.0 fb) nil) divcount)
        paramstep (if (eq fe "Negative") (- paramstep) paramstep)
        skm '(T T T)
        a1 0
        a2 a1
        x1 (eval xc)
        y1 (eval yc)
        a2 '(+ a1 paramstep)
        x2 '(eval xc)
        y2 '(eval yc))
  (cc))
