Cayley Oval

A bicircular quartic plane algebraic curve of an equipotential contour for two equal point sources in electrostatics or gravitation.

Cayley Oval parametric equations:
\[ x = \frac{b^{2} \cos(2t)}{a \sin^{4}(2t)}, \quad y = \pm \sqrt{ \frac{b^{2}}{4 \cos^{4}(t)} - (x - a)^{2} }, \quad 0 < t < \frac{\pi}{2} \]

History and Applications

The Cayley Oval is named after Arthur Cayley, a leading figure in 19th-century algebraic geometry who helped classify higher-degree plane curves and their invariants1. Although it appears in mathematical literature as a bicircular quartic, its deeper origin lies in the study of potential theory, where curves arise naturally as level sets of physical fields.

In particular, the Cayley Oval can be interpreted as a planar equipotential curve associated with two equal point sources. During the 18th and 19th centuries, mathematicians investigating electrostatics and gravitation examined such level curves to understand how scalar potentials vary in space2. Unlike conic sections—defined by linear relations in distances to foci—the Cayley Oval involves higher-order combinations of those distances, producing a quartic relation that allows for closed, lens-like shapes depending on the chosen potential level.

The primary physical interpretation of the Cayley Oval is as an equipotential contour. In electrostatics, the potential of a point charge varies as V ∝ 1/r, and equipotential curves represent locations of equal potential energy. For two equal charges, certain constant-potential contours form closed ovals, one of which corresponds to the Cayley Oval2,3. Because gravitational potential has the same inverse-distance dependence, Φ ∝ −1/r, the same geometry applies directly to gravity. The Cayley Oval can therefore be interpreted as a constant gravitational potential curve for two equal masses. In this context it separates regions of space with different energy levels; the gravitational field (force) is everywhere normal to the curve, and objects do not move along the curve, but instead accelerate across it. This places the Cayley Oval within the same conceptual framework as zero-velocity curves and energy boundaries in classical mechanics4. While actual trajectories are determined by Newton’s laws, the equipotential geometry defines how potential energy converts into kinetic energy. A well-known cultural example appears in The Moon Is a Harsh Mistress, where projectiles (“rods from God”) exploit gravitational potential energy by falling through Earth’s gravitational field5.

In engineering contexts the curve illustrates the geometry of equipotential surfaces in field problems (electrostatic shielding, gravitational assist trajectories, or potential flow analogs). It remains primarily of interest in classical algebraic geometry and as a teaching example of how quartic relations emerge from simple physical inverse-square laws.
