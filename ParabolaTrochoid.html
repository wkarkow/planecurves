Parabola Trochoid

No-slip roulette of a circle on a parabolic base, and free-ratio central-curve variant with independent spin.

No-slip (parabola-base) equations:
\[ \mathbf{M}=M_0+a\mathbf{N}+d\mathbf{U},\quad\phi=s/a,\quad\mathbf{U}=\mathrm{rot}(\phi)\mathbf{N} \]
Parabola and arc length:
\[ M_0=(t,\,a t^{2}),\quad s=\int\sqrt{1+(2 a t)^{2}}\,dt \]
Free-ratio (central) equations:
\[ \mathbf{M}=M_0+d\,(\cos(ks),\sin(ks)) \]

History and Applications

The parabola-base trochoid (no-slip) and the parabola-central trochoid (free-ratio) are modern generalizations of the classical trochoid.1 The classical trochoid itself—the path of a point fixed to a circle that rolls without slipping on a straight line—was studied by Albrecht Dürer (1525) and Ole Rømer (1674); Gilles de Roberval coined the name from the Greek trochos (wheel) in connection with the cycloid.2 The broader theory of roulettes (curves traced by a point attached to one curve as it rolls on another) developed in the seventeenth century around the cycloid and its relatives, with contributions from Roberval, Pascal, Huygens, Leibniz, Newton, and the Bernoullis.3

When the fixed base is a circle one obtains the centered trochoids (epitrochoids and hypotrochoids). When the fixed base is an arbitrary curve and the rolling curve is a circle, the result is the any-based trochoid presented by Robert Ferréol on MathCurve.1 The parabolic-base specimen is the special case in which the fixed curve is a parabola; the free-ratio (central-curve) variant on the same page is the related construction in which the center of the disk follows a prescribed path while the disk spins at an independent constant ratio.1

No classical attribution attaches specifically to the parabolic-base case. The construction appears in modern computational and educational contexts (Wolfram Demonstrations, MathStackExchange discussions of the elementary arc-length formula that arises for the parabola) rather than in the historical literature of named curves.4,5 Related classical roulettes involving the parabola are well known and distinct: the focus of a parabola rolling on a straight line traces a catenary, and the vertex of one parabola rolling on an equal parabola traces the cissoid of Diocles.6 Those constructions are not the circle-on-parabola roulette drawn here.

Real-life applications of the exact no-slip circle-on-parabola roulette (or of its free-ratio central variant) are not documented. Broader trochoidal and epitrochoidal paths appear in the design of rotary pumps, Wankel-type engines, and certain gear-generation processes, and looping tool-paths derived from the classical trochoid family are used in contemporary CNC trochoidal (or cycloidal) milling to raise material-removal rates while limiting tool engagement.7 These uses, however, employ circular-base or free-form trochoids, not the pure no-slip roulette of a circle on a parabolic base. The free-ratio central-curve version likewise lacks specific engineering applications beyond its role as a kinematic illustration.

                 (setq ff (+ ff paramstep)
                       fi (sqrt (+ 1.0 (* 4.0 fa fa ff ff)))
                       fg (+ fg (* fi paramstep))

                       fs (if (eq fe "Positive") -1.0 1.0)

                       fk (* fh (/ 1.0 fi))
                       fl (* fs (/ fg fc))
