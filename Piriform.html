Piriform Curve

A quartic algebraic curve of teardrop or pear outline, also called the pear-shaped quartic or peg-top curve.

Piriform Curve Cartesian equation:
\[ a^{4} y^{2} = b^{2} x^{3} (2a - x) \]
Piriform Curve parametric equations:
\[ x = a\bigl(1 + \sin(t)\bigr),\quad y = b \cdot \cos(t) \cdot \bigl(1 + \sin(t)\bigr) \]

History and Applications

The Piriform curve1,2,3, also known as the pear-shaped quartic or peg-top curve, derives its name from the Latin pirum, meaning “pear.” Other descriptive names include drop of water. The curve is a quartic algebraic curve with a distinctive teardrop or pear shape. It was studied by Wallis in 1685 and by Bonnet in 1844, with further analysis by French mathematician G. de Longchamps in 1886.1,2

Real-life applications are limited. The curve serves as a model in topology, where a tube whose bore follows the piriform quartic cross-section represents one construction of the Klein bottle.2 It has also been applied in astrophysics to model the theoretical vertical intensity profile of radiation through optically thin interstellar media, reproducing the characteristic “U” shape observed in structures such as the local bubble, W4 superbubble, and Fermi bubbles.4 In early 20th-century aerodynamics, especially in subsonic flow, researchers sought axisymmetric bodies that delayed boundary-layer separation, minimized pressure (form) drag, and avoided abrupt curvature changes near the tail. Pear-shaped longitudinal profiles emerged naturally in this work. Authors such as Theodore von Kármán and Ludwig Prandtl discussed families of smoothly tapering bodies similar to piriform sections when revolved. The key idea was gradual deceleration of the flow near the rear, which the piriform’s long tail accomplishes. In the 1920s–1950s, especially in naval hydrodynamics and airship research, “pear-shaped” solids were analyzed as practical approximations to optimal drag bodies, often compared with prolate spheroids, von Kármán ogives, and Sears–Haack bodies (later, supersonic). Although the piriform quartic was rarely enforced as a design constraint, its shape characteristics including blunt front, maximum thickness forward of mid-length, and long taper were deliberately sought but superseded by later research.

AutoLISP Code

(defun C:PIRIFORM (/ basept xaxisangle cd sa sb sm om os divcount cosangle sinangle x0 y0 plineents segcount baseinfo paramstep
                     divpt restartflag id iq dcl opt kw mode dpr dct dpt span eq_display xc yc skm a1 a2 x1 y1 x2 y2 fa fb)
  (setq id "Piriform Curve"
        iq "x=a·(1+sin(t)), y=b·cos(t)·(1+sin(t))"
        dcl nil
        opt nil
        kw nil
        mode 'polar
        dpr "# of divisions in curve"
        dct 360
        dpt nil
        span 360
        kw (ca id iq dcl opt kw mode dpr dct dpt span)
        fa (get_real 2 "a (x scale)" 1.0)
        fb (get_real 2 "b (y scale)" 1.0)
        eq_display (strcat "\na=" (rtos fa 2 4) ", b=" (rtos fb 2 4))
        xc '(* fa (+ 1.0 (sin (eval a2))))
        yc '(* fb (cos (eval a2)) (+ 1.0 (sin (eval a2))))
        skm '(T T T)
        a1 0
        a2 a1
        x1 (eval xc)
        y1 (eval yc)
        a2 '(+ a1 paramstep)
        x2 '(eval xc)
        y2 '(eval yc))
  (cc))
