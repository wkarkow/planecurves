Oxyhemoglobin Dissociation Curve

The sigmoid Hill relation between oxygen partial pressure \(p_{O_2}\) and percent hemoglobin saturation \(s_{O_2}\).

Oxyhemoglobin Dissociation Curve Cartesian equation:
\[ s{O_2} = \dfrac{100 \times (p{O_2})^{n}}{(P_{50})^{n} + (p{O_2})^{n}} \]

History and Applications

The oxyhemoglobin dissociation curve1, modeled by the Hill equation, quantifies the relationship between partial pressure of oxygen (\(p{O_2}\)) and hemoglobin oxygen saturation (\(s{O_2}\)). In 1910, British physiologist Archibald Vivian Hill formulated the empirical equation to fit the experimentally observed sigmoidal binding curve of oxygen to hemoglobin.2 The characteristic S-shape results from positive cooperative binding. Hemoglobin is a tetramer with four heme groups. Binding of the first \(\mathrm{O}_2\) molecule induces a conformational change from the tense (T) to relaxed (R) state, increasing affinity for subsequent \(\mathrm{O}_2\) molecules. The Hill coefficient \(n \approx 2.8\) for adult human hemoglobin quantifies this cooperativity; \(n > 1\) produces the steep central portion of the curve that allows efficient oxygen loading in the lungs and unloading in tissues.3 In healthy adults at sea level and normal body temperature, arterial blood (\(Pa{O_2} \approx 95\)–\(100\,\mathrm{mmHg}\)) averages 97 % saturation. Mixed venous blood (\(Pv{O_2} \approx 40\,\mathrm{mmHg}\)) shows approximately 75 % saturation at rest, corresponding to extraction of roughly 25 % of delivered oxygen.

Modern arterial blood gas (ABG) analyzers determine oxygen saturation by integrated co-oximetry.4 This technique hemolyzes the blood sample and applies multi-wavelength spectrophotometry. Each hemoglobin species (oxy-, deoxy-, carboxy-, and methemoglobin) has a unique absorption spectrum; light absorption at four or more specific wavelengths directly yields the fractional concentrations and thus the true saturation. This measured value is more accurate than saturation calculated from \(PO_2\) alone, especially when dyshemoglobins are present.

The curve and its shifts (Bohr effect from pH, \(pCO_2\), temperature, and 2,3-DPG) are central to respiratory physiology, critical care, anesthesiology, and pulmonology. Clinicians use it daily to interpret ABG results, manage hypoxemia, adjust mechanical ventilation, and assess \(\mathrm{O}_2\) delivery in conditions such as anemia, high altitude, or shock.
