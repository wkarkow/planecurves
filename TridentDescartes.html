Trident of Descartes

A rational cubic, also called the Cartesian parabola, with a vertical asymptote at \( x = 0 \) and an oblique asymptote.

Trident of Descartes implicit equation:
\[ a \cdot xy = (a+x)(a-x)(2a-x) \]
Trident of Descartes parametric equations:
\[ x = a \cdot t,\quad y = a \cdot \frac{(1-t^{2})(2-t)}{t} \]

History and Applications

The Trident of Descartes1,2, also known as the Cartesian parabola, is the cubic curve developed by the French mathematician and philosopher René Descartes (1596–1650) as a tool for the geometric construction of solutions to sixth-degree algebraic equations. In La Géométrie (1637) and his later work on the construction of roots, Descartes demonstrated how higher-degree polynomial equations could be reduced to the intersection of simpler curves. The trident served as one such auxiliary curve: by choosing appropriate parameters, the intersection of the trident with a circle yields the roots of a sextic equation. This approach belonged to Descartes’ broader program of replacing purely algebraic manipulation with geometric constructions. Isaac Newton encountered curves of this type while preparing his own classification of cubic equations in the late seventeenth century. In notes later published in 1710, Newton remarked on the visual similarity between his trident and the curve Descartes had employed for sextic constructions3, although the two curves are mathematically distinct. The name “trident” itself is generally attributed to Newton, who likened its three-pronged appearance to the classical weapon. Later historians of mathematics have sometimes referred to Descartes’ version as the Cartesian parabola to emphasize its origin and purpose, even though it is not a parabola in the strict sense. The curve is a rational cubic possessing a vertical asymptote at \( x = 0 \) and an oblique asymptote. It can be generated through projective transformations of simpler cubics and exhibits a point at infinity that behaves like a double point. These properties made it a useful example in classical studies of algebraic curves of degree three.

Real-life applications in engineering and the natural sciences are limited. Its primary documented use was historical: as a geometric instrument for solving sixth-degree polynomial equations in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, prior to the development of modern symbolic algebra and numerical methods. In contemporary mathematics, the curve appears mainly in texts on the history of analytic geometry and as a standard example of a rational cubic with specific asymptotic behavior. No significant applications in modern engineering disciplines (such as structural mechanics, optics, mechanism design, or trajectory analysis) are recorded in standard references.

(defun C:TRIDENTDESCARTES (/ basept xaxisangle cd sa sb sm om os divcount cosangle sinangle x0 y0 plineents segcount baseinfo paramstep
                             divpt xspan restartflag id iq dcl opt kw mode dpr dct dpt span eq_display xc yc skm a1 a2 x1 y1 x2 y2 fa)
  (setq id "Trident of Descartes"
        iq "a·xy=(a+x)(a-x)(2a-x)"
        dcl "TRIDENTDESCARTES"
        opt '("Positive branch" "Negative branch")
        kw 0
        mode 'param
        dpr "# of divisions to x-span of division point"
        dct 500
        dpt '(5.0 0.0)
        span nil
        kw (ca id iq dcl opt kw mode dpr dct dpt span)
        fa (get_real 2 "a (scale)" 1.0)
        eq_display (strcat "\na=" (rtos fa 2 4))
        paramstep (/ (abs (/ xspan fa)) divcount)
        paramstep (if (= kw 1) (- paramstep) paramstep)
        xc '(* fa (eval a2))
        yc '(* fa (/ (* (- 1.0 (* (eval a2) (eval a2))) (- 2.0 (eval a2))) (avoid_zero (eval a2))))
        skm '(T T T)
        a1 0
        a2 a1
        x1 (eval xc)
        y1 (eval yc)
        a2 '(+ a1 paramstep)
        x2 '(eval xc)
        y2 '(eval yc))
  (cc))
