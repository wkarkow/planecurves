Poisson Distribution

Four Poisson distribution plots: the discrete probability mass function, a Gaussian approximation of that mass function, the cumulative distribution function, and a Gaussian approximation of the cumulative function.

Probability Mass Function (PMF):
\[ y = a \cdot \dfrac{\lambda^{x}\, e^{-\lambda}}{x!},\quad x=0,1,2,\ldots \]
Gaussian PMF Approximation:
\[ y \approx \dfrac{a}{\sqrt{2\pi\lambda}}\, e^{-\frac{(x-\lambda)^{2}}{2\lambda}},\quad \mu=\lambda,\; \sigma^{2}=\lambda \]
Cumulative Distribution Function (CDF):
\[ y = a \cdot e^{-\lambda} \sum_{i=0}^{\lfloor x \rfloor} \dfrac{\lambda^{i}}{i!} \]
Gaussian CDF Approximation:
\[ y = a \cdot \Phi\!\left(\dfrac{x+0.5-\lambda}{\sqrt{\lambda}}\right),\quad \Phi(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{x} \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\, e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{2}}\, dt \]
Ramanujan’s Approximation:
\[ n! \approx \sqrt{\pi}\left(\dfrac{n}{e}\right)^{n}\bigl(8n^{3}+4n^{2}+n+\tfrac{1}{30}\bigr)^{\frac{1}{6}},\quad\text{approximates } n!=\Gamma(n+1)\text{ for non-integers} \]

History and Applications

The Poisson distribution1,2, named after the French mathematician, physicist, and engineer Siméon Denis Poisson (1781–1840), gives the probability of a given number of independent events occurring in a fixed interval of time or space when those events happen at a known constant average rate and independently of the time since the last event. Poisson first presented the distribution in 1837 in his treatise Recherches sur la probabilité des jugements en matière criminelle et en matière civile, where he derived it as the limiting case of the binomial distribution for a very large number of trials with a very small probability of success on each trial while their product λ remains constant. An earlier related idea had appeared in Abraham de Moivre’s work, but Poisson’s formal treatment established the distribution that now bears his name. To compute non-integer values for the smooth rather than step-wise curves, Ramanujan’s factorial approximation did not arise from extending Stirling’s series term by term. Instead, it appears to have been constructed by folding multiple asymptotic corrections into a single algebraic expression, so that its expansion matches the known behavior of \(\ln(n!)\) to unusually high order. This reflects Ramanujan’s characteristic style: recognizing patterns first and encoding them compactly, often without formal derivation. It is used in this code for the PMF and CMF functions.

Real-life applications of the Poisson distribution are well established in engineering and the natural sciences. In physics it models radioactive decay counts and the arrival of photons or elementary particles at detectors; the same model applies in nuclear physics experiments and in astronomy for the number of cosmic-ray events or stars counted in a sky region. In engineering it is used for reliability analysis to predict the number of defects per unit length or area in manufactured items, the frequency of system failures, and the arrival rates of calls at telephone exchanges or vehicles at traffic signals. Biologists apply it to count rare mutations or the number of organisms found in a fixed volume of habitat.
