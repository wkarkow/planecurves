Hyperbolic Functions

The six classical hyperbolic functions (csch, cosh, coth, sech, sinh, tanh) plotted as plane curves y = a · f(x).

Hyperbolic cosecant Cartesian equation:
\[ y = a\,\mathrm{csch}(x) = \dfrac{a}{\sinh(x)} \]
Hyperbolic cosine Cartesian equation:
\[ y = a\,\cosh(x) = a\dfrac{e^{x}+e^{-x}}{2} \]
Hyperbolic cotangent Cartesian equation:
\[ y = a\,\coth(x) = a\dfrac{\cosh(x)}{\sinh(x)} = \dfrac{a}{\tanh(x)} \]
Hyperbolic secant Cartesian equation:
\[ y = a\,\mathrm{sech}(x) = \dfrac{a}{\cosh(x)} \]
Hyperbolic sine Cartesian equation:
\[ y = a\,\sinh(x) = a\dfrac{e^{x}-e^{-x}}{2} \]
Hyperbolic tangent Cartesian equation:
\[ y = a\,\tanh(x) = a\dfrac{\sinh(x)}{\cosh(x)} \]

History and Applications

The six hyperbolic functions sinh, cosh, tanh, csch, sech, and coth serve as analogs to the ordinary trigonometric functions.1,2,3 Vincenzo Riccati introduced them formally in 1757 using the notations Sh. and Ch. for hyperbolic sine and cosine. Johann Heinrich Lambert systematized their use in the 1760s, providing exponential definitions and the modern abbreviations that remain in use today. These functions arise naturally from solutions to problems in mathematical physics involving the exponential function and parallel ordinary trigonometric functions, but arise from the geometry of the hyperbola.

The hyperbolic cosecant originated with the 18th-century formalization of hyperbolic functions by Riccati and Lambert. The csch curve exhibits a sharp spike at the origin and decays rapidly toward zero on either side, resembling the reciprocal of an exponential wave. Csch(x) is useful in modeling wave attenuation, electric field decay in plasma physics, and heat transfer problems where the solution of the differential equation involves sinh(x) and its reciprocal.

The hyperbolic cosine forms the basis of the catenary, the curve assumed by an ideal flexible chain suspended under its own weight. The underlying curve was studied as early as Galileo and Huygens, and its analytic expression using cosh(x) was formalized by Euler in the mid-18th century. Engineers employ this shape in the design of suspension bridges, power transmission lines, and related structures to ensure uniform tension and minimal material use. The Gateway Arch in St. Louis, Missouri, is built as an inverted catenary to achieve optimal performance under compression.

The hyperbolic cotangent appeared in Lambert’s 1766 Theorie der Parallellinien, in hyperbolic geometry and in exponential-based differential equations. It arises in physical modeling where ratios of exponential growth and decay dominate, for example in the current-voltage relationship of thermionic emission, in certain solutions to the diffusion equation, in describing relaxation phenomena in thermal systems, and in visualizing boundary layers.

The hyperbolic secant was introduced by Riccati, and often appears in closed-form analytic solutions of the nonlinear Schrödinger equation and the Korteweg–de Vries (KdV) equation, where sech-squared profiles model solitary wave pulses. In engineering and applied physics, the sech curve is therefore central to soliton theory, fiber-optic signal transmission, and waveguide field distributions. It is a smoother, exponentially based cousin of the Gaussian curve.

The hyperbolic sine is the foundation of all hyperbolic functions. It first appeared in the 1760s in Euler’s and Lambert’s analytic works and corresponds to the y-coordinate of a point on the unit hyperbola parameterized by a hyperbolic angle. Sinh(x) arises in the solution of linear differential equations describing steady-state heat conduction, beam deflection, and electric potential in uniform media, next most frequently encountered after sine and cosine.

The hyperbolic tangent was a central function in 19th-century studies of hyperbolic geometry and later gained prominence in electronics and control theory as a convenient analog of the logistic curve. Tanh(x) models saturation in amplifiers and neural activation functions in artificial intelligence, where its bounded output provides nonlinear compression of large inputs. In physics it describes magnetization (the Langevin function approximation), temperature-dependent transition curves, and viscous velocity profiles. In plots, it serves as an ideal sigmoid transition curve.
