Sectrix of MacLaurin

Locus of the intersection of two lines that revolve at constant but different angular speeds about two fixed poles.

Sectrix of MacLaurin polar equation:
\[ r = a \cdot \dfrac{\sin(\theta_{0}+k\theta)}{\sin(\theta_{0}+(k-1)\theta)} \]

History and Applications

The sectrix of MacLaurin1,2 is the locus of the intersection point of two lines revolving at constant but different angular speeds about two fixed poles. Colin Maclaurin (1698–1746), the Scottish mathematician, first studied the curve in 1742 while investigating the classical Greek problem of angle trisection; the special case with speed ratio 3:1 yields the Maclaurin trisectrix that bears his name. The family was later examined by Joseph Plateau in 1828 and A. B. Kempe in 1895 and is also known as the Plateau sectrix, isocyclotomic curve, or (for certain parameters) arachnida or spider curve. When the ratio of angular speeds \(q = m/n\) (in lowest terms) is rational, the curve is algebraic and functions as an \(m\)-sectrix, \(n\)-sectrix, and \((m-n)\)-sectrix. This property permits the graphical division of an arbitrary angle into \(m\), \(n\), or \(m-n\) equal parts by compass-and-straightedge constructions once the curve has been drawn. For irrational \(q\) the curve is non-algebraic and dense in the plane. Each member of the family belongs to an inversive triplet: the inverses of any sectrix with respect to either pole are again sectrices of MacLaurin.

Real-life applications exist in engineering. In computer-aided geometric design, extended forms of the sectrix of MacLaurin (with two shape parameters) generate interpolating curve segments possessing larger regions of monotonic curvature than quadratic Bézier curves.3 This property is useful for producing smooth, aesthetically pleasing paths in CAD systems without unwanted curvature oscillations.
