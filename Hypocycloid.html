Hypocycloid

The locus of a point on the circumference of a circle that rolls without slipping inside a fixed circle.

Hypocycloid parametric equations:
\[ x=(R-r)\cos\theta + r\cdot\cos\left(\dfrac{R-r}{r}\theta\right),\quad y=(R-r)\sin\theta - r\cdot\sin\left(\dfrac{R-r}{r}\theta\right) \]
Generalized parametric equations:
\[ x=a\cos\theta + b\cos(c\theta),\quad y=d\sin\theta - e\sin(f\theta) \]

History and Applications

The hypocycloid is the plane curve traced by a fixed point on the circumference of a small circle of radius r that rolls inside a fixed larger circle of radius R without slipping.1,2 The 2-cusped case (R/r = 2) produces a straight-line segment known as the Tusi couple; Persian astronomer and mathematician Nasir al-Din al-Tusi (1201–1274) first described it in the 13th century.1 European mathematicians later examined the general family: Albrecht Dürer (1525), Ole Rømer (1674), and Daniel Bernoulli (1725).2 Special integer ratios R/r yield the deltoid (3 cusps), astroid (4 cusps), and n-cusped hypocycloids. The general parametric form used in the code covers all cases by varying the coefficients a, b, c, d, e, f.

Real-life applications in engineering and the natural sciences are documented. In mechanical engineering the hypocycloid profile forms the inner rotor of gerotor and trochoidal pumps, producing sealed, expanding/contracting chambers that deliver smooth, pulsation-free flow of oils and viscous fluids at low speeds with high torque.3 Similar hypocycloidal gearing appears in compact planetary drives and internal-combustion-engine mechanisms that replace conventional slider-crank designs for improved efficiency.4 The astroid (4-cusped hypocycloid) defines the envelope traced by bus-door mechanisms, allowing the door to open while minimizing the swept volume on the ground.5 In physics the curve solves a generalized brachistochrone problem under a central force field proportional to 1/r; this models the shape of a gravity-assisted tunnel drilled through a uniform sphere that minimizes transit time between any two surface points.1,2 Related mathematics describes small oscillations of the Foucault pendulum.2
