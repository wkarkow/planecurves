Inverse Gudermannian

Graph of the inverse Gudermannian function; supplies the vertical coordinate (isometric latitude) of the Mercator map projection from geographic latitude.

Inverse Gudermannian Cartesian equation:
\[ y = a \cdot \ln\bigl(\sec(x) + \tan(x)\bigr) \qquad \bigl(|x| < \pi/2\bigr) \]
Integral form:
\[ y = a \int_0^x \sec(t)\,dt \]
Equivalent closed form:
\[ y = a \cdot \operatorname{arsinh}\bigl(\tan(x)\bigr) \]

History and Applications

The inverse Gudermannian1,2 is the real-valued function that undoes the Gudermannian. Its defining expression is the classical antiderivative of the secant function. Gerardus Mercator introduced his map projection in 1569 so that lines of constant compass bearing (rhumb lines) would appear as straight lines. The vertical coordinate required for that property is proportional to the integral of the secant of the latitude. Edward Wright (1599) showed how to construct the projection numerically from ordinary trigonometric tables; James Gregory published the closed form in 1668.

Johann Heinrich Lambert studied the same integral while developing the hyperbolic functions in the 1760s and called the related function the “transcendent angle.” Christoph Gudermann (1798–1852) examined the detailed connections between circular and hyperbolic functions in the 1830s. In 1862 Arthur Cayley named the function after Gudermann, introduced the notation gd, and noted that the Gudermannian is the inverse of the integral of the secant. The inverse itself is therefore sometimes called the Lambertian or the anti-gudermannian. In navigation and geodesy the quantity \(\operatorname{gd}^{-1}(\phi)\) (scaled by a constant) was long known as the meridional part or isometric latitude of the geographic latitude \(\phi\).

The graph of the inverse Gudermannian appears among the classical transition curves used for railway and highway alignments. When curvature must change continuously from a straight line to a circular arc, one of the traditional radioids is precisely this curve (the pseudo-elliptic radioid).3 Its arc length can be expressed with elementary functions.

The principal modern use remains the Mercator projection and its variants in cartography and navigation. On a standard Mercator chart the vertical distance from the equator to a parallel of latitude \(\phi\) is proportional to \(\operatorname{gd}^{-1}(\phi)\). The same relation converts between geographic latitude and isometric latitude in conformal map projections. Secondary appearances occur in the geometry of hyperbolic space and in the classical tables that once allowed a single set of circular-function values to serve also for hyperbolic functions.
