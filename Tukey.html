Tukey's Biweight

The influence (\(\psi\)) function of a robust M-estimator.

Tukey's Biweight Cartesian equation:
\[ y = x\left(1-\left(\dfrac{x}{c}\right)^{2}\right)^{2} \text{ for } |x| \lt c,\quad y = 0 \text{ for } |x| \ge c \]

History and Applications

Tukey's biweight, also known as the bisquare function, is the influence (\(\psi\)) function of a robust M-estimator widely used to limit the effect of outliers during regression and location estimation. It was introduced by Albert E. Beaton and John W. Tukey in their 1974 paper on the fitting of power series (polynomials) to band-spectroscopic data.1,2 John Tukey (1915–2000), an American statistician renowned for his work on exploratory data analysis, the box plot, and co-development of the fast Fourier transform, advocated resistant statistical techniques that remain stable even when data contain anomalous points.3 Classical least-squares regression squares every residual, so a single distant outlier can exert disproportionate leverage and pull the entire fitted model off course. The biweight counters this by applying a weight that falls smoothly to zero once a standardized residual exceeds the user-chosen tuning constant \(c\). Observations beyond this cutoff receive zero weight and therefore exert no further influence on the parameter estimates.

Real-life applications in engineering and the natural sciences center on robust regression and parameter estimation from experimental or process data that may contain measurement errors, sensor glitches, or occasional bad readings. The method was originally demonstrated on band-spectroscopic measurements in analytical chemistry and physics, where instrumental artifacts can produce outliers.1 In manufacturing quality control and process modeling, Tukey bisquare weighting yields stable relationships between input variables and output metrics even when some observations are contaminated.4 National statistical offices apply biweight-based procedures for editing and imputing survey, economic, and administrative data, reducing the impact of reporting errors without discarding entire records.5 The same resistance to heavy-tailed errors makes the technique useful across scientific disciplines whenever fitted models must be protected from the distorting effect of a few anomalous points.

AutoLISP Code

(defun C:TUKEY (/ basept xaxisangle cd sa sb sm om os divcount cosangle sinangle x0 y0 plineents segcount baseinfo paramstep
                           divpt xspan restartflag id iq dcl opt kw mode dpr dct dpt span eq_display xc yc a1 a2 x1 y1 x2 y2 fa)
  (setq id "Tukey's Biweight"
        iq "y=x(1-(x/c)²)²"
        dcl nil
        opt nil
        kw nil
        mode 'param
        dpr "# of divisions in half-span"
        dct 500
        dpt nil
        span nil
        kw (ca id iq dcl opt kw mode dpr dct dpt span)
        fa (get_real 2 "c (half-span)" 1.0)
        eq_display (strcat "\nc=" (rtos fa 2 4))
        paramstep (/ fa divcount)
        xc '(eval a2)
        yc '(if (> (abs (eval a2)) fa) 0.0 (* (eval a2) (expt (- 1.0 (expt (/ (eval a2) fa) 2.0)) 2.0)))
        skm '(T T nil)
        a1 0
        a2 a1
        x1 (eval xc)
        y1 (eval yc)
        a2 '(+ a1 paramstep)
        x2 '(eval xc)
        y2 '(eval yc))
  (cc))
