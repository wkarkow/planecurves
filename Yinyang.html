Yin-Yang Curve

A bounded polar spiral resembling the traditional taijitu that is a slightly different figure built from circular arcs.

YinYang Curve polar equation:
\[ r^{2} = \dfrac{\theta}{a+\theta} \]

History and Applications

The familiar yin–yang symbol (more precisely the taijitu) originates in ancient Chinese philosophy and cosmology.1 Its conceptual roots appear in early Daoist thought and in the I Ching (Book of Changes)2, where yin and yang represent complementary dualities: dark/light, passive/active, earth/heaven, contraction/expansion. The now-standard circular diagram, with interlocking black and white “fish” shapes each containing a seed of the other, became visually standardized during the Song dynasty (10th–13th century)3, particularly in Neo-Confucian cosmological diagrams. Importantly, the traditional taijitu is not defined by a single canonical algebraic equation; it is a geometric emblem whose boundary can be constructed by combinations of circular arcs.1 The curve that has the polar equation \(r^{2}=\theta/(a+\theta)\) is a modern mathematical spiral that captures the visual logic of mutual interpenetration through its asymptotic behavior, in the spirit of decorative spirals catalogued by R. Ferréol.4 It uses a continuously increasing polar radius rather than stitched circular arcs. This equation defines a polar spiral with distinctive asymptotic behavior. The curve begins at the origin with square-root growth. For small \(\theta\) (near the center), \(r\approx\sqrt{\theta/a}\). As \(\theta\) becomes large, \(r\) approaches 1 (when \(a=1\)), so the spiral winds outward but never exceeds a fixed circular boundary. By comparison, the Fermat spiral \(r^{2}=k\theta\) grows without limit, though its growth is also square-root-like.5 This curve therefore resembles the Fermat spiral near the origin but differs in its global structure: it approaches a fixed radius asymptotically rather than expanding indefinitely. In practical terms for plotting, the radial growth slows steadily, causing the curve to pack more tightly against the limiting circle after many turns.

No specific applications of this exact curve in engineering or the natural sciences have been documented in available sources. It remains primarily of mathematical interest as an example of a bounded polar spiral, useful for CAD illustration of asymptotic behavior and decorative patterns.

AutoLISP Code

(defun C:YINYANG (/ basept xaxisangle cd sa sb sm om os divcount cosangle sinangle x0 y0 plineents segcount baseinfo paramstep
                     divpt xstep restartflag id iq dcl opt kw mode dpr dct dpt span eq_display rval xc yc skm a1 a2 x1 y1 x2 y2 fa)
  (setq id "Yin-Yang Curve"
        iq "r²=θ/(a+θ)"
        dcl nil
        opt nil
        kw nil
        mode 'polar
        dpr "# of divisions around circle"
        dct 360
        dpt nil
        span 360
        kw (ca id iq dcl opt kw mode dpr dct dpt span)
        fa (get_real 6 "a (radius)" 1.0)
        eq_display (strcat "\na=" (rtos fa 2 4))
        rval '(if (< (/ (eval a2) (+ fa (eval a2))) 0.0) 0.0 (sqrt (/ (eval a2) (+ fa (eval a2)))))
        xc '(* (eval rval) (cos (eval a2)))
        yc '(* (eval rval) (sin (eval a2)))
        skm '(T T nil)
        a1 0
        a2 a1
        x1 (eval xc)
        y1 (eval yc)
        a2 '(+ a1 paramstep)
        x2 '(eval xc)
        y2 '(eval yc))
  (cc))
(C:YINYANG)
