Planck Curve

The mean energy of a quantum oscillator, the spectral intensity distribution, and the fourth-power total radiated energy of the Stefan–Boltzmann law.

Planck Quantum Oscillator equation:
\[ y = a \cdot \coth(b \cdot x) \]
Planck Blackbody Spectrum equation:
\[ y = a \cdot \dfrac{x^{3}}{e^{b \cdot x}-1} \]
Planck Stefan-Boltzmann Energy equation:
\[ y = a \cdot x^{4} \]

History and Applications

The Planck Curve family1,2,3 arises from Max Planck’s (1858–1947) groundbreaking work in 1900 on blackbody radiation. Classical physics could not explain the observed spectrum of radiation emitted by a perfect absorber and emitter (a blackbody). The Rayleigh-Jeans law predicted infinite energy at short wavelengths, the ultraviolet catastrophe. Planck assumed that the energy of oscillators in the walls of a cavity could only change in discrete increments of \(h\nu\), where \(h\) is Planck’s constant and \(\nu\) is frequency. This quantum hypothesis produced a formula that matched experimental data perfectly across all wavelengths. The spectral distribution of radiation intensity follows a curve of the form proportional to \(\frac{x^{3}}{e^{bx}-1}\). When integrated over all frequencies, the total energy radiated per unit time per unit area is proportional to \(T^{4}\), giving the Stefan-Boltzmann law (the area under the spectrum curve, computed via basic integral calculus). The average energy of a single quantum harmonic oscillator in thermal equilibrium is proportional to \(\coth(b \cdot x)\), where \(x\) is related to frequency over temperature. These relations can be understood through basic calculus concepts: derivatives describe the slope (rate of change) of the spectrum with frequency or temperature, while the integral sums contributions across the entire spectrum to obtain total radiated power.

These mathematical forms have direct applications in engineering and the natural sciences. They are used to model stellar spectra and determine the temperatures of stars in astrophysics. In engineering, they enable accurate non-contact temperature measurement with optical pyrometers in metallurgy and manufacturing, design of thermal imaging cameras for medical diagnostics and building inspection, calculation of radiative heat transfer in industrial furnaces and spacecraft, and the development of efficient incandescent and infrared sources. The Stefan-Boltzmann law underpins satellite thermal control systems and global climate modeling of Earth’s energy budget.

AutoLISP Code

(defun C:PLANCK (/ basept xaxisangle cd sa sb sm om os divcount cosangle sinangle x0 y0 plineents segcount baseinfo divpt xspan
                   paramstep restartflag id iq dcl opt kw mode dpr dct dpt span eq_display xc yc skm a1 a2 x1 y1 x2 y2 fa fb)
  (setq id "Planck Curve"
        iq ""
        dcl "PLANCK"
        opt '("Quantum Oscillator" "Blackbody Spectrum" "Stefan-Boltzmann Energy")
        kw 0
        mode 'param
        dpr "# of divisions to x-span of division point"
        dct 100
        dpt '(10.0 0.0)
        span nil
        kw (ca id iq dcl opt kw mode dpr dct dpt span))
  (cond
   ((eq kw 0)
    (princ "\nQuantum Oscillator: y = a·coth(b·x)")
    (setq fa (get_real 6 "a (energy scale)" 1.0)
          fb (get_real 2 "b (quantum factor)" 1.0)
          eq_display (strcat "\na=" (rtos fa 2 4) ", b=" (rtos fb 2 4))
          yc '(* fa (/ (+ (exp (* fb (eval a2))) (exp (- (* fb (eval a2))))) (avoid_zero (- (exp (* fb (eval a2))) (exp (- (* fb (eval a2))))))))))
   ((eq kw 1)
    (princ "\nBlackbody Spectrum: y = a·x^3/(e^(b·x)-1)")
    (setq fa (get_real 6 "a (scale - power intensity)" 1.0)
          fb (get_real 2 "b (temperature-inverse factor)" 1.0)
          eq_display (strcat "\na=" (rtos fa 2 4) ", b=" (rtos fb 2 4))
          yc '(* fa (/ (* (eval a2) (eval a2) (eval a2)) (avoid_zero (- (exp (* fb (eval a2))) 1.0))))))
   ((eq kw 2)
    (princ "\nStefan-Boltzmann Energy: y = a·x^4")
    (setq fa (get_real 6 "a (scale)" 1.0)
          eq_display (strcat "\na=" (rtos fa 2 4))
          yc '(* fa (eval a2) (eval a2) (eval a2) (eval a2)))))
  (setq xc '(eval a2)
        paramstep (/ xspan divcount)
        skm '(T T T)
        a1 0
        a2 a1
        x1 (eval xc)
        y1 (eval yc)
        a2 '(+ a1 paramstep)
        x2 '(eval xc)
        y2 '(eval yc))
  (cc))
(C:PLANCK)
