Limaçon Evolute

The locus of centers of curvature of the limaçon of Pascal.

Classical limaçon polar equation:
\[ r = a + b\cos\theta \]
Generalized limaçon polar equation:
\[ r = a + b\cdot\cos^{c}(d\theta) \]
Generalized evolute parametric equation \(x(\theta)\):
\[ x=\dfrac{ \bigl(a+b\cos^{c}(d\theta)\bigr)\cos\theta \Bigl[ \bigl(a+b\cos^{c}(d\theta)\bigr)^{2} +2b^{2}c^{2}d^{2}\sin^{2}(d\theta)\cos^{2c-2}(d\theta) -bcd^{2}\bigl(a+b\cos^{c}(d\theta)\bigr)\bigl(c\sin^{2}(d\theta)-1\bigr)\cos^{c-2}(d\theta) \Bigr] - \Bigl[-bcd\sin(d\theta)\cos^{c-1}(d\theta)\sin\theta +\bigl(a+b\cos^{c}(d\theta)\bigr)\cos\theta\Bigr] \Bigl[ \bigl(a+b\cos^{c}(d\theta)\bigr)^{2} +b^{2}c^{2}d^{2}\sin^{2}(d\theta)\cos^{2c-2}(d\theta) \Bigr] }{ \bigl(a+b\cos^{c}(d\theta)\bigr)^{2} +2b^{2}c^{2}d^{2}\sin^{2}(d\theta)\cos^{2c-2}(d\theta) -bcd^{2}\bigl(a+b\cos^{c}(d\theta)\bigr)\bigl(c\sin^{2}(d\theta)-1\bigr)\cos^{c-2}(d\theta) } \]

\[ y=\dfrac{ \bigl(a+b\cos^{c}(d\theta)\bigr)\sin\theta \Bigl[ \bigl(a+b\cos^{c}(d\theta)\bigr)^{2} +2b^{2}c^{2}d^{2}\sin^{2}(d\theta)\cos^{2c-2}(d\theta) -bcd^{2}\bigl(a+b\cos^{c}(d\theta)\bigr)\bigl(c\sin^{2}(d\theta)-1\bigr)\cos^{c-2}(d\theta) \Bigr] + \Bigl[-bcd\sin(d\theta)\cos^{c-1}(d\theta)\cos\theta -\bigl(a+b\cos^{c}(d\theta)\bigr)\sin\theta\Bigr] \Bigl[ \bigl(a+b\cos^{c}(d\theta)\bigr)^{2} +b^{2}c^{2}d^{2}\sin^{2}(d\theta)\cos^{2c-2}(d\theta) \Bigr] }{ \bigl(a+b\cos^{c}(d\theta)\bigr)^{2} +2b^{2}c^{2}d^{2}\sin^{2}(d\theta)\cos^{2c-2}(d\theta) -bcd^{2}\bigl(a+b\cos^{c}(d\theta)\bigr)\bigl(c\sin^{2}(d\theta)-1\bigr)\cos^{c-2}(d\theta) } \]

History and Applications

The limaçon of Pascal is a polar curve first investigated geometrically by Albrecht Dürer in 1525 in his treatise Underweysung der Messung, where he described a construction method.1 Étienne Pascal (1588–1651), a French magistrate and father of Blaise Pascal, studied the curve around 1630. Gilles Personne de Roberval named it “limaçon” (from the Latin limax for snail) in 1650 while using it to illustrate methods for finding tangents.2 The evolute of any plane curve is the locus of its centers of curvature (the envelope of its normals). For the classical limaçon \(r=a+b\cdot\cos\theta\), the evolute coincides exactly with the catacaustic, the envelope of rays reflected from a circle when the light source (radiant point) lies at a finite distance from the circle.3 The code generalizes the limaçon to \(r=a+b\cdot\cos^{c}(d\theta)\) and computes the corresponding evolute parametrically via the standard polar-evolute formulas for radius of curvature and center-of-curvature coordinates. Once the limaçon is generalized, its evolute grows in algebraic difficulty far faster than the original curve.

Real-life applications of the limaçon evolute arise primarily through its identity with circle catacaustics in optics. Caustics describe the bright curves formed by concentrated reflected light rays and appear in the analysis of curved mirrors, cylindrical lenses, and everyday reflection phenomena.4 In engineering, limaçon-based rotary positive-displacement machines (compressors, expanders, and pumps) use limaçon profiles for variable-volume chambers in fluid processing and small-scale power generation, including renewable-energy systems; the evolute properties aid in understanding curvature-related stress and sealing behavior in such designs.5
