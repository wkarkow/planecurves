Butterfly Curve

Families of polar curves producing four-winged butterfly figures and related multi-lobed forms.

Fay Butterfly polar equation:
\[ r = e^{\sin(\theta)} - 2\cos(4\theta) + \sin^{5}\left( \frac{2\theta-\pi}{24} \right) \]
Generalized Fay polar equation:
\[ r = a\,e^{\cos(b\theta+c)} + d\,\cos(e\theta+f) + g\,\sin^{h}(i\theta+j) \]
Classic Butterfly implicit equation:
\[ y^{6} = x^{2} - x^{6} \]
Generalized classic polar equation:
\[ r = \left( \frac{\cos^{c}(a\theta+b)}{\sin^{f}(d\theta+e) + \cos^{i}(g\theta+h)} \right)^{j} \]
Generalized |cos| polar equation:
\[ r = \left( \frac{\cos^{c}(a\theta+b)}{\sin^{f}(d\theta+e) + |\cos|^{i}(g\theta+h)} \right)^{j} \]
Sautereau-1 polar equation:
\[ r = a\sin(b\theta) + c\cos(d\theta) \]
Sautereau-2 polar equation:
\[ r = -3\cos(2\theta) + \sin(7\theta)-1 \]
Generalized Sautereau polar equation:
\[ r = a\cos(b\theta) + c\sin(d\theta) + e \]
Beetle Butterfly polar equation:
\[ r = \sin(\theta + \pi/4) - \sin(2\theta) \]

History and Applications

The transcendental butterfly curve was introduced by Temple H. Fay1, a mathematician at the University of Southern Mississippi, in a short 1989 note published in the American Mathematical Monthly2. Fay was exploring families of petal-like curves suitable for classroom demonstrations of polar and parametric plotting. His original equation produces a four-winged figure whose full symmetry appears only after the parameter θ reaches 24π (or 4320°), a range far beyond the usual 2π used for most polar plots. The generalized exponential form in the code lets the draftsman adjust each coefficient independently while keeping the essential shape and the long closure interval.

A second, purely algebraic butterfly is the sextic curve given in Cartesian form by y⁶ = x² − x⁶.3 It appears in classic collections of plane algebraic curves such as Cundy and Rollett’s Mathematical Models (1989). When converted to the polar form used in the code, it draws without gaps and reveals the same bilateral symmetry, but with sharper cusps at the origin. The two Sautereau forms are simpler sums of sine and cosine terms in polar coordinates; they are listed in some French curve compendia but carry no detailed published history. The final variant, the Beetle Butterfly, is the user’s own creation. It is derived directly from the classical Beetle (scarabée) curve first appearing in 1857 in a French textbook prepared for applicants to the École Polytechnique by Catalan and later included in Laurent’s treatise on analysis.4 The Beetle is a rational sextic that is the pedal of an astroid; the user’s modification yields a compact, four-lobed figure that closes after a single 2π rotation and reflection.

Real-life applications in engineering and the natural sciences are limited for these exact curves. The Fay form and its generalizations appear in calculus textbooks and computer-algebra demonstrations as striking examples of how small changes in frequency or phase can alter global symmetry. The algebraic sextic has been used occasionally in computer-graphics algorithms that require compact, closed algebraic boundaries. No documented uses were found for the Sautereau or Beetle variants beyond mathematical recreation. In applied fields, the term “butterfly curve” sometimes refers instead to hysteresis loops in control systems or to diffraction patterns, but those are unrelated to the geometric shapes.
