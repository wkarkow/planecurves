Exponential Curve

Family of curves of the form y = a·e^{b·x^c} that unifies ordinary exponential growth and decay, the Gaussian bell, stretched exponentials, super-Gaussians, inverse-square and reciprocal forms used in population growth, radioactive decay, laser beams, and material relaxation.

Generalized Exponential equation:
\[ y = a \cdot e^{b x^{c}} \]

History and Applications

Before formal mathematics, exponential-like growth was observed in practical contexts: unchecked population increase, compound interest in commerce, and bacterial or mold growth in food. Ancient Greek mathematicians (e.g., Euclid, Archimedes) knew of geometric progressions (ratios repeatedly multiplied), but they did not connect them to a continuous curve. Islamic mathematics (9th–11th c.): Al-Khwarizmi and others worked extensively with powers and roots in algebra. They developed rules equivalent to manipulating exponentials, but again without explicit function-curve language. In 1614, John Napier published Mirifici Logarithmorum Canonis Descriptio, introducing logarithms.1 His central motivation was to simplify astronomical and navigational calculations. Napier’s work implicitly described exponential relations: logarithms are inverses of exponential growth. Ten years later, Henry Briggs popularized the use of base-10 logarithms. Briggs collaborated with Napier and published extensive logarithmic tables. At this stage, “exponential” was not yet a named function, but the groundwork was laid: a continuous function whose rate of increase is proportional to its current value. In 1637, René Descartes in La Géométrie explored curves generated by algebraic and transcendental functions. Although he did not write the exponential function explicitly, he admitted non-algebraic functions into analytic geometry. Isaac Newton in the 1660s–1670s worked extensively with series expansions. Newton derived power series for \(e^x\), sine, cosine, and logarithm, showing the exponential could be represented analytically. Jacob Bernoulli subsequently studied compound interest and discovered the constant \(e\). In analyzing growth of \((1 + 1/n)^n\) as \(n→∞\), he essentially uncovered the natural exponential base. In the early 1700s, Leonhard Euler put the theory on a rigorous footing, defining \(e^x\) as the unique function equal to its own derivative,1 unifying exponential growth/decay with logarithms as its inverse, and introducing \(e^{iπ}+1=0\) in 1748, revealing the deep connection between exponentials and trigonometric functions. In the 19th century, Cauchy, Fourier, and Laplace used exponential functions extensively in analysis, particularly in solutions of differential equations, heat flow, and probability. By the late 19th century, radioactive decay was found to follow an exponential law,1 cementing the role of exponentials in modeling natural processes.

The general form \(y=a\cdot e^{b x^{c}}\) unifies these models. For \(c=1\) the curve is ordinary exponential growth or decay. For \(c=2\) it becomes the Gaussian bell curve.2,3 Fractional or non-integer c produces the stretched exponential (Kohlrausch function).4 Higher-order \(c\) yields super-Gaussian profiles.5

Applications span engineering and the natural sciences. The code options cover population growth and compound interest (kw 2), radioactive decay and capacitor discharge (kw 0), statistical distributions and heat diffusion (kw 1), laser beam shaping and particle-beam focusing (kw 1 and kw 6), signal apodization in optics and electronics, relaxation in polymers and glassy systems (kw 5), thermal activation and loss, chemical reaction rates, and specialized decay models such as inverse-square (kw 3) and reciprocal (kw 4) forms used in certain diffusion and attenuation processes.
