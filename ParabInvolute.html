Parabola Involute

The path of the free end of a taut string as it unwinds from the parabola \(y=ax^{2}\) while remaining tangent to it.

Parabola Involute parametric equations:
\[ x = -\dfrac{1}{a}\left(\dfrac{t}{2}-\dfrac{\ln\bigl(2t+\sqrt{1+4t^{2}}\bigr)}{4\sqrt{1+4t^{2}}}\right),\quad y = -\dfrac{1}{a}\dfrac{t\,\ln\bigl(2t+\sqrt{1+4t^{2}}\bigr)}{2\sqrt{1+4t^{2}}} \]

History and Applications

The involute of a curve1 is the path traced by the free end of a taut string as it unwinds from the curve while remaining tangent to it. The general theory of involutes and evolutes was developed by Christiaan Huygens2 (1629–1695) in Horologium Oscillatorium (1673) to design an isochronous pendulum using the cycloid. The parabola itself was studied by ancient Greek mathematicians including Menaechmus (c. 380–320 BCE), who used it to solve the duplication of the cube, and Apollonius of Perga (c. 240–190 BCE), who named it and defined its focus-directrix property. The specific involute of the parabola \(y=ax^{2}\) is a standard derived curve in differential geometry;3 its parametric equations arise directly from the arc-length integral along the generating parabola and require no special discovery beyond the general involute construction. No single mathematician is credited with isolating this particular case.

No real-life applications in engineering or the natural sciences were identified for the involute of a parabola specifically. It remains a theoretical example in mathematical handbooks.
