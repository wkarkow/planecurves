Ferréol Tetracuspid

Four-lobed tetracuspid family with inward cusps, distinct from an epicycloid.

Ferréol Tetracuspid Cartesian equation:
\[ a^\alpha \bigl(|x|^\alpha + |y|^\alpha\bigr) = (x^2 + y^2)^\alpha, \quad \alpha = 1/n \]
Ferréol Tetracuspid parametric equations:
\[ x = a \dfrac{\cos^n(t)}{\cos^{2n}(t) + \sin^{2n}(t)}, \quad y = a \dfrac{\sin^n(t)}{\cos^{2n}(t) + \sin^{2n}(t)} \]

History and Applications

The Ferréol tetracuspid (also four-lobed tetracuspid) is the one-parameter family of closed curves isolated on the MathCurve tetracuspide page under the heading of huddled (inward-cusped) tetracuspids.1 MathCurve presents the parametric equations for odd positive integers \(n\) and notes that the absolute-value Cartesian form extends the family to any real exponent \(\alpha=1/n\in(0,1]\). The case \(n=3\) is identified there as the orthoptic of the cross curve.1,2 No earlier source assigns a proper name to the general family; the designation Ferréol tetracuspid records the sole published isolation of the parametric form.

Robert Ferréol, a French mathematician and professeur agrégé, taught in the classes préparatoires (mathématiques supérieures) at the Lycée Fénelon in Paris. In 1993 he began MathCurve (the Encyclopédie des formes mathématiques remarquables) as a modest summary of classical curves intended for his students; the site has since grown into a comprehensive online reference containing roughly 550 plane curves, 150 space curves, 300 surfaces, 90 fractals and more than 250 polyhedra, with an English translation by David Michel. His work was recognized in 2008 by the Anatole-Decerf Prize of the Société mathématique de France for contributions to the teaching and popularization of mathematics. Ferréol continues to publish on curves, surfaces and related topics in Tangente and other outlets,and his ongoing correspondence has encouraged the systematic expansion of the present collection.

A tetracuspid is any simple closed curve possessing exactly four cusps. The classical (protruding) specimen is the astroid; the present family is the huddled counterpart in which the cusps, when they exist, point inward. True cusps appear only for odd positive integers \(n\); for other real values the four lobes remain but the corners are rounded. The four-cusped epicycloid is a distinct curve.

No engineering or natural-science applications peculiar to the general family have been located. The special case \(n=3\) inherits whatever uses may attach to the orthoptic of the cross curve, but none are documented for that orthoptic beyond its geometric definition.2,3
