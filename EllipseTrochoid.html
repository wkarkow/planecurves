Ellipse Trochoid

No-slip roulette of a circle on an elliptical base, and free-ratio central-curve variant with independent spin.

No-slip (ellipse-base) equations:
\[ \mathbf{M}=M_0+a\mathbf{N}+d\mathbf{U},\quad\phi=s/a,\quad\mathbf{U}=\mathrm{rot}(\phi)\mathbf{N} \]
Ellipse and arc length:
\[ M_0=(A\cos t,\,B\sin t),\quad s=\int\sqrt{A^2\sin^2 t+B^2\cos^2 t}\,dt \]
Free-ratio (central) equations:
\[ \mathbf{M}=M_0+d\,(\cos(ks),\sin(ks)),\quad k=(p/q)\cdot(2\pi/P) \]

History and Applications

The ellipse-base trochoid (no-slip) and the ellipse-central trochoid (free-ratio) are modern generalizations of the classical trochoid.1 The classical trochoid itself—the path of a point fixed to a circle that rolls without slipping on a straight line—was studied by Albrecht Dürer (1525) and Ole Rømer (1674); Gilles de Roberval coined the name from the Greek trochos (wheel) in connection with the cycloid.2 The broader theory of roulettes (curves traced by a point attached to one curve as it rolls on another) developed in the seventeenth century around the cycloid and its relatives, with contributions from Roberval, Pascal, Huygens, Leibniz, Newton, and the Bernoullis.3

When the fixed base is a circle one obtains the centered trochoids (epitrochoids and hypotrochoids). When the fixed base is an arbitrary curve and the rolling curve is a circle, the result is the any-based trochoid presented by Robert Ferréol on MathCurve.1 The elliptical-base specimen is the special case in which the fixed curve is an ellipse; the free-ratio (central-curve) variant on the same page is the related construction in which the center of the disk follows a prescribed path while the disk spins at an independent constant ratio.1

No classical attribution attaches specifically to the elliptical-base case. The construction appears in modern computational and educational contexts (Wolfram Demonstrations, MathStackExchange discussions of the elliptic integral that arises for the arc length) rather than in the historical literature of named curves.4 Because the exact perimeter of an ellipse is an elliptic integral with no elementary closed form, the code uses Ramanujan’s second approximation5 \[ P\approx\pi(A+B)\Bigl(1+\frac{3h}{10+\sqrt{4-3h}}\Bigr),\qquad h=\Bigl(\frac{A-B}{A+B}\Bigr)^2 \] to obtain a rapidly computable estimate of \(P\). This permits the rolling radius (or the free-ratio speed \(k\)) to be set so that the circumference ratio is exactly a chosen rational \(p/q\), guaranteeing that the generated curve closes after a known number of circuits.

Real-life applications of the exact ellipse-base roulette are not documented. Broader trochoidal and epitrochoidal paths appear in the design of rotary pumps, Wankel-type engines, and certain gear-generation processes, and elliptical tool-paths have been investigated in contemporary CNC trochoidal milling to raise material-removal rates.5 These uses, however, employ circular-base or free-form trochoids, not the pure no-slip roulette of a circle on an elliptical base. The free-ratio central-curve version likewise lacks specific engineering applications beyond its role as a kinematic illustration.
