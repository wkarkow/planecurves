Chrysanthemum Curve

A dense, radially symmetric polar curve resembling a chrysanthemum.

Chrysanthemum Curve polar equation:
\[ r = 5\left[1 + \sin\left(\frac{11\theta}{5}\right)\right] - 4\sin^{4}\left(\frac{17\theta}{3}\right) \cdot \sin^{8}\left[2\cos(3\theta) - 28\theta\right], \quad 0 \leq \theta \leq 21\pi \]

History and Applications

The Chrysanthemum Curve is attributed to Temple H. Fay1,2, a late-20th-century American mathematician whose work focused on mathematical modeling, numerical methods, and the visualization of complex parametric and polar curves. Fay is best known for introducing the Butterfly Curve in his 1989 paper3 "The Butterfly Curve" published in The American Mathematical Monthly, a piece that helped spark broader interest in visually rich, non-classical curves defined through trigonometric combinations. His work reflects a shift from classical analytic curves such as conics and roulettes toward curves constructed for their structural and visual complexity using modern computational tools. Fay's contributions sit at the intersection of recreational mathematics and applied numerical analysis. In addition to his curve work, he explored computational sensitivity in "A Study in Step Size" (1997)4, emphasizing how numerical step selection affects accuracy and stability in iterative calculations. This perspective is directly relevant to curve plotting: many of Fay's curves, including the Chrysanthemum, depend on dense sampling of oscillatory trigonometric expressions, where insufficient resolution can distort the geometry. His work therefore bridges theory and computation, highlighting how modern visualization relies not just on equations but on careful numerical implementation.

The Chrysanthemum Curve itself is not derived from a classical geometric construction but from layered trigonometric modulation, where terms such as sin(kθ) and powers like sin^n(·) act as frequency and amplitude shapers. This produces petal-like structures through interference of periodic components, analogous in principle to Fourier synthesis, where complex shapes arise from combinations of simpler oscillations. The result is a dense, radially symmetric structure resembling a natural flower, demonstrating how relatively simple trigonometric building blocks can generate highly intricate forms.

Although Fay originated the curve, its widespread recognition is due largely to Paul Bourke, who published detailed renderings and parameterizations on his visualization site. Bourke's work did not introduce new mathematics but played a crucial role in dissemination, making the curve accessible to engineers, programmers, and educators.

In terms of applications, the Chrysanthemum Curve has no known direct engineering or physical use. Unlike curves such as the catenary or cycloid, it does not arise from an optimization principle or physical constraint. Its importance lies instead in computational mathematics and visualization: it serves as a benchmark example for testing plotting algorithms, parametric resolution strategies, and numerical stability in highly oscillatory systems.         yc '(* (sin (eval a2)) (eval rval))

        a2 a1
