Sinusoidal Radius Curve

A family of transcendental plane curves whose radius of curvature is a sinusoidal function of arc length.

Intrinsic equation (radius of curvature):
\[ r = a\bigl(\lambda + \cos(s/a)\bigr) \]
Variation 1, \(0\le\lambda<1\):
\[ x = a(1-\lambda^2)\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\cos u}{\cosh(\sqrt{1-\lambda^2}\,u)-\lambda}\,du,\quad y = a(1-\lambda^2)\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\sin u}{\cosh(\sqrt{1-\lambda^2}\,u)-\lambda}\,du \]
Variation 2, \(\lambda=1\):
\[ x = 2a\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\cos u}{1+u^2}\,du,\quad y = 2a\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\sin u}{1+u^2}\,du \]
Variation 3, \(\lambda>1\), \(n=\sqrt{\lambda^2-1}\):
\[ x = an^2\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\cos u}{\lambda-\cos(nu)}\,du,\quad y = an^2\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\sin u}{\lambda-\cos(nu)}\,du \]
Variation 4, free \(n\) and \(\lambda\):
\[ x = an^2\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\cos u}{\lambda-\cos(nu)}\,du,\quad y = an^2\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\sin u}{\lambda-\cos(nu)}\,du \]
Variation 5, log-phase spiral:
\[ x = a\int\cos\bigl(k\ln\lvert\tan(u/2)\rvert\bigr)\,du,\quad y = a\int\sin\bigl(k\ln\lvert\tan(u/2)\rvert\bigr)\,du \]
Variation 5 radius of curvature:
\[ R_{c}(s) = \frac{a}{k}\sin(s/a) \]

History and Applications

The Sinusoidal Radius Curve (rayon sinusoidal) is a family of transcendental plane curves studied by the German mathematician Ludwig Bieberbach in 1932.1 Bieberbach analyzed curves defined intrinsically such that the radius of curvature \(\rho\) is a sinusoidal function, \(\rho = a(\lambda + \cos(s/a))\), of the curvilinear abscissa \(s\) (arc length measured along the curve). This intrinsic property leads to the parametric forms implemented in the AutoLISP code, obtained by numerical integration of the resulting differential relations \(dx/ds = \cos(\theta)\) and \(dy/ds = \sin(\theta)\), where the turning rate \(d\theta/ds = 1/\rho(s)\) incorporates the sinusoidal modulation. The family includes the four principal cases (\(\lambda < 1\) with hyperbolic denominator, \(\lambda = 1\) with polynomial denominator, \(\lambda > 1\) or free \(n\) with trigonometric denominator, and the log-phase spiral variant) that arise from different choices of the sinusoidal argument and amplitude parameters. The curve fits within the classical differential–geometric tradition of defining curves through curvature rather than by a mechanical construction or algebraic locus. The idea of determining a curve from its curvature function dates back to the eighteenth century, when Euler and others established that curvature as a function of arc length uniquely determines a plane curve up to rigid motion. Unlike cycloids, conics, or logarithmic spirals, the sinusoidal-radius family does not arise from a rolling mechanism, optical law, or variational principle.

No real-life applications in engineering or the natural sciences were found.
