Hyperbola

Classical conic section of two open branches whose asymptotes meet at the center; defined by the constant difference of distances to two fixed foci.

Hyperbola Cartesian equation:
\[ \dfrac{y^{2}}{a^{2}} - \dfrac{x^{2}}{b^{2}} = 1 \]
Hyperbola Parametric equations:
\[ x = t,\quad y = ±a\sqrt{1 + \left(\dfrac{t}{b}\right)^{2}} \]

History and Applications

The hyperbola, one of the three classical conic sections, was first studied in special rectangular form by Menaechmus around 350 BC in connection with the problem of duplicating the cube. Euclid and Aristaeus later wrote on the general hyperbola but examined only one branch. Apollonius of Perga (c. 262–190 BC), known as “the Great Geometer,” gave the curve its name (from the Greek ὑπερβολή, hyperbolē, “excess”) and provided the first comprehensive treatment in his eight-book treatise Conics. Apollonius showed that the hyperbola, ellipse, and parabola are all obtainable as plane sections of the same right circular cone and was the first to study both branches systematically. Pappus later added the focus-directrix property. In its canonical form, the curve consists of two symmetric, open branches whose asymptotes meet at the center. The constant-difference property—that the difference of distances from any point on the curve to its two foci is constant—defines it geometrically, while its reflection property makes it the counterpart of the ellipse in optics: rays directed toward one focus are reflected as if they came from the other. Historically, the hyperbola gained importance in the 17th century when Kepler showed that bodies under inverse-square forces could follow hyperbolic orbits, and when Descartes and Newton used it in analytic geometry and celestial mechanics. Later, its rectangular form xy = c appeared in early studies of inverse proportionality, logarithmic scales, and asymptotic behavior.

In engineering and the natural sciences the hyperbola has several established applications. Hyperboloid cooling towers, generated by rotating a hyperbola about its conjugate axis, are standard in nuclear and coal-fired power plants; their double-curved form provides exceptional structural rigidity and wind resistance while minimizing material use. Navigation systems such as LORAN exploit the curve’s bifocal definition—the set of points for which the absolute difference of distances to two fixed foci is constant—to locate position from measured time differences of radio signals. In optics the reflection property of the hyperbola (a ray directed toward one focus reflects as if coming from the other) is employed in Cassegrain telescopes, where a hyperbolic secondary mirror directs light precisely to the focal point. Hyperbolic orbits also describe the escape trajectories of comets and other bodies with positive total energy in gravitational fields. Additionally, in analytic modeling the curve underlies the hyperbolic functions sinh and cosh, used to describe catenaries, suspension-bridge cables, and many nonlinear growth and stress relationships.
