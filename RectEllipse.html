Rectellipse

A quartic special case of the Lamé superellipse with the exponent fixed at 4, together with a second quartic that interpolates between an ellipse and a rectangle through a squareness parameter s.

Rectellipse Curve implicit equation 1:
\[ \left(\dfrac{x}{a}\right)^{4} + \left(\dfrac{y}{b}\right)^{4} = 1 \]
Rectellipse Curve implicit equation 2:
\[ s^{2}\cdot\dfrac{x^{2}}{a^{2}}\cdot\dfrac{y^{2}}{b^{2}} - \left(\dfrac{x^{2}}{a^{2}} + \dfrac{y^{2}}{b^{2}}\right) + 1 = 0 \]
Rectellipse parametric equations (equation 1):
\[ x = a\,\mathrm{sgn}(\cos t)\,|\cos t|^{1/2},\quad y = b\,\mathrm{sgn}(\sin t)\,|\sin t|^{1/2} \]
Rectellipse parametric equations (S variant):
\[ x = a\cos t,\quad y = b\,\mathrm{sgn}(\sin t)\sqrt{\dfrac{\sin^{2}t}{1 - s^{2}\cos^{2}t}} \]

History and Applications

The Rectellipse1 is a quartic plane curve and the special case of the superellipse (also called a Lamé curve) obtained when the exponent is fixed at exactly 4. The general superellipse family was introduced by the French mathematician and engineer Gabriel Lamé (1795–1870) in 1818.2 The specific name “rectellipse” was coined on MathWorld as the natural generalization of the squircle (the equal-axes case) to unequal semi-axes a and b.1 A second Cartesian equation that employs s as a squareness parameter, creating a similar rectellipse was also given (though not named) by Fernandez Guasti in 1992.3

In engineering and the natural sciences the rectellipse supplies closed-form expressions for enclosed area and second moments of area, quantities that appear directly in structural-mechanics calculations and in the design of beams, pressure vessels, and other components whose cross-sections must balance strength and material use. In optics the closely related squircle form has been applied to model LCD pixel shapes and the resulting far-field diffraction patterns that govern display resolution and light-scattering behavior.4
