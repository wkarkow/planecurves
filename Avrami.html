Avrami Curve

Sigmoidal curve modeling the transformed fraction y(t) during isothermal nucleation-and-growth phase transformations.

Avrami Curve Cartesian equation:
\[ y = 1 - e^{-k t^n} \]

History and Applications

The Johnson–Mehl–Avrami–Kolmogorov (JMAK) model, commonly called the Avrami equation, describes the kinetics of isothermal phase transformations in solids, particularly those involving nucleation and growth processes.1,2 The model emerged from independent but converging work in the late 1930s and early 1940s. Andrey N. Kolmogorov published the foundational statistical theory in 1937, treating random nucleation in space and time with constant growth rates.3 Melvin Avrami extended this in a series of papers from 1939 to 1941, incorporating probabilistic considerations for the transformed fraction.4 Concurrently, William A. Johnson and Robert F. Mehl applied similar ideas in 1939 to describe pearlite formation from austenite in steels, emphasizing practical metallurgical kinetics.5 The combined framework is now known as the JMAK (or sometimes KJMA) model.

The equation arises from assuming spatially random nucleation, constant (or time-dependent) isotropic growth, and impingement of growing domains that prevents overlap in the extended volume calculation. The transformed fraction y(t) follows an exponential form that emerges naturally from these assumptions rather than being an empirical fit. The Avrami exponent n reflects the underlying mechanism: it combines dimensionality of growth and nucleation behavior. For three-dimensional growth with continuous (sporadic) nucleation, n = 4; for three-dimensional with site-saturated (instantaneous) nucleation, n = 3; for two-dimensional growth, n = 2 or 3; for one-dimensional, n = 1 or 2.6 Non-integer values often appear in real systems due to mixed mechanisms, surface effects, or deviations from ideal assumptions.

In materials engineering and the natural sciences, the Avrami equation is foundational for modeling time-dependent phase changes where the fraction transformed is more critical than detailed microstructure. In metallurgy, it describes austenite decomposition to pearlite, bainite, or other products during heat treatment, allowing prediction of transformation progress at constant temperature. In polymer science, it models isothermal crystallization kinetics, with parameters extracted from differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) data via Avrami plots.7 It applies to devitrification of metallic glasses, crystallization in thin films during deposition (e.g., island nucleation and coalescence), oxidation layer growth, and surface reactions. In battery electrode materials, it describes phase changes during intercalation or conversion reactions driven by nucleation fronts. The sigmoidal shape—slow initial rise due to nucleation, acceleration as interface grows, and saturation as reactants deplete—makes it a preferred mechanistic model over purely empirical sigmoids in engineering contexts.

Compared to the logistic or Gompertz curves, the Avrami form rises more gradually near t = 0 when n > 1, lacks a fixed inflection constraint, and ties curvature directly to physical dimensionality and nucleation rate. This physical interpretability favors its use where parameters carry mechanistic meaning rather than mere curve-fitting.
