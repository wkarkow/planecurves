Superhyperbola

The Lamé difference family with unbounded branches.

Superhyperbola Horizontal implicit equation:
\[ \left\lvert\dfrac{x}{a}\right\rvert^{n} - \left\lvert\dfrac{y}{b}\right\rvert^{n} = 1 \]
Superhyperbola Vertical implicit equation:
\[ \left\lvert\dfrac{y}{b}\right\rvert^{n} - \left\lvert\dfrac{x}{a}\right\rvert^{n} = 1 \]
Superhyperbola Horizontal parametric equations:
\[ x = a\cdot\operatorname{sgn}(\sec t)\cdot\lvert\sec t\rvert^{2/n},\quad y = b\cdot\operatorname{sgn}(\tan t)\cdot\lvert\tan t\rvert^{2/n} \]

History and Applications

The superhyperbola (Lamé \(\overline{L}_\alpha^-\))1,2,3 is the difference counterpart of the superellipse. Gabriel Lamé (1795–1870), a French mathematician and engineer, introduced the broader family of curves \(\lvert x/a\rvert^\alpha \pm \lvert y/b\rvert^\alpha = 1\) in his 1818 memoir on geometric methods. He examined both the sum and difference cases for rational and irrational exponents, noting that rational exponents yield algebraic curves while irrational exponents yield transcendental ones.1 The sum form later acquired the name superellipse through the work of the Danish designer Piet Hein in the mid-twentieth century; the difference form received less attention and has no comparably widespread popular name. Special cases already familiar include the ordinary rectangular hyperbola (\(\alpha = 2\)) and the bullet-nose curve (\(\alpha = -2\)).

No independent engineering or natural-science applications of the general difference equation have been located beyond those already associated with its named special cases. The rectangular hyperbola appears in optics (reflecting telescopes, grazing-incidence mirrors) and in certain kinematic linkages; the bullet-nose curve has been used as a simple model for certain flow or potential contours. The general superhyperbola itself remains primarily a mathematical generalization. When the exponent \(n < 0\), the visual opening direction of the branches is opposite the keyword label (Horizontal draws the up–down family and Vertical the left–right family); the equations themselves remain correct.
