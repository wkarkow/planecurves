Base of the Slider-Crank Mechanism

Kinematic polar locus of the base point in a slider-crank linkage, derived from crank length a and connecting-rod length b.

Base of the Slider-Crank Mechanism polar equation:
\[ r = a \pm \frac{\sqrt{b^{2} - a^{2} \sin^{2} \theta }}{\cos \theta} \]

History and Applications

The Base of the Slider-Crank Mechanism1 is a kinematic locus rather than a classical named curve from 18th or 19th century pure geometry. It arises directly from the geometry of a slider-crank linkage, tracing points along the extended crank line projected perpendicularly to the slider path. With crank length a and connecting-rod length b, its defining polar equation follows from elementary trigonometry applied to the positions of the crank and rod.

The underlying slider-crank mechanism has ancient roots, with early variants appearing in Hellenistic water-lifting devices and the Hierapolis sawmill of the 3rd century CE.2 It was fully formalized and widely applied during the Industrial Revolution; engineers such as James Watt refined crank-and-linkage systems for steam engines. By the 19th century, slider-crank kinematics formed a standard topic in machine design and analytical mechanics.

This mechanism ranks among the most common in engineering. Real applications include internal combustion engines, where the piston converts rotary crank motion into linear motion (and vice versa), steam engines, reciprocating compressors and pumps, mechanical presses and shapers, and piston-driven actuators in industrial machinery. Although most engineering work focuses on displacement, velocity, and acceleration of the slider rather than the specific polar locus, plotting the base curve in CAD provides valuable geometric insight. It clearly shows limiting positions, symmetry, and the transitions that occur when b approaches or drops below a.
