Links Curve

A quartic algebraic plane curve with a tacnode at the origin, where two branches meet with the same tangent and higher-order contact.

Links Curve implicit equation:
\[ (x^{2} + y^{2} - 3x)^{2} = 4x^{2}(2 - x) \]
Links Curve polar equation:
\[ r = a \cdot \cos(\theta)\left(3 - 2\cos^{2}(\theta) \pm 2\sqrt{(1 - \cos^{2}(\theta))(2 - \cos^{2}(\theta))}\right) \]

History and Applications

The Links curve1 is a quartic algebraic plane curve, placing it in the same family as other degree-4 curves such as the bifolium or piriform, where the equation can be plotted directly or converted to polar form for rotational symmetry studies. The curve features a tacnode at the origin, a singularity where two branches meet with identical tangent direction and higher-order contact, producing a point of smooth self-touching rather than a cusp or crossing. In practical terms, the tacnode behaves like a point where the curve “kisses” itself without penetrating. The curve encloses an outer envelope that contains an inner loop, both symmetric about the x-axis in the standard positioning.

The curve appears without earlier named discoverer or specific mathematician attribution in the literature. It is catalogued as one of many illustrative plane curves in the third edition of Mathematical Models (1989) by H. Martyn Cundy and A. P. Rollett.2 Cundy, a mathematics professor who taught at the University of Malawi, and Rollett, an educator, compiled the volume as a practical handbook for constructing physical wire or string models of curves to demonstrate geometric properties in classrooms. On page 72 the Links curve is presented alongside other quartics suitable for such tactile teaching aids, emphasizing visual inspection of singularities like the tacnode and the enclosed regions.

No real-life applications in engineering or the natural sciences were identified in available sources.
