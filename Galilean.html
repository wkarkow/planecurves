Galilean Spiral

A plane curve modelling the approximate path of a falling body in the equatorial plane of a rotating Earth.

Galilean Spiral polar equation:
\[ r = b \theta^{2} - a \]

History and Applications

The Galilean spiral1,2, also known as the parabolic spiral, is defined by the relation r = bθ², sometimes with a constant offset written r = bθ² − a. Its origin is attributed to Galileo Galilei in his Discorsi e dimostrazioni matematiche intorno a due nuove scienze (1638), in which the motion of a point receding from the center in proportion to the square of the angle traversed was discussed. This was an outgrowth of Galileo’s investigations into uniformly accelerated motion, where distance varies as the square of time. By analogy, if angular displacement were considered a “time-like” variable, then the radius traced under quadratic growth produced a spiral whose arms open much more rapidly than those of the Archimedean spiral. Polar coordinates were not yet formalized in Galileo’s era, so the spiral was not originally given in the modern form. Instead, the curve was described geometrically, with proportionality rules rather than explicit equations. The symbolic form was adopted later in the seventeenth century, after the widespread adoption of analytic geometry. Jacob Bernoulli and other curve compilers classified it as a member of the “power spirals,” a family encompassing the Archimedean spiral, Fermat’s spiral, the lituus, and the hyperbolic spiral. In this scheme, the Galilean spiral is the case where the exponent = 2. One historical anecdote frequently connected with this spiral is Galileo’s “tower problem.” A rock dropped from the top of a tower retains the eastward velocity of the tower’s top due to Earth’s rotation. Expressed in Cartesian coordinates, the path is a parabola. Expressed instead in polar coordinates centered on Earth’s axis, the same motion takes the form of a quadratic spiral. Over the small angular sector corresponding to the actual fall, this spiral segment is nearly indistinguishable from a circular arc. Galileo conjectured that the trajectory might indeed be circular, and later commentators observed that this was “not far from the truth,” since the spiral locally mimics an arc of a circle.

Real-life applications in engineering and the natural sciences center on this historical context in classical mechanics. The spiral models the approximate path of a falling body in the equatorial plane of a rotating Earth, neglecting friction and over distances small compared with the Earth’s radius. It remains a standard educational example for compound motion in rotating reference frames.

AutoLISP Code

(defun C:GALILEAN (/ basept xaxisangle cd sa sb sm om os divcount cosangle sinangle x0 y0 plineents segcount baseinfo divpt xstep paramstep
                     restartflag id iq dcl opt kw mode dpr dct dpt span eq_display rval xc yc skm a1 a2 x1 y1 x2 y2 fa fb)
  (setq id "Galilean Spiral"
        iq "r=b·θ²-a"
        dcl nil
        opt nil
        kw nil
        mode 'polar
        dpr "# of divisions around circle"
        dct 360
        dpt nil
        span 360
        kw (ca id iq dcl opt kw mode dpr dct dpt span)
        fa (get_real 2 "a (radial offset)" 1.0)
        fb (get_real 2 "b (quadratic growth)" 1.0)
        eq_display (strcat "\na=" (rtos fa 2 4) " b=" (rtos fb 2 4))
        rval '(- (* fb (alt_expt (eval a2) 2.0 nil)) fa)
        xc '(* (eval rval) (cos (eval a2)))
        yc '(* (eval rval) (sin (eval a2)))
        skm '(nil nil T)
        a1 0
        a2 a1
        x1 (eval xc)
        y1 (eval yc)
        a2 '(+ a1 paramstep)
        x2 '(eval xc)
        y2 '(eval yc))
  (cc))
(C:GALILEAN)
