Mie Curve

Pair-potential and force curves for neutral atoms or molecules.

Mie potential equation:
\[ V=C\varepsilon\left[\left(\dfrac{\sigma}{r}\right)^{n}-\left(\dfrac{\sigma}{r}\right)^{m}\right],\quad C=\dfrac{n}{n-m}\left(\dfrac{n}{m}\right)^{\frac{m}{n-m}},\quad V_{\min}=-\varepsilon \]
Lennard-Jones equation:
\[ V=4\varepsilon\left[\left(\dfrac{\sigma}{r}\right)^{12}-\left(\dfrac{\sigma}{r}\right)^{6}\right] \]
Mie force equation:
\[ F=\dfrac{C\varepsilon}{r}\left[n\left(\dfrac{\sigma}{r}\right)^{n}-m\left(\dfrac{\sigma}{r}\right)^{m}\right] \]
Lennard-Jones force equation:
\[ F=\dfrac{24\varepsilon}{r}\left[2\left(\dfrac{\sigma}{r}\right)^{12}-\left(\dfrac{\sigma}{r}\right)^{6}\right] \]

History and Applications

The Mie potential1,2, introduced in 1903 by German physicist Gustav Mie (better known for his theory of Mie scattering in electromagnetism), is a generalized empirical model for the pairwise interaction energy between neutral atoms or molecules. It balances short-range repulsion, arising from electron cloud overlap and the Pauli exclusion principle, with long-range attraction due to induced dipole–dipole (van der Waals) forces. Predating quantum mechanics, Mie’s phenomenological formulation allows independent exponents n (repulsive) and m (attractive) to flexibly fit experimental data. In 1924, British physicist John Lennard-Jones proposed a specific simplified case using repulsive exponent 12 and attractive exponent 6, now known as the Lennard-Jones (12–6) potential. The choice of 12 was largely for computational convenience (as the square of 6), yet it closely matched measured noble-gas interaction energies and became a standard model. The Lennard-Jones potential is viewed as a canonical special case of the more general Mie family. The Mie potential models three physically distinct regions: a steep repulsive wall at short range; an equilibrium region around the potential minimum; and a weak, slowly decaying attractive tail at long range. The force curve, the negative derivative of the potential, is often more intuitive: positive (repulsive) at short distances, negative (attractive) at long distances, and zero at equilibrium separation.

These potentials and their force curves are foundational in engineering and the natural sciences. They serve as core components of molecular force fields in molecular dynamics and Monte Carlo simulations used to study gases, liquids, polymers, and simple solids. Applications include computing thermophysical properties, phase equilibria, virial coefficients, vapor-liquid interfaces, and transport behavior for substances such as noble gases, alkanes, and perfluoroalkanes.3

        iq ""
        dcl "MIE"
        opt '("Mie Potential" "Lennard-Jones Potential" "Mie Force" "Lennard-Jones Force")
        kw 0

        dpr "# of divisions to division point"

  (cond
   ((eq kw 0)
    (princ "\nMie Potential: V=C·ε[(σ/r)ⁿ-(σ/r)ᵐ],  C = n/(n-m)·(n/m)^{m/(n-m)}: ")

          fe (* fe (exp (* (/ fd (- fc fd)) (log (/ fc fd)))))
