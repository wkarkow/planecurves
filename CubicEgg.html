Cubic Egg Curve

Three asymmetric cubic egg-shaped curves; the Hügelschäffer egg, Newton's egg curve, and a rationalized elliptic approximation.

Hügelschäffer egg Cartesian equation:
\[ y = \frac{b^{2} (a^{2} - (x - d)^{2}) }{a^{2} - d^{2} + 2 d x} \]
Newton's Egg Cartesian equation:
\[ y^{2} = b (x^{2} - 1)(x - a) \]
Rationalized Elliptic Egg Cartesian equation:
\[ y^{2} = b \frac{(1 - x^{2}) }{1 + a x} \]

History and Applications

There are at least three distinct Cubic Egg Curves, also referred to informally as Egg-shaped Cubics or Asymmetric Polynomial Cubics, all of which are third-degree curves that resemble an asymmetric egg shape. The Hügelschäffer egg was studied by German engineer Fritz Hügelschäffer in 19481. Hügelschäffer constructed the curve as the Newton transform of two non-concentric circles, one centered at the origin. When the circles are concentric the result is an ellipse; shifting the second circle produces the characteristic egg asymmetry. The resulting algebraic curve is cubic. Newton’s egg curve2, another member of this family, arises from Newton’s 1710 classification of cubic curves as a diverging parabola with egg-like form when the cubic equation has three distinct real roots. The rationalized ellipse3 supplies a simple rational algebraic approximation for similar egg-shaped profiles. These curves furnish compact Cartesian equations that closely approximate the profile of many biological eggs.

Real-life applications exist in engineering and the natural sciences. In biology the equations model avian egg contours for quantitative studies in ornithology, evolution, and poultry science. A 2020 study by Narushin et al.4 used 2D digital imaging and image processing on hen eggs; the authors confirmed Hügelschäffer’s model describes actual egg contours with high precision and supplied refined formulas for calculating egg volume and surface area (R = 0.962). In civil and environmental engineering egg-shaped cross-sections improve hydraulic performance in drainage pipes and gutters (higher flow velocity at low water depths and better self-cleansing). Similar ovoid forms appear in anaerobic sludge digestion tanks because the shape enhances structural strength with minimal material and promotes uniform mixing. Aero-engineering employs the continuous-curvature property of the Hügelschäffer form for glider fuselage sections5 to ensure smooth pressure distribution and undisturbed boundary-layer flow. Chicken eggs (Gallus gallus domesticus) are reported to have elongation ratios between 1.4 to 1.5, or inversely, between 0.67 to 0.71, with the maximum bulge some 41% along the length4. The three egg curves here have been ‘tuned’ to approximate real chicken eggs by setting default values for the parameters, and in the latter 2 cases by adding a scaling parameter b for y to compensate for the bulge width. Other non-cubic egg curves such as the quartic ‘Wassenaar’ egg curve also exist and are treated separately in a general "Egg Curve" entry.

(defun C:CUBICEGG (/ basept xaxisangle cd sa sb sm om os divcount cosangle sinangle x0 y0 plineents segcount baseinfo paramstep
                     divpt restartflag id iq dcl opt kw mode dpr dct dpt span eq_display xc yc a1 a2 x1 y1 x2 y2 fa fb fc skm)
  (setq id "Cubic Egg Curve"
        iq ""
        dcl "CUBICEGG"
        opt '("Hügelschäffer" "Newton's" "Rationalized Ellipse")
        kw 0
        mode 'param
        dpr "# of divisions to division point"
        dct 100
        dpt '(1.0 0.0)
        span nil
        kw (ca id iq dcl opt kw mode dpr dct dpt span))
  (cond
   ((eq kw 0)
    (princ "\ny²=b²·(a²-(x-d)²)/(a²-d²+2dx)")
    (setq fa (get_real 2 "a (radius of the first circle)" 1.0)
          fb (get_real 2 "b (radius of the second circle)" 0.7)
          fc (get_real 2 "d (distance between the centres)" 0.18)
          eq_display (strcat "\na=" (rtos fa 2 4) ", b=" (rtos fb 2 4) ", d=" (rtos fc 2 4))
          a1 (- fc fa)
          xc '(eval a2)
          yc '(* fb (if (<= (/ (- (* fa fa) (expt (- (eval a2) fc) 2)) (avoid_zero (+ (* fa fa) (- (* fc fc)) (* 2 fc (eval a2))))) 1e-9) 0.0
              (alt_expt (/ (- (* fa fa) (expt (- (eval a2) fc) 2)) (avoid_zero (+ (* fa fa) (- (* fc fc)) (* 2 fc (eval a2))))) 0.5 nil)))))
   ((eq kw 1)
    (princ "\ny²=b²·(x²-1)·(x-a")
    (setq fa (get_real 2 "a (asymmetry parameter)" 5.5)
          fb (get_real 2 "b (y-scale-factor)" 0.297)
          eq_display (strcat "\na=" (rtos fa 2 4) ", b=" (rtos fb 2 4))
          a1 -1.0
          xc '(eval a2)
          yc '(* fb (if (<= (* (- (expt (eval a2) 2) 1.0) (- (eval a2) fa)) 1e-9) 0.0 (alt_expt (* (- (expt (eval a2) 2) 1.0) (- (eval a2) fa)) 0.5 nil)))))
   ((eq kw 2)
    (princ "\ny² = b²·(1-x²)/(1 + ax)")
    (setq fa (get_real 2 "a (asymmetry parameter)" 0.18)
          fb (get_real 2 "b (y-scale-factor)" 0.697)
          eq_display (strcat "\na=" (rtos fa 2 4) ", b=" (rtos fb 2 4))
          a1 -1.0
          xc '(eval a2)
          yc '(* fb (if (<= (/ (- 1.0 (expt (eval a2) 2)) (avoid_zero (+ (* fa (eval a2)) 1.0))) 1e-9) 0.0
              (alt_expt (/ (- 1.0 (expt (eval a2) 2)) (avoid_zero (+ (* fa (eval a2)) 1.0))) 0.5 nil))))))
  (setq paramstep (/ xspan divcount 0.5)
        skm '(T nil T)
        a2 a1
        x1 (eval xc)
        y1 (eval yc)
        a2 '(+ a1 paramstep)
        x2 '(eval xc)
        y2 '(eval yc))
  (cc))
