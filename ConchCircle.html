Conchoid of the Circle

The locus of points at fixed distance along a ray rotating about a pole placed a distance from the center of a base circle, with two branches.

Conchoid of the Circle polar equation:
\[ r = a \cdot \left( \cos(\theta) \pm \sqrt{k^{2} - \sin^{2}(\theta)} \right) \pm l \]
Generalized polar equation:
\[ r = a \cdot \left( \cos(b\theta) \pm \sqrt{k^{2} - \sin^{2}(b\theta)} \right) \pm l \]

History and Applications

The conchoid of a circle1,2 is a member of the general family of conchoids. The general construction was invented by the ancient Greek mathematician Nicomedes around 180 BC to address the classical problems of duplicating the cube and trisecting an angle. The specific case arises when the base curve is a circle with center O and radius b = k · a, the pole is placed at point A a distance a from O, and the constant modulus (fixed distance along each ray) is l · a.

The curve has the mechanical interpretation of the trajectory traced by a point fixed on a connecting rod that is forced to slide through the fixed pole A while another point on the rod moves along the circle.1 Each closed curve is formed by the ± branches meeting where the square-root term vanishes. A complete AutoCAD implementation requires four plot runs: select the right- or left-hand curve, then select the (+) or (−) square-root branch to generate each half.

Real-life applications are found in engineering and the natural sciences. In engineering the mechanical construction itself models constrained motion in linkages, providing a basis for designing mechanisms that convert circular input into specific output paths. In the natural sciences the curve appears in analysis: domains bounded by a conchoid of the circle have been used to test and prove properties in the Pompeiu problem (whether vanishing integrals over all rotations of a domain imply the function itself vanishes).3 No further documented applications in other branches of engineering or the natural sciences were located.
