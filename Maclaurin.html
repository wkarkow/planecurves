Trisectrix of Maclaurin

A cubic that is the intersection of two lines that rotate about fixed centers at an angular-speed ratio of 1:3; that ratio is what lets a straightedge read one-third of a given angle off the curve..

Trisectrix of Maclaurin Cartesian equation:
\[ 2x(x^{2}+y^{2}) = a(3x^{2}-y^{2}) \]
Trisectrix of Maclaurin polar equation:
\[ r = 2a\,\dfrac{\sin(3\theta)}{\sin(2\theta)} \]
Generalized polar equation:
\[ r = 2a\,\dfrac{\sin(b\theta)}{\sin(c\theta)} \]

History and Applications

The Trisectrix of Maclaurin1,2, named after Colin Maclaurin (1698–1746), the Scottish mathematician and prominent defender of Newton’s fluxional calculus, is a cubic curve Maclaurin studied in 1742 and presented in his Treatise of Fluxions.3 It belongs to the family of trisectrices, curves constructed to solve the ancient problem of dividing an arbitrary angle into three equal parts, a task impossible in general with straightedge and compass alone.4 Maclaurin’s version is distinguished by its origin as the locus of the intersection of two lines rotating uniformly about separate fixed centers at an angular-speed ratio of 1:3, a generation that directly encodes the triple-angle relation through coordinate geometry rather than mechanical linkages.5 The resulting curve combines a finite looped branch, marked by a crunode (double point) at the origin, with a vertical asymptote. These features arise from the underlying trigonometric structure and were natural subjects for 18th-century analysis of limits, singularities, and polar representations. Unlike purely instrumental solutions, the Maclaurin trisectrix demonstrated how analytic methods could circumvent the quadratic restrictions of classical Euclidean constructions, aligning with Maclaurin’s broader efforts to establish the rigor and power of the Newtonian approach against contemporary philosophical critiques. To trisect a given angle \( \alpha \) with the curve (positioned with its node at the origin at point \( A \) in the figure below and reference point \( M \) at distance \( a \) along the positive x-axis), draw a ray from the point \( (a, 0) \) at angle \( \alpha \) to the x-axis; let \( D \) be its intersection with the trisectrix. The ray drawn from the origin \( A \) through \( D \) then forms the angle \( \alpha/3 \) with the x-axis.5

Historically the trisectrix functioned chiefly as a theoretical and pedagogical instrument rather than a routine tool for draftsmen or engineers. It illustrated the reach of analytic geometry in the calculus era and later informed discussions of algebraic constructibility. The generalized form used here, replacing the fixed integer coefficients with real parameters, moves beyond Maclaurin’s original intent but remains faithful to the analytic spirit of the curve, exploring how the balance of trigonometric frequencies controls branching, closure, and divergence.
