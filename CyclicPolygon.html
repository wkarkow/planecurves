Cyclic Polygon

Iterative sequence of regular polygons of increasing number of sides, either circumscribed about or inscribed in successive circles.

Radius ratio (circumscribed):
\[ R = \dfrac{r}{\cos(\pi/n)} \]
Radius ratio (inscribed):
\[ r = R \cdot \cos(\pi/n) \]
Successive radius (circumscribed):
\[ R_N = r_0 \prod_{n=3}^{N} \dfrac{1}{\cos(\pi/n)} \]
Successive radius (inscribed):
\[ r_N = R_0 \prod_{n=3}^{N} \cos(\pi/n) \]
Polygon circumscribing constant:
\[ \prod_{n=3}^{\infty} \dfrac{1}{\cos(\pi/n)} \approx 8.7000366252\ldots \]
Kepler–Bouwkamp constant:
\[ \prod_{n=3}^{\infty} \cos(\pi/n) \approx 0.1149420448\ldots \]

History and Applications

Two dual constructions are available. In the circumscribed sequence a circle of given radius is drawn and a regular triangle is circumscribed about it so that each side is tangent to the circle. The circumcircle of that triangle is then drawn, a regular square is circumscribed about the new circle, its circumcircle is drawn, a regular pentagon is circumscribed about that, and so on.1 The circumradius of each successive regular \(n\)-gon is the previous radius divided by \(\cos(\pi/n)\). The sequence of radii converges to the polygon circumscribing constant, approximately 8.7000366252 times the initial radius (OEIS A051762).2 In the inscribed sequence a regular triangle is inscribed in the initial circle (vertices on the circle). The incircle of that triangle is drawn, a square is inscribed in the new circle, its incircle is drawn, a pentagon is inscribed, and so on. The inradius of each successive polygon is the previous radius multiplied by \(\cos(\pi/n)\). The sequence converges to the Kepler–Bouwkamp constant, approximately 0.1149420448 times the initial radius (OEIS A085365).3 All figures remain concentric in both sequences.

Christoffel Bouwkamp computed the infinite product and derived accelerated series involving the Riemann zeta function in 1965.4 An earlier popular estimate of roughly 12 for the reciprocal appeared in Kasner and Newman’s Mathematics and the Imagination and was later corrected.5 Johannes Kepler’s 1596 Mysterium Cosmographicum nested regular polyhedra between planetary spheres; the two-dimensional polygonal nesting is related only by analogy and by the later naming of the constant. The irrationality of the constant remains open.3

The construction is a mathematical curiosity that illustrates the convergence of an infinite product and the successive approximation of a circle by regular polygons of increasing side count. It has no documented applications peculiar to engineering or the natural sciences beyond general uses of regular polygons and circles in design, approximation, and visualization of geometric iteration. Related iterative nestings appear in the literature on nested polygons; see the Whirl curve.
