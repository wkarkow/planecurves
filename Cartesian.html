Cartesian Oval

A family of quartic plane curves that produce perfect (stigmatic) point-to-point refraction between two fixed points lying in different optical media.

Cartesian Oval implicit equation:
\[ m\sqrt{(x + a)^{2} + y^{2}} + n\sqrt{(x - a)^{2} + y^{2}} = k \]
Cartesian Oval polar equation:
\[ r =\rho_{1}^{2}\cos\theta+\rho_{2}\pm\sqrt{\rho_{1}^{2}\Bigl[(\rho_{2}+\cos\theta)^{2}+(1-\rho_{1}^{2})\sin^{2}\theta\Bigr]} \]
Cartesian Oval parametric equations:
\[ x =a_{1}+r(\theta)\cos\theta,\qquad y = r(\theta)\sin\theta \]

History and Applications

The Cartesian oval is a family of plane curves introduced by René Descartes in 16371 in his work La Géométrie. Descartes developed these curves while investigating the refraction of light rays passing between two different media, motivated by the need to find surfaces that focus all rays from one point to another perfectly (stigmatic imaging). This work was part of his broader efforts in dioptrics and followed challenges from contemporaries such as Fermat on explaining light bending. Given two fixed points F₁ and F₂, and two positive constants m and n, a Cartesian oval is the set of points P such that m·PF₁ + n·PF₂ = k. The Cartesian oval becomes an ellipse when m = n and a hyperbola if one of m or n is negative.

While not common in mass-market optics, Cartesian ovals have been investigated or used in several technical areas. In high-end optics2 (microscopy, lasers), they offer an ideal aspherical lens surface for perfect point-to-point refraction, eliminating spherical aberration and improving clarity. Certain reflective surfaces (e.g., in telescopes or military optics) use Cartesian oval segments to control multi-medium transitions. Their structure appears in the eikonal equation, which models wave propagation in varying media, particularly in nonlinear optics and plasma physics. In medical ultrasound imaging3, the constant time-of-flight level sets through layered tissues (skin, fat, muscle) are Cartesian ovals, enabling accurate ray-tracing and focusing algorithms that correct refraction-induced aberrations.

Many thanks to Rida T. Farouki, U.C. Davis, for converting the implicit equation into polar coordinates.4
