Duporcq Curve

Family of orthogonal pursuit curves obtained by superposing a Newtonian central-force trajectory of eccentricity e with a uniform translation; the family comprises elliptic, two hyperbolic, and parabolic (Tschirnhausen cubic) members linked to Sturmian spirals.

Duporcq Curve elliptic parametric equations:
\[ x = \dfrac{a}{\sqrt{1-e^{2}}}(e u - \sin u),\quad y = a(-e + \cos u) \]
Duporcq Curve parabolic parametric equations:
\[ x = a(u^{3} - 3u),\quad y = 3a(1 - u^{2}) \]
Duporcq Curve hyperbolic minus parametric equations:
\[ x = \dfrac{a}{\sqrt{e^{2}-1}}(\sinh u - e u),\quad y = a(\cosh u - e) \]
Duporcq Curve hyperbolic plus parametric equations:
\[ x = \dfrac{a}{\sqrt{e^{2}-1}}(\sinh u + e u),\quad y = a(\cosh u + e) \]

History and Applications

The Duporcq curves form a family of plane curves defined by the geometric property that the element of arc length is a constant multiple e of the segment cut by the normals at the ends of that arc on a fixed base line. Ernest Duporcq (1873–1903) and A. Mannheim examined these curves in papers published in 1902 in Nouvelles Annales de Mathématiques.1 F. Balitrand (1914) and especially M. Egan (1919) supplied the complete analytic solution by expressing the curves as the result of superposing a Newtonian central-force trajectory (a conic of eccentricity \( e \) ) with a uniform translation orthogonal to the focal axis.2 C. Masurel (2014) revisited the family through Gregory’s transformation, deriving explicit Cartesian parametric equations for all four regimes of e together with elementary arc-length formulas and the precise link to the associated Sturmian spirals defined by \( R_c = e ρ \).3

Geometrically the curves are the trajectories of a pursuer that always moves perpendicular to the line of sight to a pursuee traveling in a straight line at constant speed; they are therefore classified as orthogonal pursuit curves (sometimes called crab curves). The case \(e = 0\) reduces to an ordinary circle; the parabolic case \(e = 1\) yields Tschirnhausen’s cubic as the Duporcq curve and the Norwich spiral as its Sturmian counterpart, with the pole of the Norwich spiral rolling on a line tracing the cubic.4 The hyperbolic branches \((e > 1)\) produce two distinct families distinguished by the sign in the parametric expressions.

Real-life applications in engineering and the natural sciences are chiefly kinematic. The roulette and glissette constructions that generate the Duporcq curves from Sturmian spirals appear in classical mechanism theory, where rolling or sliding contact between curves is used to produce prescribed motions in cams, gears, and linkages. The orthogonal-pursuit interpretation supplies a geometric model for certain constant-bearing navigation or interception problems, although documented modern engineering deployments of the specific parametric forms remain limited.
