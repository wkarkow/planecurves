History and Applications

The L'Hospital quintic1, also called the curve of constant reaction or curva aequabilis pressionis, originated from a mechanical problem posed by Johann Bernoulli in 1695 (in a letter to Leibniz and in Acta Eruditorum, 1696). Bernoulli sought the plane curve along which a particle descending under uniform gravity experiences a constant normal reaction force from its rigid support. Guillaume de l'Hôpital published the general solution in 1700 in the Mémoires de l'Académie royale des Sciences, including the special algebraic case (k = 1) that yields the quintic. For k > 1 the base curve is transcendental and periodic. Its evolute solves the inverse mechanical problem (also due to Bernoulli): the curve on which to wind the flexible wire of a pendulum such that tension in the wire remains constant throughout the motion.2 In the second image, if the blue pendulum is confined by the red Hospital Quintic Evolute, it will swing with equal tension throughout its arc that follows the black quintic curve itself up to the vertices on either side. This will happen only if the pendulum has only one particular base velocity at the bottom of its arc. And the pendulum bob must rebound with perfect elastic recoil off the dashed green walls. The equations are shown in fully expanded form to make the point that conversion of a curve to its evolute can result in considerable expansion and complexity.

In the natural sciences and classical mechanics these curves model constrained particle motion under gravity with prescribed constant normal reaction, relevant to theoretical studies of tracks or loops where uniform reaction force is required. The evolute specifically provides the profile for constant-tension pendulum mechanisms.
