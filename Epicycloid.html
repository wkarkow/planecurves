Epicycloid

The locus of a point on the circumference of a circle that rolls without slipping around the outside of a fixed circle.

Epicycloid parametric equations:
\[ x=(R+r)\cos\theta - r\cdot\cos\left(\dfrac{R+r}{r}\theta\right),\quad y=(R+r)\sin\theta - r\cdot\sin\left(\dfrac{R+r}{r}\theta\right) \]
Generalized parametric equations:
\[ x=a\cos\theta - b\cos(c\theta),\quad y=d\sin\theta - e\sin(f\theta) \]

History and Applications

The epicycloid1,2,3,4 is a classical curve in the study of kinematics and geometry, defined as the locus of a point on the circumference of a circle as it rolls without slipping around the outside of a fixed circle. The name comes from the Greek roots epi- meaning “upon” and kyklos meaning “circle.” The ancient Greeks explored related rolling-circle motions in their attempts to model planetary orbits (e.g., epicycles in Ptolemaic astronomy) without formal equations. This curve was studied by Hipparchus in the second century BC in the context of modeling planetary motions (e.g., epicycles in Ptolemaic astronomy) using combinations of circular paths. It received further attention from Albrecht Dürer in 1525, Ole Rømer in 1674, and many prominent mathematicians of the 17th and 18th centuries including Christiaan Huygens, Isaac Newton, Gottfried Leibniz, and members of the Bernoulli family. By the 18th century, Leonhard Euler thoroughly classified the cycloidal curves, epicycloids, hypocycloids, and the standard cycloid, as part of his foundational work on the geometry of curves. He formalized their equations and investigated their curvature, cusps, and periodicity.

One of the most enduring practical applications of the epicycloid is in cycloidal gear teeth, particularly in clockmaking and high-efficiency mechanical gear systems. In contrast to the involute profile used in most modern gears, the cycloidal form (derived from both epicycloids and hypocycloids) was historically favored in early clocks due to its lower friction and smooth engagement. Epicycloids also arise in the analysis of epicyclic (planetary) gear trains, where the paths traced by points on rotating gears follow epicycloidal motion relative to a fixed frame. This makes them important in understanding torque transmission in compact mechanical systems such as automatic transmissions and differential gear assemblies.

The epicycloid is the mathematical base for many Spirograph patterns when the pen is placed on the circumference of the rolling disk. These visual representations provide intuitive introductions to the beauty and complexity of cycloidal motion.

For \(x(\theta)\), an epicycloid with a rolling circle of radius \(r\) rolling around a fixed circle of radius \(R\) has the parametric equation \(x(\theta)=(R+r)\cos\theta-r\cdot\cos\bigl(\frac{R+r}{r}\theta\bigr)\), whereas the epitrochoid has \(x(\theta)=(R+r)\cos\theta-d\cdot\cos\bigl(\frac{R+r}{r}\theta\bigr)\) where \(d\neq r\) generalizes the curve. Thus, the epicycloid is just the \(d=r\) case of the epitrochoid. The epicycloid has cusps, the number of which is determined by the ratio \(R/r\) and the curve closes if this ratio is rational.
