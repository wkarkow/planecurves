Harmonic Spiral

Polar spirals that combine classical Archimedean (Poinsot) or logarithmic growth with a sinusoidal modulation of the radius.

Harmonic Proportional Poinsot Spiral:
\[ r = a\,\theta^{b}\,(1 + A\cdot\sin(N\theta + \varphi)) \]
Harmonic Constant Poinsot Spiral:
\[ r = r_{0} + a\,\theta^{b} + A\cdot\sin(N\theta + \varphi) \]
Harmonic Proportional Logarithmic Spiral:
\[ r = (r_{0} + a\cdot e^{k\theta})\,(1 + A\cdot\sin(N\theta + \varphi)) \]
Harmonic Constant Logarithmic Spiral:
\[ r = r_{0} + a\cdot e^{k\theta} + A\cdot\sin(N\theta + \varphi) \]

History and Applications

The Harmonic Spiral variants combine features of classical spirals with a sinusoidal modulation of the radius. The proportional forms build upon the Archimedean spiral, known since Archimedes (c. 287–212 BCE), where radius grows linearly with angle.1 The logarithmic forms trace their origins to René Descartes and later Jakob Bernoulli, who named the equiangular spiral spira mirabilis for its self-similar growth property.2 Louis Poinsot (1777–1859), a French mathematician, studied related spiral trajectories in central force problems, although his namesake spirals use hyperbolic functions.3 The addition of the harmonic term \(A \cdot \sin(N\theta + \varphi)\) introduces periodic undulations, creating wavy or rippled spirals.

In engineering and the natural sciences, modulated spirals have practical uses. In telecommunications, spiral modulation techniques employ complex spiral waveforms in the complex plane to achieve higher spectral efficiency than traditional periodic modulation.4 In optics and photonics, radial phase-modulated spiral zone plates and spiral phase elements generate structured light beams, including autofocusing and multi-spiral beams useful for optical trapping and laser processing.5 Sinusoidal corrugated tube structures, sharing the periodic radial variation concept, are widely applied in mechanical engineering for controlled energy absorption during impacts, such as in vehicle crashworthiness components.6
