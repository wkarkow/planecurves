Syntrepent

A mating pitch curve that rolls without slip on a circle whose hub lies on the circle, both members rotating about fixed centers.

Syntrepent parametric equations:
\[ r = a\bigl(k - \cos t\bigr),\qquad \theta(t) = \int \frac{\cos t}{\cos t - k}\, dt \]
Distance between rotational centers:
\[ d = ka \]
For a number of lobes \(= n\):
\[ k = -\dfrac{n+1}{\sqrt{2n+1}} \]

History and Applications

The syntrepent curves,1 literally “curves that turn together,” are pairs of mating pitch curves (profiles) designed so that two gears or mechanisms roll on each other without slipping while rotating about fixed centers (hubs). The term was introduced by the French geometer Auguste Miquel in 1838 in his studies of contact curves and roulettes; Leonhard Euler had earlier explored related properties of rolling curves and their envelopes in the context of mechanism geometry.2 These curves satisfy the fundamental kinematic requirement that the arc lengths traversed along each profile remain equal at every instant of contact, a condition that follows directly from the no-slip condition and can be expressed through a definite integral that accumulates the angular displacement of one curve relative to the other.

Real-life applications appear throughout mechanical engineering wherever periodic, non-uniform angular velocity is required without auxiliary cams or linkages. Non-circular gears whose pitch curves belong to the syntrepent or limaçon family are employed in agricultural machinery (for example, parallelogram planting mechanisms that use denatured Pascal-limaçon gears to produce precise, asymmetric insertion motions),4 in packaging and printing equipment for synchronized variable-speed transport, in textile and light-industry drives needing smooth speed modulation within each revolution, and in specialized speed-control transmissions such as historical logarithmic-spiral “varistart” systems.1 Because one member can remain a conventional circular gear, manufacturing complexity is reduced while the custom syntrepent mate supplies the exact kinematic law. The same rolling-without-slip principle underlies certain cam designs and, at the limit of infinite hub distance, the classic wheel-and-road contact problem.
