Parabola Inverse Curve

The image of the standard parabola \(X=at^{2}\), \(Y=2at\) under inversion in a circle centered at (0,0).

Parabola Inverse Curve parametric equations:
\[ x = x_{0} + \dfrac{k(a t^{2}-x_{0})}{(a t^{2}-x_{0})^{2}+(2 a t-y_{0})^{2}},\quad y = y_{0} + \dfrac{k(2 a t-y_{0})}{(a t^{2}-x_{0})^{2}+(2 a t-y_{0})^{2}} \]

History and Applications

The Parabola Inverse Curve1,2 is obtained by applying geometric inversion to a standard parabola with respect to a circle. Given a parabola parametrized by \(x(t)=at^{2}\), \(y(t)=2at\), and inversion with respect to a circle of center \((x_{0},y_{0})\) and radius \(k\), each point P on the parabola is mapped to a point P′ lying on the same ray from the center such that the product of the distances from the center to P and to P′ equals \(k^{2}\). This transformation belongs to inversive geometry3, which maps generalized circles (circles and straight lines) to generalized circles while preserving angles—a property useful for simplifying certain constructions in plane geometry. Inversive geometry3 was explored sporadically in antiquity but received systematic study in the nineteenth century by mathematicians such as Jakob Steiner. Special positions of the inversion center yield classical curves. When the center coincides with the parabola vertex, the inverse is the cissoid of Diocles. When it coincides with the focus, the result is a cardioid.

Real-life applications specifically of the general Parabola Inverse Curve in engineering and the natural sciences are not documented in the primary mathematical references consulted. The underlying inversion technique, however, enables practical mechanisms such as the Peaucellier–Lipkin linkage in mechanical engineering, which converts circular motion into exact straight-line motion. The special-case cardioid appears in acoustics as the directional sensitivity pattern of cardioid microphones.

In applied settings, inverse curves appear most naturally in problems involving conformal mapping, optical ray tracing, and geometric constructions where distance relationships relative to a fixed point are central. For the art world, the inverse of a parabola, for appropriate parameter values, gives rise to a paisley-like form.

        skm '(T T T)

        a2 a1

        y1 (eval yc)

        x2 '(eval xc)
        y2 '(eval yc))
