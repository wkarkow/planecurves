Antiparallelogram Coupler Curve

The path traced by a point on the connecting rod of a crossed antiparallelogram four-bar linkage mechanism, generating a rational bicircular quartic curve.

Antiparallelogram Coupler Curve parametric equations:
\[ x = (1 - c) \cdot (a + 2b \cdot \cos(\theta)) + c \cdot (-a + 2b \cdot \cos(\phi)) \]
\[ y = (1 - c) \cdot (2b \cdot \sin(\theta)) + c \cdot (2b \cdot \sin(\phi)) \]
\[ \text{where}\quad \cos(\phi) = \frac{(a^{2} + b^{2}) \cdot \cos(\theta) + 2ab}{a^{2} + b^{2} + 2ab \cdot \cos(\theta)},\quad \sin(\phi) = \sin(\theta) \cdot \frac{b^{2} - a^{2}}{a^{2} + b^{2} + 2ab \cdot \cos(\theta)} \]

History and Applications

The antiparallelogram coupler curve1, also known as a butterfly or bow-tie linkage curve, traces the path of a point on the connecting rod of a crossed four-bar mechanism where opposite sides are equal: the two cranks (rotating links) have length 2b pivoting at fixed points separated by 2a, and the coupler (connecting rod) also spans 2a. As one crank rotates uniformly, the other follows, generating the curve through basic geometric constraints, no advanced math needed beyond trigonometry for the angles and distances. This setup, a special case of three-bar linkages, was studied kinematically by Gabriel Koenigs at the end of the 19th century, building on Gaston Darboux's 1879 work using elliptic functions to analyze planar quadrilaterals2. The term "antiparallelogram" dates back further, appearing in Frans van Schooten's 1646 treatise on conic sections and Robert Muirhead's 1901 paper on isosceles trapeziums3. Unlike parallelograms that produce circles, the crossed antiparallelogram allows full rotations and yields rational bicircular quartics—curves symmetric about one axis but not left-right due to driving one crank (mirroring by switching drivers).

Real-life applications span engineering fields. In mechanism design, it features in Hart's inversor for converting rotary to straight-line motion, used historically in precision instruments4. Modern uses include deployable structures like space antennas and masts, where antiparallelogram loops enable compact folding and expansion5. In robotics, multi-mode mobile mechanisms based on antiparallelogram units adapt to terrains by switching between rolling and obstacle-climbing modes, enhancing adaptability in rough environments6. Gear design employs it for non-circular elliptical gears that transform uniform rotation to variable speeds, applied in pumps and compressors7. In celestial mechanics, it models central configurations in the four-body problem for equal-mass bodies rotating rigidly, aiding simulations of astronomical motions8.
