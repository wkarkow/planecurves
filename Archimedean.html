Archimedean Spiral

The locus of a point moving away from a fixed origin at constant linear speed along a line that rotates at constant angular velocity.

Archimedean Spiral polar equation:
\[ r = a + b \cdot \theta \]

History and Applications

The Archimedean spiral, also known as the arithmetic spiral, is the locus of points corresponding to the location over time of a point moving away from a fixed origin at constant linear speed along a line that rotates at constant angular velocity. Any ray from the origin intersects successive turnings of the spiral at points separated by the constant distance 2πb (when θ is measured in radians). Archimedes first described the curve around 225 BC in his work On Spirals; his friend Conon of Samos had considered it earlier.1 The neoid (also called the swan-like curve or streamline) is simply the Archimedean spiral extended to negative angles and is not treated separately here. A hyperbolic spiral is the inverse curve of an Archimedean spiral (with inversion center at the origin); the inverse of an Archimedean spiral with constant a with respect to a circle centered at the origin is another Archimedean spiral with constant −a. The curve is the pedal of the involute of a circle and the conchoid of the Archimedean spiral is itself an Archimedean spiral.2

The spiral has numerous real-life applications in engineering and the natural sciences. Scroll compressors use two interleaved curves that closely resemble Archimedean spirals to compress gases. The coils of watch balance springs follow the spiral for uniform spacing. The grooves of very early gramophone records were cut as Archimedean spirals to keep the spacing even (variable track spacing came later to fit more music). Physicians ask patients to draw an Archimedean spiral by hand as a simple way to quantify tremor.

The curve models the pattern formed by a roll of paper or tape of constant thickness wrapped around a cylinder and powers Wirtz pumps. It also converts uniform angular motion into uniform linear motion: a cam made of two branches of the spiral, rotated at constant speed about its center, drives a straight follower through the origin at constant linear speed; this principle was used in some older sewing machines. For the quadrature of the circle, the spiral provides a direct construction: at angle π the radius equals the required length ½ times a given line. Angle trisection (and division into n equal parts) is likewise straightforward because the angle is linear with radius. Three-dimensionally the curve is the orthogonal projection (onto a plane perpendicular to the axis) of the spiral cone of Pappus.1

In nature the form appears in pinecones. Some ammonites have the shape of an Archimedean spiral (while most ammonites follow a logarithmic spiral). The Cesàro equation relates radius of curvature ρ to arc length s: ρ = s.2 Also see the Spiral entry for more generalized spiral options.

AutoLISP Code

(defun C:ARCHIMEDEAN (/ basept xaxisangle cd sa sb sm om os divcount cosangle sinangle x0 y0 plineents segcount baseinfo paramstep
                      divpt xstep restartflag id iq dcl opt kw mode dpr dct dpt span skm eq_display xc yc a1 x1 y1 a2 x2 y2 fa fb fc fd p1 rval)
  (setq id "Archimedean Spiral"
        iq "r=a+b·θ"
        dcl "ARCHIMEDEAN"
        opt '("a,b values" "curve point")
        kw 0
        mode 'polar
        dpr "# of divisions around circle"
        dct 360
        dpt nil
        span 360
        kw (ca id iq dcl opt kw mode dpr dct dpt span))
  (cond
    ((= kw 0)
      (setq fa (get_real 0 "a (rotation)" 0.0)
            fb (get_real 0 "b (growth rate)" 1.0)))
    ((= kw 1)
     (setq fa (get_real 0 "a (rotation)" 0.0)
           p1 (get_point 0 "Point on curve" (polar basept xaxisangle 1.0) basept)
           fc (get_real 0 "# of extra revolutions to point" 0.0)
           fd (+ (* fc (* 2 pi)) (- (angle basept p1) xaxisangle))
           fd (if (= fd 0.0) (+ (* (1+ fc) (* 2 pi)) (- (angle basept p1) xaxisangle)) fd)
           fb (/ (- (distance basept p1) fa) fd))))
  (setq eq_display (strcat "\na=" (rtos fa 2 4) ", b=" (rtos fb 2 4))
        rval '(+ fa (* fb (eval a2)))
        xc '(* (eval rval) (cos (eval a2)))
        yc '(* (eval rval) (sin (eval a2)))
        skm '(nil T nil)
        a1 0 
        a2 a1
        x1 (eval xc)
        y1 (eval yc)
        a2 '(+ a1 paramstep)
        x2 '(eval xc)
        y2 '(eval yc))
  (cc))
(C:ARCHIMEDEAN)
