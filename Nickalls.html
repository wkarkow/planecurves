Nickalls-Pulfrich Curve

Apparent path of a vertical rod fixed on a horizontal turntable rotating at constant angular velocity, viewed binocularly from the side with a light-reducing filter before one eye.

Nickalls-Pulfrich parametric equations:
\[ x=\dfrac{2b\cos^{2}(t-a)+2c(b\cos a-c\sin a)\cos(t-a)+(b\cos 2a-c\sin 2a-b)}{2(b\cos a+c\sin a)\cos(t-a)+\sin a\cos a+b\cdot c} \]
\[ y=\dfrac{b\sin(t-a)\cos(t-a)+(b\cdot c\cos a+b^{2}\sin a)\sin(t-a)}{(b\cos a+c\sin a)\cos(t-a)+\sin a\cos a+b\cdot c} \]

History and Applications

The Nickalls-Pulfrich curve1,2 describes the apparent path traced by a vertical rod fixed on a horizontal turntable rotating at constant angular velocity when viewed binocularly from the side with a light-reducing filter before one eye. This is a specific geometric realization of the Pulfrich effect, an optical illusion first reported in 1922 by German physicist Carl Pulfrich while investigating stereoscopic photometry. Pulfrich observed that dimming the image to one eye (via a filter) caused objects moving in a frontal plane to appear to follow curved trajectories in depth because the filtered eye processes visual information with a measurable delay relative to the other eye.2 In 1986 R. W. D. Nickalls (a British anesthetist and mathematician) analyzed the rotating-turntable case and derived the equations for the apparent locus of the rod, showing that the curve’s shape depends on: the latency angle \(a\) (half the angular delay), \(b\) (half the interpupillary distance divided by rod-to-center radius), and \(c\) (observer-to-center distance divided by rod-to-center radius).2 Varying the latency angle while holding distances fixed produces families of curves that reverse apparent rotation sense. Nickalls’ derivation also furnished a simple, sensitive clinical method for quantifying small inter-ocular latency differences without additional pointers or complex apparatus.2

Real-life applications lie in ophthalmology and neurology by detecting and measuring unilateral delays in the visual pathway, such as those caused by optic neuritis in multiple sclerosis, cataracts, ischaemic optic neuropathy, or facial trauma. Neutral-density filters placed before the unaffected eye can neutralize the illusory depth and restore normal perception, providing both diagnostic confirmation and therapeutic management. In astronomy the same latency difference contributes to the classical “personal equation” that produces small systematic timing discrepancies among observers of moving celestial objects.3

With default parameters of \(b=\frac{1}{3}\) and \(c=10\), the curve turns into a single line at the transition value of the half-latency angle, \(\frac{1}{30}\) radians = 1.909152432996376° or \(\arctan(\frac{b}{c})\).

        fc (get_real 2 "c = eye to turntable center/rod to turntable center" 10.0)
        fd (* pi (/ fa 180.0))
        eq_display (strcat "\na=" (rtos fa 2 4) ", b=" (rtos fb 2 4) ", c=" (rtos fc 2 4))
        fe '(+ (* (+ (* fb (cos fd)) (* fc (sin fd))) (cos (- (eval a2) fd))) (* (sin fd) (cos fd)) (* fb fc))
        xc '(/ (+ (* 2.0 fb (expt (cos (- (eval a2) fd)) 2)) (* 2.0 fc (+ (* fb (cos fd)) (* -1.0 fc (sin fd))) (cos (- (eval a2) fd)))
            (+ (* fb (cos (* 2.0 fd))) (* -1.0 fc (sin (* 2.0 fd))) (* -1.0 fb))) (* 2.0 (avoid_zero (eval fe))))
        yc '(/ (+ (* fb (sin (- (eval a2) fd)) (cos (- (eval a2) fd))) (* (+ (* fb fc (cos fd)) (* fb fb (sin fd))) (sin (- (eval a2) fd)))) (avoid_zero (eval fe)))
        maxdraw (if (< fc 1)(get_real 2 "Maximum x and y values allowed?" 5) nil)
        skm '(T T T)
        a1 0
        a2 a1
        x1 (eval xc)
        y1 (eval yc)
        a2 '(+ a1 paramstep)
        x2 '(eval xc)
        y2 '(eval yc))
  (cc))
