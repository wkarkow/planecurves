Hoerl Curve

A power-exponential product curve that starts at the origin, rises to a single peak at x = b/c, and then decays exponentially to zero.

Hoerl Curve Cartesian equation:
\[ y = a \, x^{b} \, e^{-c x} \]

History and Applications

The Hoerl curve1, also known as the Hoerl function, takes the form \( y = a \cdot x^{b} \cdot e^{-c x} \). It is named after statistician Arthur E. Hoerl, who presented practical methods for fitting curves of this type to data in the 1954 Chemical Business Handbook.2 The function combines a power-law term with exponential decay. For positive \(a, b\), and \(c\), the curve starts at the origin, rises with an initially increasing then decreasing positive slope to a single peak at \(x=\frac{b}{c}\), and then decays exponentially toward zero as \(x\) grows large. This shape arises directly from the competing effects of the power term (which can accelerate early growth) and the exponential decay (which dominates at larger \(x\)).

In engineering and the natural sciences the curve serves as a standard parametric model for data that exhibit a rise followed by exponential tail-off. Actuarial science employs it extensively for stochastic claims reserving in general insurance.3 There the shape matches the typical pattern of incremental claim payments over development periods: payments increase rapidly at first and then decline. In physics, the specific case \(a=-1\) (with \(x\) replaced by distance) appears in models of the strong nuclear force between nucleons.1 In acoustics and psychoacoustics, the case \(a=1\) contributes to auditory filter models that describe frequency selectivity in the human inner ear; when summed with an exponential term it yields the rounded-exponential (roex) filter.1 The reciprocal of the Hoerl curve describes vapor-pressure relationships in thermodynamics.1 In agriculture and ecology, it describes growth rates constrained by nutrient depletion or competition. In forestry, it models annual biomass increment versus tree age. In reliability studies, it can approximate unimodal hazard functions where a failure rate first increases and then decreases. It endures mainly as a flexible empirical model rather than any fundamental law.

AutoLISP Code

(defun C:HOERL (/ basept xaxisangle cd sa sb sm om os divcount cosangle sinangle x0 y0 plineents segcount baseinfo paramstep
                  divpt xspan restartflag id iq dcl opt kw mode dpr dct dpt span eq_display xc yc a1 a2 x1 y1 x2 y2 fa fb fc)
  (setq id "Hoerl Curve"
        iq "y=a·xᵇ·e^(-c·x)"
        dcl nil
        opt nil
        kw nil
        mode 'param
        dpr "# of divisions to x-span of division point"
        dct 200
        dpt '(20.0 0.0)
        span nil
        kw (ca id iq dcl opt kw mode dpr dct dpt span)
        fa (get_real 2 "a (scale)" 1.0)
        fb (get_real 2 "b (power)" 2.0)
        fc (get_real 2 "c (decay)" 1.0)
        eq_display (strcat "\na=" (rtos fa 2 4) ", b=" (rtos fb 2 4) ", c=" (rtos fc 2 4))
        xc '(eval a2)
        yc '(* fa (alt_expt (eval a2) fb nil) (exp (- (* fc (eval a2)))))
        paramstep (/ xspan divcount)
        skm '(T T T)
        a1 0
        a2 a1
        x1 (eval xc)
        y1 (eval yc)
        a2 '(+ a1 paramstep)
        x2 '(eval xc)
        y2 '(eval yc))
  (cc))
(C:HOERL)
