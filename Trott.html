Trott Curve

A smooth plane quartic with four separate ovals in 90° rotational symmetry that attains the classical maximum of 28 real bitangents.

Trott Curve implicit equation:
\[ 144(x^{4} + y^{4}) - 225(x^{2} + y^{2}) + 350x^{2}y^{2} + 81 = 0 \]

History and Applications

The Trott curve is a smooth plane quartic that realizes the maximum possible number of 28 real bitangents. It was introduced by Michael Trott in 1997 as an explicit real algebraic example attaining this classical upper bound for smooth plane quartics.1,2 The particular coefficient set chosen produces a curve whose real locus in the affine plane consists of four separate ovals arranged with 90° rotational symmetry, rather than a single connected component. This realization is convenient for visualization and computational testing because each oval is compact and the bitangents can be studied both within individual ovals and across the symmetric arrangement.

In algebraic geometry the curve serves as a standard benchmark for algorithms that compute real bitangents, determine the topology of real algebraic curves, and render high-degree plane curves without missing components or introducing spurious gaps. In computer algebra systems it is routinely used to test plotting routines, numerical stability of resultant-based methods, and the handling of curves with many real inflectional and bitangent lines. While direct engineering applications of this specific coefficient set are limited, the broader study of bitangents on quartics connects to classical problems in optics (caustics and envelopes) and mechanism theory, where bitangent lines appear as common tangents to coupler curves or as lines of stationary curvature.
