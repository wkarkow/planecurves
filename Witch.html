Witch of Agnesi

Symmetric bell with a horizontal asymptote, drawn as x = a tan(t), y = b [cos(t)]^c.

Witch of Agnesi Curve Cartesian equation:
\[ y = \dfrac{b \, a^{2}}{a^{2} + x^{2}} \]
Generalized Cartesian equations:
\[ y = \dfrac{b \, a^{c}}{(a^{2} + x^{2})^{c/2}} \]
Witch of Agnesi Curve parametric equations:
\[ x = a \cdot \tan(t), \quad y = b \cdot \cos(t)^{2} \]
Generalized parametric equations:
\[ x = a \cdot \tan(t), \quad y = b \cdot \cos(t)^{c} \]

History and Applications

The Witch of Agnesi1,2,3, named after Maria Gaetana Agnesi (1718–1799), is a plane curve that she presented in her 1748 calculus textbook Instituzioni analitiche ad uso della gioventù italiana, the first major calculus work written by a woman. The curve had been studied earlier by Pierre de Fermat around 1630 and by Guido Grandi in 1703. Agnesi called it versiera, a nautical term for a rope that turns a sail or a versed sine curve; the English name “witch” arose from a mistranslation of versiera as avversiera (“she-devil”) by John Colson in 1801.

The same functional form appears in the natural sciences and engineering as the Lorentzian (or Cauchy) profile. In physics it models resonance phenomena in electrical circuits, where power dissipation near the resonant frequency follows this symmetric bell shape that falls off with distance from the peak. The identical shape describes the intensity distribution of spectral lines in optics and spectroscopy4, giving the characteristic linewidth caused by natural (homogeneous) broadening or pressure effects; the same Lorentzian profile governs line shapes in nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra because the Fourier transform of an exponential free-induction decay is Lorentzian.5 In particle physics the Breit–Wigner distribution, which is mathematically a Lorentzian, describes the energy distribution of resonant particles.

As the probability density function of the Cauchy distribution, the curve is used in hydrology to model extreme events such as annual maximum one-day rainfalls and river discharges, where heavy tails capture the higher probability of rare, catastrophic floods compared with normal distributions. In signal processing and communications engineering the Cauchy distribution models noise and interference that exhibit occasional large spikes. The curve also approximates the cross-section of smooth hills and serves as a generic topographic obstacle in mathematical modeling of fluid flow; solitary waves in deep water can take this shape as well.

(defun C:WITCH (/ basept xaxisangle cd sa sb sm om os divcount cosangle sinangle x0 y0 plineents segcount baseinfo paramstep
                  divpt restartflag id iq dcl opt kw mode dpr dct dpt span eq_display xc yc skm a1 a2 x1 y1 x2 y2 fa fb fc)
  (setq id "Witch of Agnesi"
        iq "x=fa·tan(t), y=fb·(cos(t))^{fc}"
        dcl nil
        opt nil
        kw nil
        mode 'polar
        dpr "# of divisions to x-span of division point"
        dct 360
        dpt nil
        span 180
        kw (ca id iq dcl opt kw mode dpr dct dpt span)
        fa (get_real 2 "a (horizontal scale / width control)" 1.0)
        fb (get_real 2 "b (peak height, 1/π for Cauchy Distribution if a=1)" 0.31830988618)
        fc (get_real 2 "c (exponent on cos(t); higher values narrow the peak, 2 for Cauchy Distribution)" 2.0)
        eq_display (strcat "\na=" (rtos fa 2 4) ", b=" (rtos fb 2 4) ", c=" (rtos fc 2 4))
        xc '(* fa (tan (eval a2)))
        yc '(* fb (alt_expt (cos (eval a2)) fc nil))
        skm '(T nil T)
        a1 (/ pi -2.0)
        a2 a1
        x1 (eval xc)
        y1 (eval yc)
        a2 '(+ a1 paramstep)
        x2 '(eval xc)
        y2 '(eval yc))
  (cc))
(C:WITCH)
