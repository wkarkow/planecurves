Joachimsthal Tetracuspid

Envelope of a fixed-length segment whose ends slide on two lines at angle α to the x-axis; reduces to an astroid when α=45°.

Joachimsthal tetracuspid equation:
\[ x=a\cos\alpha\,(u+v)\bigl(1-2uv\sin^2\alpha\bigr), \quad y=a\sin\alpha\,(v-u)\bigl(1+2uv\cos^2\alpha\bigr), \quad (u+v)^2=1+4uv\cos^2\alpha. \]
Single-parameter form:
\[ x=\frac{a^2}{2}\sin\theta\Bigl(\frac{2}{\tan\alpha}-\frac{a^2}{\tan\alpha}+\frac{a^2\cos^2\theta}{\sin\alpha\cos\alpha}\Bigr), \quad y=\frac{a^2}{2}\cos\theta\Bigl(2\tan\alpha-a^2\tan\alpha+\frac{a^2\sin^2\theta}{\sin\alpha\cos\alpha}\Bigr)\]

History and Applications

The Joachimsthal tetracuspid is the envelope of a line segment of fixed length \(a\) whose endpoints slide on two fixed lines that intersect at an angle \(2\alpha\).1 The problem was posed by Merlieux in 1842 and solved by Ferdinand Joachimsthal in 1847.1 Joachimsthal (1818–1861), a German mathematician, is also remembered for his work on the theory of surfaces and for notations used in the study of conics.2 When the two guiding lines are perpendicular (\(\alpha=45^\circ\)), the envelope reduces to an astroid (a four-cusped hypocycloid).1 For other angles the curve remains a rational sextic with four outward-pointing cusps located at \((\pm a\cos\alpha,\pm a\sin\alpha)\). Points fixed on the moving segment itself trace ellipses aligned with the coordinate axes.1 The original parametric equations are expressed in the auxiliary quantities \(u\) and \(v\) linked by the length constraint. Substituting the explicit solution of that constraint in terms of the segment angle \(\theta\), \(u=\frac a2\Bigl(\frac{\sin\theta}{\sin\alpha}-\frac{\cos\theta}{\cos\alpha}\Bigr)\), \(v=\frac a2\Bigl(\frac{\sin\theta}{\sin\alpha}+\frac{\cos\theta}{\cos\alpha}\Bigr)\) and simplifying produces the single-parameter form shown above, free of \(u\) and \(v\).

No engineering or scientific applications peculiar to the general-\(\alpha\) Joachimsthal tetracuspid appear in the literature. The special case \(\alpha=45^\circ\) inherits the well-known uses of the astroid (e.g., as a caustic, in certain linkage mechanisms, and as the evolute of an ellipse).3
