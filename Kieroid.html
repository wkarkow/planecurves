Kieroid Curve

A rational quartic algebraic curve determined by a circle and two lines.

Kieroid implicit equation:
\[ y^{2}(x-a)^{2} + x^{2}(x-b)^{2} = c^{2}x^{2} \]
Kieroid polar equation:
\[ r = \dfrac{a}{\cos t} + (b-a)\cos t \pm \sqrt{c^{2}-(a-b)^{2}\sin^{2}t} \]

History and Applications

The Kieroid curve1 was studied by P. J. Kiernan in 1945 and named after him in R. C. Yates’ A Handbook on Curves and Their Properties (1947).2 It is a rational quartic algebraic curve constructed as the locus of intersection points of a circle of fixed radius c centered at point P (moving vertically on the line x = b) with the line ON, where N moves vertically on the parallel line x = a and shares the same ordinate with P. The resulting curve has a singular point at the origin and lies in the strip between x = a and x = b (or is bounded otherwise if a < b), with x = a as an asymptote when a < b. The general polar equation unifies several classical curves through parameter selection. When a = b + c, the curve decomposes into a right rational circular cubic plus the asymptote x = a; all such cubics arise this way. Specific cases include the Cissoid of Diocles (when a = 2b = 2c), the right strophoid (a = c, b = 0), the Maclaurin trisectrix (2a = −2b = c), Visiera (when a = b − c), conchoids of lines (when a = b), and piriform-like shapes (when b = c and a ≫ 2b). These special cases trace to ancient Greek geometry. Diocles (c. 180 BC) invented the Cissoid of Diocles3 to solve the Delian problem of doubling the cube. Nicomedes (c. 200 BC) introduced the Conchoid of Nicomedes4 for neusis constructions including angle trisection and cube duplication; it later appeared in 17th-century mechanics. Colin Maclaurin examined the trisectrix5 bearing his name in 1742 for the same trisection task.

No real-life applications in engineering or the natural sciences were identified for the Kieroid curve itself. Its value lies in the unified parametric generation of the classical special cases, useful in geometric constructions and CAD.
