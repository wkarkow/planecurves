Klein Quartic

A plane algebraic curve of degree four that realizes the highest possible symmetry for a genus-3 Riemann surface.

Klein Quartic implicit polar equation:
\[ (r^{2} - b^{2})^{2} = c \cdot a \cdot (a^{3} - 2 r^{3} \sin(3\theta) - 3 a r^{2}) \]

History and Applications

The Klein quartic1,2, named after Felix Klein (1849–1925), is a plane algebraic curve of degree four first studied by the German mathematician in his 1879 paper on seventh-order transformations of elliptic functions. Klein explored the curve while investigating modular functions and the symmetries of the modular group reduced modulo 7. The curve is remarkable for realizing the highest possible symmetry for its type: it corresponds to a Riemann surface of genus 3 with an automorphism group of order 168 (336 if reflections are included), achieving the Hurwitz bound. In the real affine plane the curve can be plotted using the implicit polar form shown below; depending on the relationship between parameters b and c it ranges from smooth loops to forms with cusps. Its tangents at the six inflection points form equilateral triangles, a property shared with certain other quartics such as the Loriga quartic. Topologically the associated compact surface is equivalent to a three-holed torus. The high symmetry group and hyperbolic tiling properties extend the idea of Platonic solids into hyperbolic geometry, with 24 regular heptagons meeting three at each vertex.

Real-life applications in engineering and the natural sciences are limited; no direct uses in drafting, manufacturing, optics, or ultrasound imaging appear in the literature. The negative-curvature tilings associated with the curve have, however, inspired theoretical models in materials chemistry for low-density polymeric carbon allotropes (such as schwarzites) and analogous boron-nitride structures.3
