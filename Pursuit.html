Pursuit Curve

The path of a pursuer that moves at constant speed while always directing its velocity toward the current position of a target traveling in a straight line at constant speed.

Pursuit Curve traveler equations:
\[ x_{t} = x_{t0} + s_{t}\, t,\qquad y_{t} = 0 \]
Pursuit Curve dog equations:
\[ \dfrac{dx}{dt} = \dfrac{s_{d}(x_{t}-x)}{\sqrt{(x_{t}-x)^{2}+y^{2}}},\qquad \dfrac{dy}{dt} = \dfrac{-s_{d}\, y}{\sqrt{(x_{t}-x)^{2}+y^{2}}} \]

History and Applications

The pursuit curve, often called the dog curve, describes the path traced by a pursuer that moves at constant speed while always directing its velocity vector directly toward the current position of a target moving along a straight line at constant speed. Leonardo da Vinci considered the idea conceptually, but the first systematic mathematical analysis was given by the French scientist and hydrographer Pierre Bouguer in 1732 in a memoir on naval tactics.1,2 The term “pursuit curve” itself entered the literature later in the nineteenth century. In the classic straight-line case coded here, a traveler moves along the x-axis with position \(x_{t}=x_{t0}+s_{t}\cdot t\), \(y_{t}=0\). The dog starts at offset position \((x_{d0},\,y_{d0}>0)\) with constant speed \(s_{d}\) and always heads directly toward the traveler. Its motion is governed by the pair of coupled differential equations that keeps its velocity aligned with the instantaneous line of sight to the target. Derivatives here represent velocities (rate of change of position with time), a basic calculus concept familiar to engineers who work with slopes and rates. The code generates the curve by stepwise numerical integration of these equations, advancing the state variables (time and position) incrementally. When \(s_{d}>s_{t}\) the dog intercepts the traveler; when \(s_{d}\le s_{t}\) capture does not occur and the path approaches an asymptote parallel to the traveler’s line. In the images above, the traveler starts from the origin, moving along the x-axis.

In engineering and the natural sciences the geometry appears directly in guidance systems. Pure pursuit guidance, a literal implementation of this rule, has been used in missile control and aircraft targeting studies, where the interceptor continuously steers to point toward the current target location.3

        iq "xt=xt₀+st·t (yt=0); dog: x'=sd·(xt-x)/R , y'=-sd·y/R"
        dcl nil
        opt nil
        kw nil
        mode 'param
        dpr "# of divisions to division point"
        dct 200
        dpt '(2.0 0.0)
        span nil
        kw (ca id iq dcl opt kw mode dpr dct dpt span)

        fa (get_real 2 "st (speed of traveler, -= go left)" 1.0)
        fb (get_real 2 "sd (speed of dog, -= flee): " 2.0)
        fc (get_real 0 "xt₀ (traveler start x)" 0.0)
        fd (get_real 0 "xd₀ (dog start x)" 0.0)
        fe (get_real 2 "yd₀ (dog start y)" 3.0)
        eq_display (strcat "\nst=" (rtos fa 2 4) ", sd=" (rtos fb 2 4) ", xt₀=" (rtos fc 2 4) ", xd₀=" (rtos fd 2 4) ", yd₀=" (rtos fe 2 4))
        ff 0.0
        fg fc
        fh fd
        fi fe
        fj 1
        fk 0
        a2 '(progn (setq fl (abs xstep)) (if (= fj 1) (progn (setq fj 0) ff) (progn (if (= fk 1) (progn (setq ff  (+ ff fl) fg (+ fg (* fa fl)) fh fg fi 0.0) ff)
            (progn (setq fm (- fg fh) fn  (sqrt (+ (* fm fm) (* fi fi))) fo (* (abs fb) fl)) (if (or (< (abs fn) 1e-9) (<= fo fn)) (if (< (abs fn) 1e-9) ff
            (progn (setq fp (/ (* fb fl) (avoid_zero fn))) (setq fh (+ fh (* fp fm)) fi (+ fi (* fp (- fi)))) (setq ff  (+ ff fl) fg (+ fg (* fa fl))) (if (and
            (< (abs fi) 1e-9) (< (- fg fh) 0.0)) (setq fh fg fi 0.0 fk 1)) ff)) (progn (setq fh fg fi 0.0 fk 1) (setq ff  (+ ff fl) fg (+ fg (* fa fl))) ff)))))))
        x2 'fh
        y2 'fi
        xc x2
        yc y2
        skm '(T T T)
        a1 0.0
        x1 fh
        y1 fi
        restartflag nil)
  (cc))
