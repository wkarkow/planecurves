Sine Trochoid

Roulette of a circle of radius \(a\) that rolls without slip on the sine wave \(y=\sin x\).

Sine Trochoid parametric equations:
\[ \mathbf{r}(t)=\begin{pmatrix}t\\ \sin t\end{pmatrix}+a'\,\mathbf{N}(t)+d'\,\mathbf{U}(t) \]
Unit normal and rotated tracer:
\[ \mathbf{N}(t)=\pm\dfrac{(-\cos t,\,1)}{\sqrt{1+\cos^{2}t}},\qquad \phi=\dfrac{s}{a'},\qquad \mathbf{U}(t)=\mathrm{rot}(\phi)\,\mathbf{N}(t) \]
Arc length and input scaling:
\[ s=\int\sqrt{1+\cos^{2}t}\,dt,\qquad s_{\text{period}}\approx 7.640395578055424,\qquad k=\dfrac{s_{\text{period}}}{2\pi}\approx 1.21600672342498 \]
Scaled rolling radius and tracer distance:
\[ a'=k\cdot a,\qquad d'=k\cdot d \]

History and Applications

The generalized trochoid is the curve traced by a point fixed at a distance \(d\) from the center of a circle of radius \(a\) that rolls without slipping on an arbitrary base curve. The classical theory of roulettes and trochoids was developed in the seventeenth century by Roberval, Pascal, and others in connection with the cycloid and its generalizations; the kinematic condition that the rotation angle equals arc length divided by radius is the standard definition used in mechanism theory.1 The specific case of a sinusoidal base curve is a modern illustrative example that produces rich families of loops and cusps when the rolling rate and tracing distance are varied. Such constructions appear in the kinematic analysis of mechanisms with undulating guides and in the generation of certain non-circular gear and cam profiles.2 In engineering they are related to trochoidal rotary pumps and engines, where the rolling or sliding contact between a rotor and housing generates epitrochoidal or hypotrochoidal paths.3 The version implemented here starts the rolling at a peak of the sine wave and employs a scaling of the input parameters so that the visual rolling rate (revolutions per base period) matches common published illustrations that advance the rotation angle with the parameter rather than true arc length.

When the user supplies values \(a\) and \(d\), the code implements the geometrically faithful equations below. The final curve is reflected across the x-axis to match standard orientation.
