Cotes Spiral

A family of five spirals arising in classical orbital mechanics under inverse-cube central forces; each corresponds to a distinct solution of the differential equation of motion in polar coordinates.

Cotes’ Spiral polar equation 1:
\[ \frac{1}{r} = a \cdot \cosh(k\theta + \epsilon) \]
Cotes’ Spiral polar equation 2:
\[ \frac{1}{r} = a \cdot \cos(k\theta + \epsilon) \]
Cotes’ Spiral polar equation 3:
\[ \frac{1}{r} = a \cdot (k\theta + \epsilon) \]
Cotes’ Spiral polar equation 4:
\[ \frac{1}{r} = a \cdot e^{k\theta + \epsilon} \]
Cotes’ Spiral polar equation 5:
\[ \frac{1}{r} = a \cdot \sinh(k\theta + \epsilon) \]

History and Applications

Roger Cotes (1682–1716) was an English mathematician and the first Plumian Professor of Astronomy and Experimental Philosophy at Trinity College, Cambridge, becoming a fellow and professor by age 23. Cotes collaborated closely with Isaac Newton, editing the second edition of the Principia (1713) over three-and-a-half years of detailed correspondence; he recomputed solar and planetary tables, refined lunar theory sections, and wrote a preface defending Newton's experimental method against Cartesian vortices. Newton later remarked on Cotes' death from a violent fever at age 33: "If he had lived we might have known something." Cotes' only lifetime publication, Logometria (1714), advanced logarithms and rectified the logarithmic spiral; his broader work appeared posthumously in Harmonia Mensurarum (1722), edited by his cousin Robert Smith.1,2 At the time, orbital theory centered on Newton’s inverse-square law for gravity producing conic sections (ellipses, parabolas, hyperbolas). Cotes extended this to the inverse-cube force law \( F(r) ∝ \frac {1}{r^3} \), deriving the family of trajectories now called Cotes’ spirals. These describe particle paths under attractive and repulsive forces, starting from a given position with given speed and direction. The five polar cases he identified emerge from solving the differential equation of motion in polar coordinates, with the parameter \( k \) depending on the ratio of force strength to angular momentum squared. Cases 1 and 5 are Poinsot’s spirals, Case 2 the epispiral, Case 3 the hyperbolic spiral, and Case 4 the equiangular (logarithmic) spiral (see separate entries for details and code).3,4 \( a \) governs size, \( k \) governs shape, and \( ε \) governs rotation for all cases.

Real-life applications in engineering and the natural sciences are confined to the theoretical framework of classical mechanics. Cotes’ spirals model orbits under inverse-cube central forces, a case Newton discussed but did not fully resolve; they appear in textbooks on analytical dynamics (e.g., Whittaker 1944) for illustrating non-closed trajectories that spiral into or away from the center.
