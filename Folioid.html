Folioid

A polar curve that generalizes the rose curves through an eccentricity parameter e.

Folioid polar equation:
\[ r = a\bigl(e\cdot\cos(n\theta)\pm\sqrt{1-e^{2}\sin^{2}(n\theta)}\bigr) \]
Special case e = 1 (rose):
\[ r = 2a\cos(n\theta) \]
When e > 1:
\[ n = \dfrac{p}{q}\quad\text{(integers }p,q\text{ in lowest terms)} \]

History and Applications

The folioid curve was first studied by the Dutch mathematician P. van Geer in 1918 and subsequently discussed by Gino Loria.1 This polar curve forms a versatile family that generalizes the rose curves through an eccentricity parameter e. When e = 1, the folioid reduces directly to a rose curve. For e < 1 the lobes are less indented, while e > 1 produces multiple separate closed loops, the number depending on whether n = p/q has odd or even numerator and denominator. The “Extended” mode treats the square root term by a signed real continuation, converting negative radicands into reflected real values; this causes the otherwise imaginary branches to appear as additional real petals, yielding fully closed rosette forms for artistic purposes. The total angular span required for full closure in Extended mode is governed by θclose ≈ (2q2/p)·(π/arcsin(1/e)), where θclose is measured in multiples of π. With the default sampling of 180 segments per π, the predicted segment count is segments ≈ 180·θclose/π.

In the natural sciences, folioid-shaped projections have been employed in the study of phyllotaxis, the arrangement of leaves, petals, and florets on plant stems. Botanists model the shoot apical meristem as a cone and project primordia onto the transverse plane, yielding folioid outlines that help explain self-organizing spiral patterns observed in plants.2 No applications in engineering were identified in available sources.
