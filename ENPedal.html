Ellipse Negative Pedal Curve

The envelope of lines perpendicular to the radii from a fixed pedal point to the points of tangency on an ellipse.

Ellipse Negative Pedal Curve parametric equations:
\[ x=\dfrac{\bigl((a^{2}-b^{2})\sin^{2}t+a^{2}+d^{2}\bigr)\cos t-2ad}{a-d\cos t},\quad y=\dfrac{\bigl((b^{2}-a^{2})\cos t\,(a\cos t-2d)+a(b^{2}-d^{2})\bigr)\sin t}{b(a-d\cos t)} \]

History and Applications

The negative pedal curve of an ellipse is the envelope of lines perpendicular to the line connecting a fixed pedal point to points of tangency on the ellipse. When the pedal point is the center of the ellipse, the resulting curve is known as Talbot’s curve. It was studied by Roche and Talbot in 1821 and later by Tortolini in 1846.1 When the pedal point is a focus of the ellipse, the resulting family of curves was examined by Lockwood in 1957.2 The general theory of negative pedal curves of conics was developed by Ameseder in 1879.3 For the ellipse, the negative pedal curve (with respect to its center, or occasionally another point such as a focus) produces shapes closely connected to Lamé curves and astroid-like forms. In fact, if the pedal point is the ellipse’s center, the negative pedal is a Lamé curve of order 4, sometimes called a superellipse. When \(d = 0\), the pedal point is at the center. When \(d = c\) (where \( c = \sqrt{√a^2 − b^2} \) ), the pedal point is at a focus. When \(d = a\), the pedal point is at the end of the major axis and the resulting curve is a deltoid. The code allows \(b > a\) for experimentation, but in such a case the resulting curve is no longer a true ellipse negative pedal curve.

No real-life applications in engineering or the natural sciences are documented for the ellipse negative pedal curve in the standard references consulted.
