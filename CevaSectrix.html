Ceva Sectrix

A family of polar curves generalizing the classical trisectrix of Ceva (n = 1), enabling precise geometric division of an angle into 2n + 1 equal parts.

Ceva Trisectrix Cartesian equation:
\[ (x^{2} + y^{2})^{3} = \left( (b + 1) x^{2} - (b - 1) y^{2} \right)^{2} \]
Ceva Sectrix polar equation:
\[ r = \frac{\sin \left( (2n+1)\theta \right) }{\sin(\theta)} \]
Generalized polar equation:
\[ r = a \cdot \frac{\sin^{b} \left( (2n+1)\theta \right) }{\sin^{c} (\theta)} \]
2dcurves.com polar equation:
\[ r = a \cdot (1 + b \cos(2\theta)) \]
Generalized 2dcurves.com polar equation:
\[ r = a \cdot (1 + b \cdot \cos^{c}(d \theta)) \]

History and Applications

Ceva's Sectrix1,2 is a family of polar curves originally devised by Giovanni Ceva (1647–1734) in his exploration of classical angle subdivision problems such as trisection that are impossible to solve using compass and straightedge alone. These curves provided a precise method for subdividing angles by tracing intersection points along the curve path, a valuable tool in centuries before numerical computation. Ceva, a mathematics professor at the University of Pisa, applied geometric reasoning not only to theoretical problems but also to physical applications in engineering and mechanics. In "Opus Hydrostaticum" (1728), he addressed the distribution and flow of water, offering insights into river management, irrigation systems, and public infrastructure. In his treatise "Geometria Motus", he extended his geometric thinking to the analysis of motion, developing early formulations that connected geometry with statics and equilibrium. The curve that bears his name generalizes the concept of the trisectrix into a broader class of sectrix curves, capable of dividing an angle into 2n + 1 equal parts. These curves are still of interest today for their rich polar symmetry and are useful in computer graphics and generative design. The classic Ceva trisectrix, corresponding to n = 1, is also known as the cycloid of Ceva, and appears in both polar and algebraic forms.

Real-life applications in engineering and the natural sciences are confined to geometric constructions and educational tools. The curve supports STEM activities in mathematics and technical education, where students design and build mechanical trisection devices such as hand-made wooden mechanisms with 3D-printed joints or fully 3D-printed aids based on the smaller loops for classroom demonstrations of angle division.3,4 No broader uses in physics, biology, manufacturing, or other applied sciences appear in available references.

(defun C:CEVASECTRIX (/ basept xaxisangle cd sa sb sm om os divcount cosangle sinangle x0 y0 plineents segcount baseinfo paramstep
                       divpt xspan restartflag id iq dcl opt kw mode dpr dct dpt span eq_display rval xc yc skm a1 a2 x1 y1 x2 y2 fa fb fc fd)
  (setq id "Ceva Sectrix"
        iq ""
        dcl "CEVASECTRIX"
        opt '("Classical Ceva Trisectrix" "Classical New Values" "2DCurve Bow Tie" "2DCurve Double Egg" "2DCurve Peanut" "2DCurve New Values")
        kw 0
        mode 'polar
        dpr "# of divisions around circle"
        dct 360
        dpt nil
        span 360
        kw (ca id iq dcl opt kw mode dpr dct dpt span))
  (cond
   ((eq kw 0)
    (princ "\nClassical Ceva Trisectrix: r = a·sin(3θ)/sin(θ)")
    (setq fa (get_real 2 "a (overall scale)" 1.0)
          eq_display (strcat "\na=" (rtos fa 2 4))
          rval '(if (< (abs (sin (eval a2))) 1e-6) (* fa 3.0) (* fa (/ (sin (* 3.0 (eval a2))) (avoid_zero (sin (eval a2))))))))
   ((eq kw 1)
    (princ "\nGeneralized Ceva Sectrix: r = a·[sin((2n+1)θ)]ᵇ/[sin(θ)]ᶜ")
    (setq fa (get_real 2 "a (overall scale)" 1.0)
          fb (get_real 2 "n (sectrix order: divides angles into 2n+1)" 1.0)
          fc (get_real 2 "b (numerator exponent)" 1.0)
          fd (get_real 2 "c (denominator exponent)" 1.0)
          eq_display (strcat "\na=" (rtos fa 2 4) ", n=" (rtos fb 2 4) ", b=" (rtos fc 2 4) ", c=" (rtos fd 2 4))
          rval '(if (< (abs (sin (eval a2))) 1e-6) (* fa (cond ((= fc fd) (alt_expt (+ (* 2.0 fb) 1.0) fc T)) ((> fc fd) 0.0) (T 1e6)))
                (* fa (/ (alt_expt (sin (* (+ (* 2.0 fb) 1.0) (eval a2))) fc nil) (avoid_zero (alt_expt (sin (eval a2)) fd nil)))))))
   ((eq kw 2)
    (princ "\n2DCurve Bow Tie: r = a·(1 + 2·cos(2θ))")
    (setq fa (get_real 2 "a (overall scale)" 1.0)
          eq_display (strcat "\na=" (rtos fa 2 4))
          rval '(* fa (1+ (* 2.0 (cos (* 2.0 (eval a2))))))))
   ((eq kw 3)
    (princ "\n2DCurve Double Egg: r = a·(1 + cos(2θ))")
    (setq fa (get_real 2 "a (overall scale)" 1.0)
          eq_display (strcat "\na=" (rtos fa 2 4))
          rval '(* fa (1+ (cos (* 2.0 (eval a2)))))))
   ((eq kw 4)
    (princ "\n2DCurve Peanut: r = a·(1 + 0.5·cos(2θ))")
    (setq fa (get_real 2 "a (overall scale)" 1.0)
          eq_display (strcat "\na=" (rtos fa 2 4))
          rval '(* fa (+ 1 (* 0.5 (cos (* 2.0 (eval a2))))))))
   ((eq kw 5)
    (princ "\n2DCurve New Values: r = a·(1 + b·cosᶜ(d·θ))")
    (setq fa (get_real 2 "a (overall scale)" 1.0)
          fb (get_real 2 "b (cosine coefficient)" 2.0)
          fc (get_real 2 "c (cosine exponent)" 1.0)
          fd (get_real 2 "d (angle multiple)" 2.0)
          eq_display (strcat "\na=" (rtos fa 2 4) ", b=" (rtos fb 2 4) ", c=" (rtos fc 2 4) ", d=" (rtos fd 2 4))
          rval '(* fa (+ 1 (* fb (alt_expt (cos (* fd (eval a2))) fc nil)))))))
  (setq xc '(* (eval rval) (cos (eval a2)))
        yc '(* (eval rval) (sin (eval a2)))
        skm '(T T T)
        a1 0
        a2 a1
        x1 (eval xc)
        y1 (eval yc)
        a2 '(+ a1 paramstep)
        x2 '(eval xc)
        y2 '(eval yc))
  (cc))
