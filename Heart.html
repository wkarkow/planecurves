Heart Curve

A collection of plane curves that produce a heart outline through polar, parametric, algebraic, Fourier, and mechanical-linkage constructions.

Algebraic Heart (polar):
\[ (r^{2}-a^{2})^{3}=a\cdot r^{5}\cos^{2}\theta\sin^{3}\theta \]
Boddorf Heart (polar):
\[ r=a\cdot|\tan\theta|^{|\cot\theta|} \]
Bos Heart (parametric):
\[ x=\cos t,\quad y=\sin t+\sqrt{|\cos t|} \]
Cardioid (polar):
\[ r=a(1-\sin\theta) \]
Caspar Heart (polar):
\[ r=\Bigl(-\frac{324}{\pi^{2}}\Bigr)\theta^{2}+\Bigl(\frac{396}{\pi}\Bigr)\theta-105 \]
Pierre-Daniel Heart (parametric):
\[ x=\sqrt{\frac{\cos^{3}(2(\theta-\pi/4))}{\cos^{2}(\theta-\pi/4)}},\quad y=2\sin(2(\theta-\pi/4))-\tan^{2}(\theta-\pi/4) \]
Dascanio-1 Heart (parametric):
\[ x=\sin t\cdot\cos t\cdot\ln|t|,\quad y=|t|^{0.3}\sqrt{\cos t} \]
Dascanio-2 Heart (parametric):
\[ x=\sin t\cdot\cos t\cdot\ln|t|,\quad y=\sqrt{|t|}\cdot\cos t \]
Fourier Heart (parametric):
\[ x=16\sin^{3}t,\quad y=13\cos t-5\cos 2t-2\cos 3t-\cos 4t \]
Kuriscak Heart (implicit):
\[ x^{2}+\Biggl[y-\frac{2(x^{2}+|x|-6)}{3(x^{2}+|x|+2)}\Biggr]^{2}=36 \]
Laporte Heart (parametric):
\[ x=\sin^{3}t,\quad y=\cos t-\cos^{4}t \]
Log-Modified Heart (polar):
\[ r=2-2\sin\theta+\frac{\sin\theta\cdot\sqrt{|\cos\theta|}}{\sin\theta+1.4} \]
Mangaldan Heart (parametric):
\[ x=-\sqrt{2}\sin^{3}t,\quad y=2\cos t-\cos^{2}t-\cos^{3}t \]
Samothrace Heart (polar):
\[ r=\frac{\tan(\theta/2)}{1+0.5\cos(3\theta)} \]
Sillke-1 Heart (polar):
\[ r=-1-\cos\bigl(\theta/2+\pi/4\bigr) \]
Sillke-2 Heart (polar):
\[ r=\frac{|\theta|-\pi/2}{\pi} \]
Sillke-3 Heart (polar):
\[ r=(1-|\theta|)(1+3|\theta|) \]
Sillke-4 Heart, Ellipse-heart case:
\[ x^{2}+4\bigl(y-0.5\cdot|x|\bigr)^{2}=1 \]
Sillke-4 Heart, New-values case:
\[ x^{2}+5\bigl(y-0.75\cdot|x|\bigr)^{2}=1 \]
Sillke-4 Heart, Square-root case:
\[ x^{2}+4\bigl(y-0.5\cdot|x|^{0.5}\bigr)^{2}=1 \]
Sillke-4 Heart, Schroeder case:
\[ x^{2}+4\bigl(y-0.5\cdot|x|^{2/3}\bigr)^{2}=1 \]
Ueki Heart:
\[ \begin{align*} x &= \Biggl(\cos t - \frac{c\cos t}{\sqrt{\cos^{2}t+(\sin t-a)^{2}}}\Biggr) \Biggl(1 - \frac{d}{\sqrt{\Biggl(\cos t - \frac{c\cos t}{\sqrt{\cos^{2}t+(\sin t-a)^{2}}}\Biggr)^{2} + \Biggl(\sin t - \frac{c(\sin t-a)}{\sqrt{\cos^{2}t+(\sin t-a)^{2}}}-b\Biggr)^{2}}}\Biggr) \\[1em] y &= \Biggl(\sin t - \frac{c(\sin t-a)}{\sqrt{\cos^{2}t+(\sin t-a)^{2}}}\Biggr) - \frac{d}{\sqrt{\Biggl(\cos t - \frac{c\cos t}{\sqrt{\cos^{2}t+(\sin t-a)^{2}}}\Biggr)^{2} + \Biggl(\sin t - \frac{c(\sin t-a)}{\sqrt{\cos^{2}t+(\sin t-a)^{2}}}-b\Biggr)^{2}}} \Biggl(\sin t - \frac{c(\sin t-a)}{\sqrt{\cos^{2}t+(\sin t-a)^{2}}}-b\Biggr) \end{align*} \]
Valentine Heart (parametric):
\[ x=-(1+\cos t)\sin t,\quad y=a(1+\cos t)\sin\bigl(3t/4-\pi/4\bigr) \]
Zeitler Heart (polar):
\[ r=a\cdot\sin^{7}t\cdot e^{|2t|} \]

History and Applications

The mathematical heart shape has no single inventor or canonical formula; it is a composite of several traditions—geometric, anatomical, and artistic—that converged into the modern symbol. The earliest examples of the “heart” icon appear in medieval European manuscripts of the 13th–14th centuries, where the almond- or fig-shaped motif symbolized the human soul, sincerity, or charity rather than physical anatomy. Its codification as the symbol of romantic love followed in Renaissance emblem books and playing-card design. Mathematically, however, “heart curves” arise as independent byproducts of quite different analytical investigations.

The classical cardioid, derived from rolling-circle kinematics and defined by \( r = a(1 \pm \cos\theta) \) or \( r = a(1 \pm \sin\theta) \), is the oldest truly analytical heart form. It appears in 17th-century studies of epicycloids (La Hire, de Roberval, Bernoulli) and in later optics as the caustic of a circle.1,2,3 When rotated 90° it yields the familiar heart outline. The name “cardioid” itself was introduced by de Castillon in 1741.

The so-called Fourier heart, popular in computer graphics, traces a smooth, closed curve through harmonic summation. It is effectively a truncated Fourier series chosen to approximate a symmetric heart outline; it is widely used for plotting demonstrations, Valentine’s graphics, and CNC engraving patterns.

Algebraic and polynomial hearts such as \( (x^{2} + y^{2} - 1)^{3} = x^{2} y^{3} \) (traced to Eugen Beutel, 1909)4 or Kuriscak’s rational form reemerged in the 1980s–1990s in recreational-mathematics circles, showing that a simple polynomial relation can mimic the heart contour. The algebraic heart is implemented here with a polar conversion of the equation.

Dascanio and Samothrace hearts belong to modern generative families derived from trigonometric or logarithmic modulations. Their asymmetry and spiral decay connect more to artistic rendering than to classical geometry. The Samothrace form, with a tangent-based radial modulation, has been used in animation paths and logo design for its soft pointed tip and broad upper lobe.

Torsten Sillke’s 1990s exploration on Mathematische Basteleien5 catalogued several minimal-formula hearts of increasing algebraic simplicity. Sillke-1 is a half-angle cardioid variant. Sillke-2 and -3 are normalized piecewise-linear/quadratic hearts built from \( |\theta| \) polynomials. Sillke-4 serves as a template, with the Schroeder, square-root, and ellipse-heart forms as parameter cases.

The Ueki heart is a mechanical linkage-derived form involving two Wittgenstein rods and a circular crank. The Valentine heart is a modern trigonometric composition chosen for balanced proportion when rotated upright; it intentionally reproduces the stylized “Valentine” graphic while preserving smooth curvature near the apex. Zeitler’s exponential-sine form introduces a damping-like amplitude \( e^{|2t|} \) together with \( \sin^{7} \), producing a pointed upper cusp and rapidly vanishing sides that yield a heart-flame pattern popular in mathematical art renderings.

Additional named forms present in the code (Boddorf, Bos, Caspar, Pierre-Daniel, Laporte, Log-Modified, Mangaldan) arise from the same recreational and generative sources. Applications remain concentrated in computer graphics, CNC decorative toolpaths, animation and logo design, and mathematical art. The classical cardioid retains its historical roles in epicycloid kinematics and as an optical caustic. No broader industrial engineering applications for the modern algebraic or generative hearts have been identified beyond these decorative and educational uses.
