Cardioid

A heart-shaped plane curve traced by a point fixed on the circumference of a circle rolling without slipping around a fixed circle of equal radius.

Cardioid polar equation:
\[ r = a(1 + \cos \theta) \]
Generalized parametric equations:
\[ x = \cos(a\theta + b)^c \left( d + e \cos(f\theta + g)^h \right), y = \sin(i\theta + j)^k \left( l + m \cos(n\theta + o)^p \right) \]

History and Applications

The cardioid is a heart-shaped plane curve traced by a point fixed on the circumference of a circle that rolls without slipping around the outside of a fixed circle of the same radius. Engineers, architects, and draftsmen recognize it immediately as the one-cusped epicycloid1 generated by that rolling motion. It is also a special case of the limaçon of Pascal, a broader family of curves defined by r(θ) = a + b cos θ.

Ole Rømer first examined the curve in 1674. Vaumesle studied it in 1678, and Philippe de La Hire calculated its arc length in 1708. Giovanni Francesco Melchiorre Salvemini de Castillon gave the curve its name “cardioid” (from the Greek kardia, heart) in a paper published in the Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society in 1741.2 The cardioid is the catacaustic, the envelope of reflected light rays, formed when rays originate from a point on the circumference of a reflecting circle.2 This property is accessible through basic calculus: the caustic is the envelope of the family of reflected rays, each obeying the law of reflection (angle of incidence equals angle of reflection). Its equation, r(θ) = a(1 + cos θ), is a special case of an epicycloid, and also of a limaçon of Pascal.

Real-life applications exist in engineering and the natural sciences. In acoustics, the cardioid polar pattern is the directional response used in most studio, stage, and broadcast microphones. Sound arriving from the front (0°) is captured at full sensitivity while sound from the rear (180°) is attenuated by 20–30 dB; this directivity isolates the desired source and suppresses feedback and ambient noise.3 In radar and antenna design, some directional arrays use cardioid-shaped radiation patterns to enhance directionality.3 The cardioid also arises as the boundary of the main bulb of the Mandelbrot set in complex dynamics.4 In optics the same caustic appears whenever light reflects inside a circular surface such as the inner wall of a cup of coffee or tea; the bright heart-shaped curve is the visible result of the reflected rays converging along the cardioid envelope. The nature of its generation from a rolling circle spurred interest in drawing machines like the spirograph.
