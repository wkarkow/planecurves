Parabola

Classical conic section of eccentricity 1.

Parabola Cartesian equation:
\[ y = a \cdot x^{2} \]

History and Applications

The parabola1,2,3, one of the three classical conic sections with eccentricity exactly equal to 1, was first investigated by the Greek mathematician Menaechmus (c. 380–320 BCE) in the 4th century BC. Menaechmus employed the curve in geometric constructions while attempting to solve the ancient problem of duplicating the cube (finding two mean proportionals between given lengths). Euclid later discussed the parabola in his lost treatise on conic sections. Apollonius of Perga (c. 262–190 BCE) systematized the study of conics in his eight-book work Conics and assigned the name “parabola” (from the Greek parabolê, meaning “application” or “comparison” beside a line) based on the curve’s defining properties relative to the cone. Archimedes (c. 287–212 BCE) computed the area of a parabolic segment using the method of exhaustion in The Quadrature of the Parabola, proving it equals four-thirds the area of the inscribed triangle with the same base and height. Pappus of Alexandria (c. 290–350 CE) further described the focus–directrix property. In the 17th century, analytic geometry allowed algebraic treatment of the curve. René Descartes and Pierre de Fermat advanced its study through coordinate methods, while Galileo Galilei experimentally showed that the trajectory of a projectile under uniform gravity (neglecting air resistance) traces a parabola. James Gregory and Isaac Newton examined the reflective (catacaustic) property: rays parallel to the axis reflect through the focus, and rays emanating from the focus reflect parallel to the axis.

Real-life applications of the parabola abound in engineering and the natural sciences. In optics and telecommunications, the reflective property concentrates parallel incoming rays (light, radio waves, or microwaves) to a single focal point or collimates rays from the focus into a parallel beam. This underpins parabolic reflectors in satellite dishes, radio telescopes, automobile headlights, parabolic microphones, and solar ovens. Parabolic trough collectors harness solar energy in concentrated solar power plants. In civil engineering and architecture, parabolic arches distribute compressive forces efficiently, enabling strong, material-efficient designs for bridges (such as the Garabit viaduct) and large structures; the upper cable of a suspension bridge under uniform load closely approximates a parabola. In physics, projectile motion follows a parabolic path under constant gravity, with direct use in ballistics, sports science, and space trajectory calculations. A rotating liquid in a cylindrical vessel forms a parabolic surface whose parameter depends on gravitational acceleration and angular velocity, a principle applied in liquid-mirror telescopes.

Unlike the other construction modes, fitting a parabola through four arbitrary points with unknown vertex and orientation has no closed-form solution. In this case, the curve is obtained by Gauss–Newton iteration, which minimizes the squared distance of the points from the ideal parabola in a rotated coordinate frame. The result is therefore a numerical approximation, but for well-spaced points it converges rapidly and is visually indistinguishable from an exact fit. The code for that takes up the majority of the code below.

  (defun set_nth (n val lst / i out)
    (setq i 0 out nil)
    (foreach e lst
      (setq out (cons (if (= i n) val e) out)
            i (1+ i)))
    (reverse out))
  (defun swap_nth (i j lst / vi vj)
    (setq vi (nth i lst)
          vj (nth j lst)
          lst (set_nth i vj lst)
          lst (set_nth j vi lst)) lst)
  (defun solve4x4 (m b / i j k p piv mi mj bi bj f row x sum)
    (setq m (mapcar '(lambda (r) (append r nil)) m)
          b (append b nil)
          i 0)
    (while (< i 4)
      (setq p i piv (abs (nth i (nth i m))) j (1+ i))
      (while (< j 4)
        (if (> (abs (nth i (nth j m))) piv) (setq piv (abs (nth i (nth j m))) p j))
        (setq j (1+ j)))
      (if (/= p i) (setq m (swap_nth i p m) b (swap_nth i p b)))
      (setq piv (avoid_zero (nth i (nth i m))))
      (setq j (1+ i))
      (while (< j 4)
        (setq f (/ (nth i (nth j m)) piv)
              row (nth j m)
              k i)
        (while (< k 4)
          (setq row (set_nth k (- (nth k row) (* f (nth k (nth i m)))) row)
                k (1+ k)))
        (setq m (set_nth j row m)
              b (set_nth j (- (nth j b) (* f (nth i b))) b)
              j (1+ j)))
      (setq i (1+ i)))
    (setq x '(0.0 0.0 0.0 0.0)
          i 3)
    (while (>= i 0)
      (setq sum (nth i b)
            j (1+ i))
      (while (< j 4)
        (setq sum (- sum (* (nth j (nth i m)) (nth j x)))
              j (1+ j)))
      (setq x (set_nth i (/ sum (avoid_zero (nth i (nth i m)))) x)
            i (1- i))) x)
