Whirl Curve

A nest of regular \(n\)-gons, each scaled by a constant factor \(s\) and rotated so that the vertices of one copy lie on the sides of the copy before it.

Whirl Curve equation:
\[ \theta = \dfrac{\pi}{n} - \arccos\left(\dfrac{\cos(\pi/n)}{s}\right) \]
Grow, vertex contact:
\[ s = \dfrac{1}{\cos(\pi/n)}, \quad \theta = -\dfrac{\pi}{n} \]
First circumradius (side \(a\)):
\[ R = \dfrac{a}{2\sin(\pi/n)} \]
Level \(m\), vertex \(k\), before axis rotation:
\[ r_{m} = R\, s^{m}, \quad \phi_{m,k} = m\theta + \dfrac{2\pi k}{n} \]

History and Applications

A whirl is a geometric figure constructed by nesting a sequence of regular polygons having the same number of sides \(n\), each scaled by a constant factor and rotated relative to its predecessor so that the vertices of each successive polygon lie on the sides of the preceding one.1 The vertices of the entire family therefore trace \(n\) discrete logarithmic spirals. This construction arises directly from the classic \(n\)-mice (or bugs) pursuit problem: \(n\) agents placed at the vertices of a regular \(n\)-gon simultaneously pursue one another at constant speed. Their successive positions at equal time intervals form a shrinking and rotating regular \(n\)-gon whose vertices follow logarithmic spirals and meet at the center.2 The discrete polygonal figure with scale factor \(s\) obtained by connecting these positions is the whirl. A related but distinct iterative nesting, in which each polygon is scaled so that its incircle coincides with the circumcircle of the previous copy and successive polygons are rotated by \(\pi/n\), is described separately in the literature on nested polygons.3 The continuous pursuit problem itself dates to at least the late nineteenth century. Miller posed a three-mice version in 1871; Lucas stated the three-dogs problem in 1877, which Brocard solved the same year. The problem and its logarithmic-spiral solutions were later popularized through Martin Gardner’s columns and books. Generalizations to irregular polygons and differing speeds were given by Bernhart in 1959.2

Real-life applications in engineering and the natural sciences are primarily illustrative and didactic. The underlying continuous mice problem has served as a model in pursuit-evasion theory, differential games, and certain robotic navigation or coverage algorithms (bug algorithms). Logarithmic spirals appear in contexts such as biological pursuit trajectories and some path-planning geometries, but the specific discrete polygonal whirl remains chiefly a tool for visualizing self-similar nesting and the discrete sampling of log spirals.
