Booth Curve

A bicircular quartic plane algebraic curve that morphs continuously between near-circular and lemniscate-like regimes as the ratio a/b varies; the generalized polar form replaces the classical double-angle term with nθ.

Booth Curve polar equation:
\[ r = \sqrt{\dfrac{a^{2} \cos(2\theta) + \sqrt{a^{4} \cos^{2}(2\theta) + 4 b^{4}}}{2}} \]
Generalized Booth Curve polar equation:
\[ r = \sqrt{\dfrac{a^{2} \cos(n\theta) + \sqrt{a^{4} \cos^{2}(n\theta) + 4 b^{4}}}{2}} \]

History and Applications

The Booth curve implemented here is the classical bicircular quartic version in the sense of Loria (1911)1 and Salmon2 (1869). This polar form is obtained by solving the classical bicircular quartic for r. It differs from the hippopede variant labeled “Booth oval” and “Booth lemniscate” on modern sites such as MathCurve3 and 2dcurves.com, which is covered separately under Hippopede. Long before Booth, the ancient hippopede was known to Greek geometers such as Eudoxus and Proclus as the toric intersection curve. James Booth (1810–1878), a British mathematician, revisited these curves in the 19th century, analyzing the oval and lemniscate regimes. However, Loria and Salmon applied Booth’s name instead to this distinct bicircular quartic family.1,2,3

The curve changes continuously with the ratio a/b. When a/b → 0, it tends to a circle of radius b. When a/b → ∞, it approaches the polar form of the lemniscate of Bernoulli. The generalization with parameter n replaces the classical cos(2θ) term with cos(nθ). This makes the curve a useful illustration of how a single algebraic family can interpolate between qualitatively different conic and quartic behaviors through elementary trigonometric substitution.

No real-life applications in engineering or the natural sciences were identified for this exact polar form in the sources consulted (the related hippopede variant has limited use in physics modeling of radial spin waves in magnetic materials).4 It remains primarily of interest in classical algebraic geometry and as a teaching example of bicircular quartics.

  2. Salmon, G. A Treatise on the Higher Plane Curves, 2nd ed. (1879) (bicircular quartics).

(defun C:BOOTH (/ basept xaxisangle cd sa sb sm om os divcount cosangle sinangle x0 y0 plineents segcount baseinfo paramstep
                  restartflag id iq dcl opt kw mode dpr dct dpt span eq_display xc yc skm a1 a2 x1 y1 x2 y2 rval fa fb fc)
  (setq id "Booth Curve"
        iq "r=√[(a²·cos(2θ)+√(a⁴·cos²(2θ)+4·b⁴))/2]"
        dcl nil
        opt nil
        kw nil
        mode 'polar
        dpr "# of divisions in curve"
        dct 360
        dpt nil
        span 360
        kw (ca id iq dcl opt kw mode dpr dct dpt span)
        fa (get_real 0 "a (scale)" 1.0)
        fb (get_real 0 "b (b→∞ circle, b→0, lemniscate)" 0.1)
        fc (get_real 2 "n (θ multiplier)" 2.0)
        eq_display (strcat "\na=" (rtos fa 2 4) ", b=" (rtos fb 2 4) ", n=" (rtos fc 2 4))
        rval '(if (<= (/ (+ (* fa fa (cos (* fc (eval a2)))) (alt_expt (+ (* fa fa fa fa (cos (* fc (eval a2))) (cos (* fc (eval a2))))
              (* 4.0 fb fb fb fb)) 0.5 nil)) 2.0) 1e-9) 0.0 (alt_expt (/ (+ (* fa fa (cos (* fc (eval a2)))) (alt_expt (+ (* fa fa fa fa
              (cos (* fc (eval a2))) (cos (* fc (eval a2)))) (* 4.0 fb fb fb fb)) 0.5 nil)) 2.0) 0.5 nil))
        xc '(* (eval rval) (cos (eval a2)))
        yc '(* (eval rval) (sin (eval a2)))
        skm '(T T T)
        a1 0
        a2 a1
        x1 (eval xc)
        y1 (eval yc)
        a2 '(+ a1 paramstep)
        x2 '(eval xc)
        y2 '(eval yc))
  (cc))
