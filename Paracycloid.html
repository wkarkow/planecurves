Paracycloid

A pseudocycloidal curve, paired with the hypercycloid, whose differential equations for the radius of curvature result in the use of hyperbolic functions.

Intrinsic equation (pseudocycloidal family):
\[ \dfrac{d\rho}{ds} = k\cdot\rho \]
Paracycloid parametric equations:
\[ x = a\bigl(\omega\cosh(\omega t)\cos t + \sinh(\omega t)\sin t\bigr),\quad y = a\bigl(\omega\cosh(\omega t)\sin t - \sinh(\omega t)\cos t\bigr) \]
Hypercycloid parametric equations:
\[ x = a\bigl(\omega\sinh(\omega t)\cos t + \cosh(\omega t)\sin t\bigr),\quad y = a\bigl(\omega\sinh(\omega t)\sin t - \cosh(\omega t)\cos t\bigr) \]

History and Applications

The paracycloid and hypercycloid1 (collectively known as pseudocycloidal curves) satisfy the intrinsic equation \(d\rho/ds = k\cdot\rho\) with \(k<0\) relating radius of curvature to arc length that is analogous to the relation for ordinary cycloids, which have \(k=-1\). They employ hyperbolic functions and were studied by Leonhard Euler in papers from 1750 and 1783 and later examined by Ernesto Cesàro in 1887. Mathematically, the paracycloid is the negative pedal curve of the hyperbolic sine spiral, while the hypercycloid is the negative pedal of the hyperbolic cosine spiral; the two curves are evolutes of each other.

No specific real-life applications in engineering or the natural sciences for these exact curves were identified in the primary mathematical references. The word hypercycloid is also used in some texts as a synonym for epicycloid; that usage is unrelated to the hyperbolic form implemented here.
