Braked Parabola

Trajectory of a projectile under quadratic air resistance (braked/ballistic parabola) obtained by 4th-order Runge-Kutta integration of the coupled ODEs; ideal parabola recovered exactly in the vacuum limit.

Braked Parabola differential equations (quadratic drag):
\[ \frac{dx}{dt}=v_x,\quad \frac{dy}{dt}=v_y,\quad \frac{dv_x}{dt}=-\frac{g}{V_t^2}v\cdot v_x,\quad \frac{dv_y}{dt}=-g-\frac{g}{V_t^2}v\cdot v_y,\quad v=\sqrt{v_x^2+v_y^2} \]
Vacuum (ideal) Parabola differential equations:
\[ \frac{dx}{dt}=V_0\cos\theta,\quad \frac{dy}{dt}=V_0\sin\theta-gt \]

History and Applications

The braked parabola1,2 trajectory of a projectile subject to air resistance, often called a ballistic curve, has been studied since the early development of calculus. While the idealized parabola (no drag) was understood in the 17th century through the work of Galileo Galilei3, incorporating air resistance proved far more difficult. Early mathematicians including Johann Bernoulli4, Leonhard Euler5, Adrien-Marie Legendre6, and Carl Gustav Jacob Jacobi7 analyzed projectile motion with resistive forces. In the simplest case, where drag is proportional to velocity, closed-form solutions can be derived. However, for real-world projectiles where drag is proportional to the square of velocity, the governing equations become nonlinear and must be solved numerically8. Historically, this limitation shaped practical ballistics. Before digital computation, artillery tables were produced using laborious numerical methods or mechanical analog computers9. Systems aboard warships such as the USS Iowa (BB-61) used electromechanical fire-control computers to approximate solutions of these same differential equations in real time, enabling accurate long-range targeting despite the absence of closed-form formulas10. In modern terms, the motion is governed by a coupled system of ordinary differential equations (ODEs) where drag depends on instantaneous speed. Without explicit solutions, numerical methods such as Runge–Kutta are used to generate the trajectory11 by step-by-step integration of velocity and position.

Applications of this model are widespread. It describes the flight of bullets, artillery shells, and sports projectiles such as baseballs, golf balls, and basketballs. It also appears in engineering contexts such as re-entry trajectories, debris motion, and particle transport in fluids12. Compared to the ideal parabola, the ballistic curve shows reduced range, lower peak height, and a noticeably steeper descent, especially for objects with high drag relative to their mass.
