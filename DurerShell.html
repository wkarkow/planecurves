Dürer's Shell Curve

A glissette of a point on a line segment sliding between a parabola and one of its tangents.

Dürer's Shell Curve implicit equation:
\[ 2y^{2}(x^{2}+y^{2})-2a y^{2}(x+y)+(a^{2}-3b^{2})y^{2}-b^{2}x^{2}+2ab^{2}(x+y)+b^{2}(b^{2}-a^{2})=0 \]
Dürer's Shell Curve parametric equations:
\[ x = a \dfrac{\cos\theta}{\cos\theta - \sin\theta} + b \cos\theta,\quad y = b \sin\theta \]

History and Applications

The curve known as Dürer's Shell Curve1,2,3,4 is attributed to Albrecht Dürer (1471–1528), a central figure of the Northern Renaissance who combined artistic mastery with a deep interest in geometry. In his 1525 treatise Underweysung der Messung mit dem Zirkel und Richtscheyt (Instruction in Measurement with Compass and Ruler), Dürer presented numerous geometric constructions intended for artists, craftsmen, and architects. Among these was a shell-like curve produced not from algebraic derivation, but from a mechanical drawing process involving constrained motion. The construction described in later analyses1,2 involves a line segment whose endpoints \(Q = (q,0)\) and \(R = (0,r)\) slide along the coordinate axes under the constraint \(q+r=a\). Points \(P\) and \(P\)' lying a fixed distance \(b\) from \(Q\) on the line \(QR\) are then traced as the segment moves. As \(Q\) travels from \((a,0)\) to \((0,0)\), \(R\) travels from \((0,0)\) to \((0,a)\). The locus of \(P\) and \(P'\) is Dürer’s shell curve. This places Dürer's curve within the broader class of linkage-generated curves, predating the formal study of kinematic mechanisms. Unlike the classical conchoid of Nicomedes, which is defined radially from a fixed point, Dürer's construction is linear and mechanical, reinforcing that it is not a true conchoid despite historical naming conventions. Multiple algebraic representations of the curve exist today1,2,3, reflecting different interpretations of Dürer's construction. These include implicit quartic equations and parametric forms derived from the geometry of the sliding mechanism. The parametric form used here expresses the curve in terms of an angular parameter θ, corresponding to the orientation of the moving segment, and directly models the mechanical linkage that likely inspired Dürer's original figure.

In terms of applications, Dürer's Shell Curve does not appear in modern engineering or physical modeling. Its significance lies instead in the history of geometric construction and mechanical drawing systems. It represents an early example of generating curves through constrained motion, a concept that later evolved into formal linkage theory and mechanical curve tracing in the 17th–19th centuries. Dürer's work also helped establish geometry as a practical tool in art and design, influencing architectural drafting and proportional systems for centuries.

AutoLISP Code

(defun C:DURERSHELL (/ basept xaxisangle cd sa sb sm om os divcount cosangle sinangle x0 y0 plineents segcount baseinfo paramstep
                       divpt xspan restartflag id iq dcl opt kw mode dpr dct dpt span eq_display xc yc skm a1 a2 x1 y1 x2 y2 fa fb)
  (setq id "Dürer's Shell Curve"
        iq "x=a·cos(θ)/(cos(θ)-sin(θ))+b·cos(θ),y=b·sin(θ)"
        dcl nil
        opt nil
        kw nil
        mode 'polar
        dpr "# of divisions in curve"
        dct 720
        dpt nil
        span 360
        kw (ca id iq dcl opt kw mode dpr dct dpt span)
        fa (get_real 2 "a (x+y segment length)" 1.0)
        fb (get_real 2 "b (fixed distance from Q)" 1.0)
        eq_display (strcat "\na=" (rtos fa 2 4) ", b=" (rtos fb 2 4))
        xc '(+ (* fa (/ (cos (eval a2)) (avoid_zero (- (cos (eval a2)) (sin (eval a2)))))) (* fb (cos (eval a2))))
        yc '(* fb (sin (eval a2)))
        skm '(T T T)
        a1 0
        a2 a1
        x1 (eval xc)
        y1 (eval yc)
        a2 '(+ a1 paramstep)
        x2 '(eval xc)
        y2 '(eval yc))
  (cc))
(C:DURERSHELL)
