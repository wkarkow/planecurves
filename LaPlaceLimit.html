Laplace Limit

A parametric plane curve that visualizes the domain of convergence of the power-series solution to Kepler’s equation in celestial mechanics.

Laplace Limit parametric equations:
\[ x = \sqrt{t \bigl(\coth t - t\bigr)},\quad y = \sqrt{t \bigl(t - \tanh t\bigr)} \]
Domain:
\[ 0 \le t \le \mu \approx 1.19967864 \quad ( \coth\mu = \mu ) \]

History and Applications

The Laplace limit curve1,2 is a parametric plane curve tied directly to the convergence analysis of power series solutions for Kepler’s equation in celestial mechanics. Pierre-Simon Laplace (1749–1827), the French mathematician and astronomer, examined the power series expansion of the eccentric anomaly E in terms of the eccentricity ε in Kepler’s equation M = E − ε sin(E), where M is the mean anomaly. He identified the critical eccentricity value beyond which the series diverges for mean anomalies other than multiples of π. Earlier work by Italian astronomer Francesco Carlini (circa 1820) gave an approximate value near 0.66, while Augustin-Louis Cauchy refined it to approximately 0.66274 in 1829. The limit, e = 0.662743419\ldots, now known as the Laplace limit, equals the maximum value of the function x / cosh(x) for real x > 0 and corresponds to the unique positive real solution μ ≈ 1.19967864 of the transcendental equation derived from setting the derivative of that function to zero, which is equivalent to a fixed-point relation involving coth(μ) = μ. The parametric equations x(t) = √[t (coth(t) − t)], y(t) = √[t (t − tanh(t))] for t from 0 to μ trace the curve up to this special parameter value, where boundary clamping ensures the plot reaches the origin without gaps. This visualization connects the hyperbolic functions in the limit derivation to a plotted path in the plane.

Real-life applications exist in astronomy and orbital mechanics. The Laplace limit determines the range of eccentricities for which series methods reliably compute planetary, comet, and satellite positions without divergence, supporting ephemeris generation and trajectory predictions. Space agencies apply it when validating analytical orbit solutions before switching to numerical iteration for higher-eccentricity cases, as in solar system dynamics and spacecraft navigation.
