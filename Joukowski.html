Joukowski Airfoil

A classical analytic airfoil generated by conformal mapping of a circle in the complex plane.

Joukowski parametric equations:
\[x = \dfrac{a}{4}\,(b + \sqrt{(1 - b)^{2} + c^{2}}\cos\theta)\left(1 + \dfrac{1}{(b + \sqrt{(1 - b)^{2} + c^{2}}\cos\theta)^{2} + (c + \sqrt{(1 - b)^{2} + c^{2}}\sin\theta)^{2}}\right) \\ y = \dfrac{a}{4}\,(c + \sqrt{(1 - b)^{2} + c^{2}}\sin\theta)\left(1 - \dfrac{1}{(b + \sqrt{(1 - b)^{2} + c^{2}}\cos\theta)^{2} + (c + \sqrt{(1 - b)^{2} + c^{2}}\sin\theta)^{2}}\right) \]
WK-27 upper body curve (polar / parametric):
\[ r = e^{a\theta},\qquad x = e^{a\theta}\cos\theta,\qquad y = e^{a\theta}\sin\theta \]

History and Applications

The Joukowski airfoil1,2 (also spelled Zhukovsky) is one of the earliest mathematically defined airfoil families and represents a foundational milestone in classical aerodynamics. Introduced by the Russian mathematician Nikolai Yegorovich Joukowski in the early 1900s, the method maps a simple circle in the complex plane into a cambered, airfoil-shaped curve using the transformation \(z = \zeta + 1/\zeta\).3 Although the mathematics can be presented in highly theoretical terms, the engineering motivation was straightforward: to create an airfoil with a predictable lift coefficient derived from circulation theory. The mapping accomplishes three key geometric and aerodynamic effects; thickness and camber arise naturally from the offset of the generating circle, a finite leading-edge radius is produced (avoiding the mathematical cusp seen in earlier theoretical airfoils), and the resulting airfoil has a pressure distribution that aligns with the Kutta–Joukowski theorem4 for lift, providing a closed-form relationship between circulation and lift. Because the Joukowski airfoil is defined entirely by geometry in the complex plane, it offered something revolutionary for its time: a fully analytic airfoil whose lift characteristics could be computed without wind tunnel tests. This was decades before NACA developed large experimental catalogs, and at a time when empirical shaping and trial-and-error carving were still common.

Practically, the method influenced many researchers in the 1910–1930 period. Although the resulting shapes did not become standard production airfoils, largely because the Joukowski mapping ties thickness and camber together, making it hard to optimize each independently, the mathematical approach introduced several important ideas: camber as a circulatory effect, not just a geometric choice; leading-edge radius control; smooth pressure-recovery capability; and airfoil families generated systematically rather than by hand. These concepts laid the groundwork for later analytic and semi-analytic airfoil families, including the Göttingen, RAF, Clark-Y, and eventually the well-known NACA 4-digit and 5-digit series.1,3,4

Today, Joukowski airfoils serve more as instructional and theoretical tools than as practical production airfoils. They are extensively used in aerodynamics education to illustrate circulation and conformal mapping, computational fluid dynamics validation, research into inverse design, historical studies of early airfoil development, and occasionally low-speed experimental projects, where their simple analytic form is convenient. Despite their limited use in modern aircraft, the Joukowski airfoil’s importance lies in its legacy. It was the first example of a mathematically generated airfoil whose aerodynamic behavior could be derived from theory rather than experiment. Many later methods—analytic camber lines, thickness distributions, conformal mapping techniques, and geometric constructions using circles—trace their intellectual ancestry back to Joukowski.

The WK-27 airfoil originated as an independent design effort by the author aimed at maximizing lift-to-drag ratio for small model aircraft operating at very low Reynolds numbers, where viscous effects dominate and conventional NACA airfoils underperform. The concept began with an observation that early home-built airfoils carved from balsa blocks and sanded by eye often flew surprisingly well because the curvature of their upper surfaces tended to decay smoothly from the nose toward the tail. Rather than approximating a hand-drawn curve or repurposing a classical NACA thickness distribution, the WK-27 instead pursued a mathematically precise way to achieve that same effect. The inspiration came partly from reading about Joukowski’s circular-flow theory, where a simple circle under conformal mapping produces an airfoil with camber and circulation. The intent was not to reproduce Joukowski’s analytic transformation, but to adopt the underlying geometric intuition: a circle as a generative object that controls camber and curvature. From that starting point, the WK-27 took a different path.

The upper surface was defined as a pure logarithmic spiral, with \(r(\theta)=e^{a\theta}\) and \(a=-4.9\). Logarithmic spirals are mathematically attractive because their curvature decreases monotonically with increasing angle. This produces the kind of gentle pressure recovery that delays separation and reduces drag, a critical advantage at low Reynolds numbers. The chosen exponent was determined empirically by fitting a full-length spiral to CAD geometry and later validated by aerodynamic analysis using XFOIL.

To avoid a knife-edge nose tip and to achieve a predictable stagnation region, the spiral was blended tangentially into a leading-edge circle of selected radius to make it amenable to manufacture. The camber construction is where WK-27 diverges most clearly from classical methods. Instead of prescribing a polynomial camber line (as in NACA 4- and 5-digit families) or relying on implicit camber generated by conformal mapping, the WK-27 uses a camber circle, a geometric circle to be used as a curved mirror: the radial distance from the camber circle to the upper surface equals the radial distance to the lower surface along the same ray. The camber circle intersects the tail end, the center of the nose circle, and has a center determined by rotating a normal line drawn from the tail to create a tail wedge angle. There are therefore three variables in this construction: the value of the exponent \(a\), the nose circle radius chosen, and the tail wedge angle. Although the individual components—logarithmic spirals, nose circles, and camber-line geometry—have all appeared in aerodynamic design literature, the combination used in WK-27 does not appear in published airfoil families.

When analyzed in XFOIL across a range of low Reynolds numbers, WK-27 demonstrated lift-to-drag ratios peaking at 42.05 at an angle of attack of 5.7°, superior to many entries in the UIUC Airfoil Database, which has long served as a benchmark repository for subscale aircraft performance. Particularly at modest angles of attack where laminar behavior dominates, the logarithmic spiral upper surface produced unusually gentle pressure recovery that delayed separation and reduced drag. The WK-27 is suited for lightweight model aircraft, hand-launched gliders, and UAVs operating in the low-Re regime (Re < 200,000). It represents an example of a designer-invented airfoil whose geometry is fully specified by fundamental mathematical curves (logarithmic spirals and circles), rather than by traditional empirical coefficients. Although inspired in part by the historical ideas of Joukowski, the WK-27 is a distinct, original airfoil family with its own geometric logic and aerodynamic merits.

WK-27 Construction steps:

  1. Using the Joukowski Curve WK-27 option, pick a negative exponential value for \(a\) (default =-4.9).
  2. Draw the upper airfoil exponential curve to 360 degrees.
  3. Choose a nose radius ~0.01 (unscaled units), selected according to material strength and intended final chord size.
  4. Offset the upper exponential curve downward by this radius, forming the “offset curve” that defines the nose-circle tangent point.
  5. Draw a pline “A” from the tail end of the upper airfoil curve to the tail end of its offset and then choose the “L” for length option to extend that pline a considerable length, somewhere along which the camber circle center will lie.
  6. Rotate that tail pline counterclockwise from its tail end as the rotation center by half the intended tail wedge angle, such as 1 degree in order that the camber circle will no longer be tangent at the tail so that later reflection on the circle will result in a narrow angle between upper and lower airfoil surfaces and not an impossibly thin tail.
  7. Draw a line “B” from tail end of exponential airfoil curve to nose end of its offset.
  8. Draw a line “C” perpendicular to “B”, and move “C” to “B”s midpoint.
  9. Mirror tail pline “A” using “C” as the mirror line to create a nose pline “D” that intersects the nose end of the offset.
  10. Draw the camber circle using the intersection of plines “A” and “D” as its center, and the tail end of the exponential airfoil curve as its radius. The camber circle should also pass through the nose end of the offset.
  11. Draw a temporary nose circle centered on the nose end of the offset.
  12. Draw plines from the center of the camber circle to each vertex on the exponential curve, starting at the tail and working forward a few vertices past where the exponential curve crosses inside the nose circle. This is the tedious part...
  13. Using the camber circle as the cutting edge, trim all the radial plines so that only the segments from the camber circle to the upper airfoil surface remain.
  14. Copy all the segments into the same location with no displacement.
  15. Move each duplicated upper segment’s top end to its bottom, constructing a series of vertices for the lower airfoil.
  16. Draw the lower airfoil pline, connecting all the bottom vertices from nose circle to tail, then use Pedit to “Fit curve”.
  17. Offset the lower airfoil pline the same distance as the nose radius, above the lower airfoil surface.
  18. Erase the temporary nose circle and redraw a nose circle centered on the nose-end intersections between upper and lower offsets with the same chosen nose radius.
  19. Trim nose circle using the upper and lower airfoil curves so that only the forward nose arc remains.
  20. Trim the upper and lower airfoils using that same arc so both surfaces meet the nose arc tangentially.
  21. Use Pedit→Join to merge the upper and lower airfoils and nose arc into one closed curve.
  22. To standardize the airfoil’s rotation, rotate it with tail end as center point and reference angle from tail to intersection of camber circle and nose arc to a new angle of 180 degrees.
  23. To standardize the airfoil’s scale, scale it with tail end as base point and reference length from tail end to front nose quadrant to a new length of 1.0.
  24. Finally, remove any construction lines, including the offsets, camber circle, and camber width segments, as desired.

(defun C:JOUKOWSKI (/ basept xaxisangle cd sa sb sm om os divcount cosangle sinangle x0 y0 plineents segcount baseinfo paramstep
                      divpt restartflag id iq dcl opt kw mode dpr dct dpt span eq_display xc yc skm a1 a2 x1 y1 x2 y2 fa fb fc fd)
  (setq id "Joukowski Airfoil"
        iq ""
        dcl "JOUKOWSKI"
        opt '("Canonical" "WK-27 upper body curve")
        kw 0
        mode 'polar
        dpr "# of divisions around circle"
        dct 360
        dpt nil
        span 360
        kw (ca id iq dcl opt kw mode dpr dct dpt span))
  (cond
   ((eq kw 0)
    (princ "\nx=a·(b+d·cosθ)·(1+1/((b+d·cosθ)²+(c+d·sinθ)²)), \ny=a·(c+d·sinθ)·(1-1/((b+d·cosθ)²+(c+d·sinθ)²)))")
    (setq fa (get_real 2 "a  (approximate chord length)" 1.0)
          fb (get_real 2 "b (chord thickness factor)" 0.1)
          fc (get_real 0 "c (camber factor)" 0.1)
          fd (sqrt (+ (expt (- 1.0 fb) 2.0) (expt fc 2.0)))
          eq_display (strcat "a=" (rtos fa 2 4) ", b=" (rtos fb 2 4) ", c=" (rtos fc 2 4))
          xc '(* 0.25 fa (* (+ fb (* fd (cos (eval a2)))) (+ 1.0 (/ 1.0 (avoid_zero (+ (expt (+ fb (* fd (cos (eval a2)))) 2) (expt (+ fc (* fd (sin (eval a2)))) 2)))))))
          yc '(* 0.25 fa (* (+ fc (* fd (sin (eval a2)))) (- 1.0 (/ 1.0 (avoid_zero (+ (expt (+ fb (* fd (cos (eval a2)))) 2) (expt (+ fc (* fd (sin (eval a2)))) 2)))))))))
   ((eq kw 1)
    (princ "\nWK-27 upper airfoil curve: r(θ) = e^aθ")
    (setq fa (get_real 2 "a (exponent, -6 is flatter, -3 more rounded)" -4.9)
          eq_display (strcat "\na=" (rtos fa 2 4))
          xc '(* (exp (* fa (eval a2))) (cos (eval a2)))
          yc '(* (exp (* fa (eval a2))) (sin (eval a2))))))
  (setq skm '(T T T)
        a1 0
        a2 a1
        x1 (eval xc)
        y1 (eval yc)
        a2 '(+ a1 paramstep)
        x2 '(eval xc)
        y2 '(eval yc))
  (cc))
