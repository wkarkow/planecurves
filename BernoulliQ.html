Bernoullian Quartic

Locus of midpoints between two concentric circles of inner (a) and outer (b) radii whose enclosed area equals that of the inner circle, πa².

Bernoullian Quartic implicit equation:
\[ x^{2} y^{2} = \left( \left( \frac{a + b}{2} \right)^{2} - y^{2} \right) \left( y^{2} - \left( \frac{a - b}{2} \right)^{2} \right) \]
Bernoullian Quartic parametric equations:
\[ x = \frac{ a \cos(\theta) + \sqrt{ b^{2} - a^{2} \sin^{2}(\theta) } }{2} , \quad y = a \sin(\theta) \]

History and Applications

The Bernoullian quartic1 was studied by Jakob Bernoulli in 1687 and by Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz in 16961. Jakob Bernoulli (1654–1705) was a Swiss mathematician from the prominent Bernoulli family in Basel; he contributed foundational ideas to the early development of calculus, geometry, and the calculus of variations1.

This algebraic curve of degree 4 is the polizonal median (locus of midpoints) between two concentric circles of radii a (inner) and b (outer, with b ≥ a). A useful property for drafters and engineers is that the total area enclosed by the curve equals the area of the smaller circle, πa². The curve can be generated mechanically with a slider-crank linkage by tracing the path of a point attached to the connecting rod when the geometry produces the concentric circular paths1.

Applications in engineering and the natural sciences are limited but directly relevant to mechanism design. The primary documented use is in kinematics of slider-crank mechanisms, the core linkage found in steam engines, internal-combustion engines, reciprocating pumps, and compressors2, as well as drawing machines and pantographs, especially in the 18th–19th centuries. These convert rotary motion to linear (or vice versa) and trace the curve as a coupler path2. No widespread modern uses in structures, fluid flow, or optics were identified beyond this classical mechanical context.

Issues that must be addressed include stress accumulation at points of high curvature (particularly at the "pinch" near the origin), inertial wear due to alternating accelerations at the lobes of the curve, and torque irregularities, since the mechanical advantage of the crank varies nonlinearly along the path. These challenges can lead to material fatigue, making such linkages less preferred in high-speed industrial systems.

        kw nil

        dpr "# of divisions in curve"

        skm '(nil T T)
        a1 0
        a2 a1
        x1 (eval xc)
        y1 (eval yc)
        a2 '(+ a1 paramstep)
        x2 '(eval xc)
        y2 '(eval yc))
