L'Hospital Quintic

The algebraic quintic (k=1) of the family of curves of constant reaction force under gravity.

General Cartesian parametrization:
\[ x=2a\int_{0}^{\phi}\frac{\cos\phi_{1}}{(k+\cos\phi_{1})^{3}}\,d\phi_{1},\quad y=\frac{a}{(k+\cos\phi)^{2}} \]
k < 1 solution:
\[ x=-a\frac{\sin\phi\,(2k^{3}+k+(k^{2}+2)\cos\phi)}{(1-k^{2})^{2}(k+\cos\phi)^{2}} -\frac{6ak}{(1-k^{2})^{2}\sqrt{1-k^{2}}}\cdot\frac12\ln\Bigl(\frac{1+\tan(\phi/2)\sqrt{(1-k)/(1+k)}}{1-\tan(\phi/2)\sqrt{(1-k)/(1+k)}}\Bigr), \quad y=\frac{a}{(k+\cos\phi)^{2}} \]
k = 1 (algebraic quintic):
\[ x=\frac{a}{2}\Bigl(\tan(\phi/2)-\frac{\tan(\phi/2)^{5}}{5}\Bigr),\quad y=\frac{a}{4}(1+\tan(\phi/2)^{2})^{2} \]
k > 1 solution:
\[ x=-a\frac{\sin\phi\,(2k^{3}+k+(k^{2}+2)\cos\phi)}{(k^{2}-1)^{2}(k+\cos\phi)^{2}} -\frac{6ak}{(k^{2}-1)^{2}\sqrt{k^{2}-1}}\,\Theta(\phi), \quad y=\frac{a}{(k+\cos\phi)^{2}} \]

History and Applications

The L’Hospital quintic1,2, named after Guillaume François Antoine de l’Hôpital (1661–1704), a French mathematician who wrote the first printed textbook on differential calculus, is the special algebraic case (k=1) of a family of curves with constant reaction force. The underlying problem, called the “curva aequalis pressionis” (curve of constant pressure), was posed by Johann Bernoulli in 1695. L’Hôpital published the complete solution in 1700. The curve is shaped so that a particle descending along it under uniform gravity experiences a constant normal reaction force from the supporting surface (or the particle exerts constant force on the curve). This follows directly from Newton’s second law projected onto the normal direction together with conservation of mechanical energy: the speed squared is proportional to the vertical drop, and the normal acceleration component must balance gravity to keep the reaction magnitude fixed. The general family depends on the dimensionless ratio k = reaction/weight. For k = 1 the curve becomes a polynomial quintic of degree five; for k < 1 and k > 1 it is transcendental and solved with inverse hyperbolic or trigonometric functions.

For the \(k>1\) regime the inverse-tangent term \(\Theta(\phi)\) must be kept continuous so that the \(x\)-coordinate does not jump. The ordinary arctangent experiences a discontinuity of \(\pi\) each time the argument passes through a singularity (when \(\sin\phi\approx0\) and \(1+\cos\phi\approx0\), i.e., at the odd multiples of \(\pi\)). To produce a steadily advancing \(x\) the expression therefore “marches” by adding the appropriate multiple of \(\pi\): \(\Theta(\phi)=\dfrac{\pi}{2}+\pi\left\lfloor\dfrac{\phi}{2\pi}\right\rfloor\) when the singularity condition holds, otherwise \(\Theta(\phi)=\operatorname{atan2}\!\Bigl(\sqrt{\dfrac{k-1}{k+1}}\,\sin\phi,\;1+\cos\phi\Bigr)+\pi\left\lfloor\dfrac{\phi}{2\pi}\right\rfloor+1_{\{\phi\bmod 2\pi>\pi\}}\). This continuous “marching” form is the one implemented in the AutoLISP code.

Real-life applications are found in classical mechanics and the natural sciences. The curve itself and its evolute illustrate exact solutions to constrained motion under gravity with constant force or tension, concepts used in theoretical treatments of particle trajectories, pendulum dynamics, and early studies of mechanical constraints. No additional modern engineering or scientific applications beyond these foundational mechanical illustrations are documented in primary sources. While no real roller coaster follows this curve exactly (due to friction, structural limits, and lateral stability requirements), the underlying principle remains relevant in the conceptual design of motion paths with controlled load factors. Of note, the L’Hospital Quintic curve has constant force for only one value of speed. At the vertex, \( V = \frac{1}{k}\sqrt{2ga} \), so that speed is inversely proportional to \(k\) and proportional to the square root of \(a\).
