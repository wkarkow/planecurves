Cassinian Oval

A quartic algebraic curve; locus of points P such that the product of distances from P to two fixed foci is constant.

Cassinian Oval implicit equation:
\[ [(x - a)^{2} + y^{2}][(x + a)^{2} + y^{2}] = b^{4} \]
Cassinian Oval polar equation:
\[ r^{4} - 2 a^{2} r^{2} \cos(2\theta) = b^{4} - a^{4} \]
Multiple θ polar equation:
\[ r^{4} - 2 a^{2} r^{2} \cos(c\theta) = b^{4} - a^{4} \]

History and Applications

The Cassinian oval¹,², also known as the Cassini oval, was first investigated by the Italian-French astronomer Giovanni Domenico Cassini (1625–1712) in 1680. While studying the relative motions of the Earth and the Sun, Cassini proposed that the Sun traveled around the Earth along one of these ovals, with the Earth located at one focus. This model appeared in his son Jean Cassini’s 1740 work Éléments d’astronomie. Although ultimately superseded by Keplerian ellipses, the curve remains an important quartic plane curve. The Cassinian oval is the locus of points P such that the product of the distances from P to two fixed foci is constant.

Real-life applications include radar and sonar engineering, where the constant signal-to-noise ratio contours and detection zones of bistatic and multistatic systems are described by Cassini ovals³. In biophysics, Cassini ovals provide a useful mathematical approximation for the axisymmetric meridional profile of human red blood cells⁴, enabling calculations of volume, surface area, and elastic energy. They also appear as equipotential lines in certain electrostatic and magnetostatic configurations involving two parallel wires.

Depending on the ratio b/a, the shape of the curve can vary significantly:

  • If b < a, the curve forms two disjoint loops.
  • If b = a, the curve becomes the Lemniscate of Bernoulli, a special figure-eight shape.
  • If b > a, the curve is a single, connected, peanut- or oval-shaped loop.
  • If (2θ) is replaced by (cθ) and b > a, one has the option of creating a single loop with multiple foci.
