Airy Disk

The diffraction pattern of light through a circular aperture.

Airy Disk Cartesian equation:
\[ y = y_0 \cdot \frac{4 \left( \operatorname{J}_1 (a x) \right)^2 }{(a x)^2} \]
Approximation used:
\[ \operatorname{J}_1(z) \approx 1.02361001539456 \cdot \left( \frac{0.1601 z^2 + 0.8660 \cdot \sin(z)}{(1 + 0.3489 z^2)(1 + 0.4181 z^2)^{0.25}} - \frac{z \left(0.1007 z^2 + 0.3718\right) \cdot \cos(z)}{(1 + 0.4181 z^2)^{0.75} (1 + 0.3489 z^2)} \right)^2 \]

History and Applications

The Airy disk1,2, named after George Biddell Airy (1801–1892), describes the diffraction pattern of light through a circular aperture. Airy, an English mathematician and astronomer who served as Astronomer Royal from 1835 to 1881, provided the first mathematical treatment in his 1835 paper "On the Diffraction of an Object-glass with Circular Aperture," building on John Herschel's 1828 empirical observations. Airy's work advanced optics by explaining resolution limits beyond simple ray tracing, using integrals to model wave interference—concepts accessible via basic calculus as the summation of light waves adding constructively or destructively.

The intensity distribution is \( I(\rho) = I_0 \left( \frac{2 \operatorname{J}_1 (k a \rho / f)}{k a \rho / f} \right)^2 \), where \( \operatorname{J}_1 \) is the first-order Bessel function of the first kind, \( k = 2\pi / \lambda \) (wavenumber), \( a \) is aperture radius, \( \rho \) is radial distance in the image plane, \( f \) is focal length, and \( \lambda \) is wavelength. This simplifies to the first equation in the equation ribbon above in normalized form, with the central bright spot surrounded by concentric rings. The first dark ring occurs where the intensity drops to zero, giving a disk radius of about \( 1.22 \lambda f / D \) (where \( D \) is aperture diameter). The code employs the approximation shown in the ribbon, accurate to within 0.01 over relevant domains for efficient plotting without external libraries3.

Real-life applications abound in engineering and natural sciences. In optics, it defines resolution limits for telescopes, microscopes, and cameras, per the Rayleigh criterion: minimum angular separation \( \theta \approx 1.22 \lambda / D \) (\( D \) = aperture diameter). This governs telescope design for astronomy, microscope objectives for biology (e.g., cell imaging), and camera lenses for photography, where diffraction blurs details at small apertures. In laser engineering, it models focused beam spots for manufacturing (e.g., cutting or welding) and beam delivery systems. Confocal microscopy uses Airy units for pinhole sizing to optimize contrast in biological samples. In human vision studies, it explains acuity limits with larger pupils, aiding ophthalmic device design. Acoustics applies similar patterns for sound wave diffraction in varying media, like sonar or architectural soundproofing.
