Gompertz Curve

An asymmetric sigmoid growth (or decay) curve whose nested exponential form models mortality, population, tumor, and microbial growth more flexibly than the logistic function.

Gompertz Curve Cartesian equation:
\[ y = a \, e^{-b \, e^{-c x}} \]

History and Applications

The Gompertz curve1,2 was first presented in an 1825 paper “On the Nature of the Function Expressive of the Law of Human Mortality” by Benjamin Gompertz (1779–1865), an English self-taught mathematician and actuary elected a Fellow of the Royal Society in 1819. Gompertz analyzed empirical life tables and showed that mortality increases exponentially with age. In his 1825 paper Gompertz analyzed death records from England, France and Sweden for ages roughly 20 to 60 and observed that the force of mortality increases exponentially with age. William Makeham extended the model in 1860 by adding a constant term for age-independent mortality risks. This produced a simple analytic expression for the survival function that became fundamental to life-table construction and premium calculations in actuarial science, leading to the development of probability density (PDF) and cumulative distribution (CDF) functions. These functions remain standard tools wherever exponential age-dependent failure or survival is observed.

The Gompertz model has been used to describe constrained growth of organisms, tumors, and microbial colonies where early exponential increase gives way to logarithmic saturation. Its appeal lies in the asymmetry of the curve about its inflection point: growth is slower near the start and faster near the midpoint, unlike the symmetric logistic function. This makes it a closer empirical fit to many natural processes where limiting factors act progressively rather than abruptly. Hydrology uses the Gompertz distribution to model extreme events such as annual maximum one-day rainfalls and river discharges. Computer scientists employ it to describe failure rates of computer code. In animal growth studies, parameters a, b, and c respectively determine the mature mass, the displacement or initial condition, and the intrinsic growth rate. In oncology it has described tumor growth since the 1960s, capturing how cell proliferation slows in confined spaces with limited nutrients3, in epidemiology for disease spread, and in actuarial science for mortality and survival analysis. When a is negative, the curve models inhibition, depletion of a resource, or decline of concentration over time. A negative c reverses the direction of time dependence, widely used in reliability engineering to express the probability of survival and in pharmacology to depict drug washout or decay of efficacy. A negative b sometimes matches empirical regression where a measurement zero does not coincide with the natural starting point of a process. Because of its nested exponential structure, the Gompertz equation permits flexible use in both growth and decay domains including the Gompertz–Makeham law in actuarial science and the double-Gompertz form in agriculture and biomedicine.

(defun C:GOMPERTZ (/ basept xaxisangle cd sa sb sm om os divcount cosangle sinangle x0 y0 plineents segcount baseinfo paramstep divpt xspan
                     restartflag id iq dcl opt kw mode dpr dct dpt span eq_display xc yc skm a1 a2 x1 y1 x2 y2 maxdraw fa fb fc fd)
  (setq id "Gompertz Curve"
        iq "y=a·e⁻ᵇ·e⁽⁻ᶜˣ⁾"
        dcl nil
        opt nil
        kw nil
        mode 'param
        dpr "# of divisions to x-span of division point"
        dct 100
        dpt '(1.0 0.0)
        span nil
        kw (ca id iq dcl opt kw mode dpr dct dpt span)
        fa (get_real 2 "a (maximum value)" 1.0)
        fb (get_real 2 "b (horizontal shift)" 1.0)
        fc (get_real 2 "c (growth rate)" 1.0)
        fd (get_keyword 0 "Positive Negative" "Positive or Negative direction?" "Positive")
        eq_display (strcat "\na=" (rtos fa 2 4) ", b=" (rtos fb 2 4) ", c=" (rtos fc 2 4))
        xc '(eval a2)
        yc '(* fa (exp (- (* fb (exp (- (* fc (eval a2))))))))
        maxdraw (get_real 2 "Maximum x and y values allowed?" 50)
        paramstep (/ xspan divcount)
        paramstep (if (eq fd "Negative") (- paramstep) paramstep)
        skm '(T T T)
        a1 0
        a2 a1
        x1 (eval xc)
        y1 (eval yc)
        a2 '(+ a1 paramstep)
        x2 '(eval xc)
        y2 '(eval yc))
  (cc))
