Sturm Roulette

Locus of the center of an ellipse or hyperbola rolling without slipping on a straight line.

Elliptic case (0 ≤ e < 1):
\[ x = a(1-e^{2})\int_{0}^{t}\dfrac{du}{(1-e^{2}\sin^{2}u)^{3/2}},\quad y = a\sqrt{\dfrac{1-e^{2}}{1-e^{2}\sin^{2}t}} \]
Hyperbolic case (e > 1):
\[ x = a(e^{2}-1)\int_{0}^{t}\dfrac{\sin^{2}u\,du}{(e^{2}-\sin^{2}u)^{3/2}},\quad y = a\sqrt{\dfrac{e^{2}-1}{e^{2}-\sin^{2}t}}\,\sin t \]
where
\[ e = \dfrac{c}{a} \quad \text {is the eccentricity of the rolling conic} \].

History and Applications

The Sturm roulette is the path traced by the center of a centered conic (ellipse or hyperbola) that rolls without slipping on a fixed straight line. It is called elliptic or hyperbolic according to the type of the rolling conic. The curve was studied by Jacques Charles François Sturm in 1841.1 Sturm was a Swiss and French mathematician2, primarily known for Sturm's theorem that calculates the number of distinct real roots of a polynomial within an interval.3 With the major axis held fixed, the Sturmian roulette elliptic family passes from a straight line (rolling circle, e = 0) to a chain of semicircles (degenerate “rolling segment,” e = 1). The hyperbolic family passes from a chain of semicircles (e = 1) to a straight segment (e → ∞). The special case of a rectangular hyperbola yields the rectangular Sturm roulette, which is simultaneously a lintearia and a Ribaucour curve.1 The related Delaunay roulette is the path of a focus (rather than the center) of the same rolling conic; its parabolic member is the ordinary catenary. One classical use of the elliptic Sturm roulette is the determination of the road profile on which an elliptic wheel will roll with its center moving horizontally.1 No other independent engineering applications are recorded for the general curve.
