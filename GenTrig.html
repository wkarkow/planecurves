GenTrig Curve

A flexible parametric curve in which each Cartesian coordinate is formed as a quotient of powered, phase-shifted sine and cosine terms linked by selectable arithmetic operators.

GenTrig parametric equations:
\[ x = \dfrac{(a_1 + b_1\sin(c_1\theta + d_1))^{e_1}\;\mathrm{op}_1\;(a_2 + b_2\cos(c_2\theta + d_2))^{e_2}}{(a_3 + b_3\sin(c_3\theta + d_3))^{e_3}\;\mathrm{op}_3\;(a_4 + b_4\cos(c_4\theta + d_4))^{e_4}} \]
\[ y = \dfrac{(a_5 + b_5\sin(c_5\theta + d_5))^{e_5}\;\mathrm{op}_4\;(a_6 + b_6\cos(c_6\theta + d_6))^{e_6}}{(a_7 + b_7\sin(c_7\theta + d_7))^{e_7}\;\mathrm{op}_6\;(a_8 + b_8\cos(c_8\theta + d_8))^{e_8}} \]
Operators:
\[ \mathrm{op}_i \in \{+,-,\times,\div\} \]

History and Applications

Curves expressed parametrically as sums or ratios of trigonometric terms have their foundation in Fourier analysis. In the early 19th century Joseph Fourier demonstrated that periodic functions can be represented as infinite sums of sine and cosine terms.1 This work, extended by Peter Gustav Lejeune Dirichlet and Bernhard Riemann, produced families of plane curves in which each coordinate is built from combinations of sinusoidal functions, sometimes raised to powers or using non-integer frequencies. A prominent special case is the family of Lissajous figures studied by Jules Antoine Lissajous in the 1850s.2 Similar patterns were generated mechanically by harmonograph devices employing coupled pendulums in the late 19th and early 20th centuries.

In May 1988 Stanley S. Miller introduced the “Madness” curve, illustrated by A. K. Dewdney in Scientific American.3 This example used ratios of powered sine and cosine terms with slightly irrational frequency ratios to generate visually complex, closed figures. In 2003 Johan Gielis published the superformula, a polar generalization that produces a wide range of natural and abstract shapes from trigonometric functions.4,5

The GenTrig curve supplies a systematic parametric framework unifying these constructions. Each coordinate is defined as a quotient of powered and phase-shifted sine and cosine terms, with independent offsets, amplitudes, frequencies, phases, exponents, and selectable arithmetic operators (+, −, ×, ÷) connecting the terms, with \(op_2\) and \(op_5\) representing the changeable division lines in the equations above. This construction includes many Lissajous-type figures and “Madness”-style parametrics as special cases.

Real-life applications of trigonometric parametric curves and rational trigonometric splines occur in engineering and the natural sciences. In CAD/CAM systems they generate free-form curves and surfaces for tool-path planning and geometric modeling.6 In data visualization they preserve monotonicity for scientific datasets such as dose-response curves in pharmacology, concentration profiles in economics, and concentration profiles in ground-water studies.7
