Diverging Parabola of Newton

The family of real plane curves defined by y² = a·x³ + b·x² + c·x + d encompassing the semicubical parabola, crunodal and acnodal forms.

Diverging Parabola of Newton Cartesian equation:
\[ y^{2} = a \cdot x^{3} + b \cdot x^{2} + c \cdot x + d \]

History and Applications

The divergent parabola family consists of all real plane curves defined by a cubic polynomial on the right-hand side. Isaac Newton (1643–1727) first classified all cubic curves systematically in his 1704 appendix to Opticks, “Enumeratio linearum tertii ordinis.”1 Newton showed that every cubic can be obtained as the central projection (shadow) of one of five basic “divergent parabolas.” The equation y² = a · x³ + b · x² + c · x + d is precisely the general affine form that arises in Newton’s third class of diverging parabolas once the leading term is normalized. The four cases handled in the code—single real root, crunodal double root, acnodal double root, and the semicubical parabola (a = b = c = d = 0)—correspond exactly to the different root structures of the cubic and the resulting node, isolated point, or cusp geometries.2,3,4,5 The semicubical parabola y² = a · x³ itself was discovered earlier by William Neile in 1659 and studied by Christiaan Huygens and Isaac Newton for its cusp singularity. In the 18th and 19th centuries these curves appeared in classical treatises on algebraic geometry by Euler, Maclaurin, and Cramer.

Real-life applications exist in engineering and the natural sciences. In structural mechanics the semicubical parabola describes the exact deflection curve of a cantilever beam under a concentrated end load (Euler-Bernoulli beam theory).6 Cubic transition curves derived from this family are used in highway and railway design to provide smooth curvature change between straight and circular sections, reducing lateral jerk. In computer-aided geometric design the parametrizations of the nodal and cuspidal cases serve as building blocks for smooth splines and surface patches. The acnodal and crunodal forms appear in the study of singular points in algebraic surfaces and in certain optical caustics.
