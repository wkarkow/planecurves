Elastic Catenary

The shape taken by a flexible elastic cable of uniform cross-section hanging under its own weight between two fixed supports.

Elastic Catenary differential equations:
\[ \dfrac{dV}{ds_{0}} = w_{0},\quad \dfrac{dx}{ds_{0}} = \dfrac{H}{\sqrt{H^{2}+V^{2}}} + \dfrac{H}{EA},\quad \dfrac{dy}{ds_{0}} = \dfrac{V}{\sqrt{H^{2}+V^{2}}} + \dfrac{V}{EA} \]

History and Applications

The elastic catenary1,2 is the shape taken by a flexible, elastic cable or wire of uniform cross-section hanging under its own weight between two fixed points, allowing for stretch according to Hooke’s law. While the classical (inextensible) catenary was correctly solved mathematically in 1691 by Christiaan Huygens, Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz and Johann Bernoulli, the elastic variant that incorporates axial extensibility was studied by Finck and Bobillier in 18263.

The governing differential relations follow from equilibrium of forces and the linear elastic extension of the cable. These equations are widely used when the change in length due to tension is not negligible. Modern treatments often express the governing equations in terms of the axial stiffness EA, emphasizing that the product of elastic modulus and cross-sectional area, not either quantity alone, controls stretching.

Real-life applications occur mainly in civil, structural, and offshore engineering. The elastic catenary model is fundamental in the design and analysis of mooring lines and steel catenary risers for floating oil platforms and floating wind turbines3, where accurate prediction of sag, tension, and stretch is critical for system performance and fatigue life. Anchor chains and synthetic mooring lines have finite unstretched lengths and significant elasticity. Given a water depth and horizontal offset, engineers evaluate how much restoring force and vertical clearance result from deploying a fixed length of line. In this setting, the unstretched length and span are known, while sag and tension follow from equilibrium, precisely the scenario modeled by the “Unstretched vs Span” mode. Elastic effects are also important in overhead lines and guy wires, particularly when accounting for temperature change. As a cable warms or cools, its effective unstretched length changes, altering both sag and tension while the supports remain fixed. Sag–tension tables used in utility engineering are built on elastic catenary principles, even when simplified parabolic approximations are employed for routine calculations. In temporary rigging, lifting slings, and safety cables, the available cable length is often fixed by fabrication or certification. Engineers and technicians must determine whether a given span can be achieved safely and what tensions will develop. In such cases, sag is evaluated rather than prescribed, and elastic stretch may supply a significant portion of the required reach. The elastic catenary also appears in architectural tension structures, cable-supported roofs, and lightweight bridges, where material efficiency and visual form are both influenced by axial deformation. Conversely, in clearance-driven problems—such as ensuring minimum ground clearance under a suspended line—the desired sag may be specified first, and the required cable length and tension are then determined. This corresponds to the “Span + Sag” formulation.

The following code is based on a set of standard engineering assumptions. The cable lies in a vertical plane and is subjected only to its own weight. The gravitational load is taken as a uniform weight per unit unstretched length, denoted w0 (force per unit length). The cable is assumed to be linearly elastic in axial extension, characterized by its axial stiffness EA (force). Bending stiffness is neglected, so the cable carries only tension. The cross-section is constant along the length, so problems of “equal strength” are excluded. The configuration is quasi-static, with no dynamic effects. Under these assumptions, the elastic catenary does not admit a simple closed-form solution. Instead, the geometry follows from a coupled system of differential equations written with respect to the unstretched arc-length parameter s0. The vertical component of tension increases linearly with unstretched length, dV/ds0 = w0, while the horizontal and vertical coordinates satisfy dx/ds0 = H/√(H2+V2) + H/EA, dy/ds0 = V/√(H2+V2) + V/EA. Here H is the horizontal component of tension, which remains constant along the cable, and V(s0) is the vertical component. The additional terms involving EA account for axial stretching of the cable. Integration begins at the lowest point of the cable, where the initial conditions are x(0) = 0, y(0) = 0, V(0) = 0, with H > 0 supplied by the user or determined by an outer iteration. The code implements a stateful, step-by-step numerical integration of these equations, updating the tension components and coordinates incrementally along the cable. When geometric constraints are imposed, such as a prescribed span, a prescribed sag, or a prescribed unstretched length, auxiliary “shooting method” solvers are used to adjust the unknown parameters. Depending on the selected mode, the solver iterates on horizontal tension, unstretched length, or both, until the required end conditions are satisfied.

(defun xend (a / b c d e f g h)
  (setq c (- paramstep)
        d (* fa c)
        e 0.0
        f 0.0
        g 1
        b 0)
  (while (< b divcount)
    (setq c (+ c paramstep)
          d (+ d (* fa paramstep)))
    (if (= g 1)
      (setq g 0)
      (progn
        (setq h (alt_expt (+ (* a a) (* d d)) 0.5 nil)
              e (+ e (* paramstep (+ (/ a h) (/ a fb))))
              f (+ f (* paramstep (+ (/ d h) (/ d fb)))))))
    (setq b (1+ b)))
  (list e f))

(defun solx (a b / c d e f g h)
  (setq c 1e-6
        d (max b c)
        e (car (xend d))
        f 0)
  (while (and (< e a) (< f 50))
    (setq d (* 2.0 d)
          e (car (xend d))
          f (1+ f)))
  (setq f 0)
  (while (< f 32)
    (setq g (* 0.5 (+ c d))
          h (car (xend g)))
    (if (< h a) (setq c g) (setq d g))
    (setq f (1+ f)))
  d)
