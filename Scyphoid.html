Scyphoid

Rational quartic with a triple point at the origin; cup-shaped curve studied by Huber 1910.

Classical Cartesian equation:
\[ x^{4}-y^{4}-4axy^{2}=0 \]
Classical polar equation:
\[ r=2a\tan(2\theta)\sin\theta \]
Classical parametric equations:
\[ x=a\tan t\sin t,\quad y=a(\tan t-\sin t) \]
General parametric sin equations:
\[ x=a\tan t\sin(\dfrac{b}{c}t),\quad y=a(\tan t-\sin(\dfrac{b}{c}t)) \]
General parametric tan equations:
\[ x=a\tan(\dfrac{b}{c}t)\sin t,\quad y=a(\tan(\dfrac{b}{c}t)-\sin t) \]
General polar sin equations:
\[ r=2a\tan(2t)\sin(\dfrac{b}{c}t) \]
General polar tan equations:
\[ r=2a\tan(2\cdot\dfrac{b}{c}t)\sin t \]

History and Applications

The scyphoid1 was studied by Paul Huber in 1910. His doctoral thesis at the University of Bern, Die Scyphoide, eine Kurve 4. Ordnung, examined this rational quartic; the work is cited by Loria (p. 136). The name comes from the Greek scyphos (“cup”), referring to the curve’s overall appearance of a rounded body with extended rays. The curve is a rational quartic possessing a triple point at the origin. MathCurve supplies a geometric construction: a variable line through the fixed point \(A(-a,0)\) meets the \(y\)-axis at \(P\); the scyphoid is the locus of the intersections of the line perpendicular to \(AP\) through \(P\) with the circle centered at \(P\) and passing through the origin. No further historical details or independent rediscoveries are recorded. Four one-parameter variants (scaling a frequency \(b/c\) in the sine or tangent factors of the classical parametric or polar forms) are also introduced solely for exploratory plotting; they recover the classical scyphoid when \(b/c=1\) and generate related shapes.

No applications in engineering or the natural sciences have been found. The curve remains a pure geometric object, useful for illustrating properties of rational quartics, triple points, and polar/parametric representations, but without documented practical employment.
