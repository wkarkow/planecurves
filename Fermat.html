Fermat Spiral

A plane curve with polar equation r = a √θ, producing constant area between consecutive turns.

Fermat Spiral polar equation:
\[ r = a \sqrt{\theta} \]

History and Applications

The Fermat spiral1,2, also known as the parabolic spiral, is a plane curve studied by Menelaus of Alexandria toward the end of the first century and discussed by Pierre de Fermat in 1636. Fermat (1601–1665), a French mathematician whose work included analytic geometry and number theory, examined the curve in his spiral investigations, finding that the area between any two consecutive full turns around the origin remains constant3. This differs from the Archimedean spiral (constant spacing between turns) and the logarithmic spiral (radius grows proportionally with angle). Unlike the Archimedean spiral (r = aθ), which was known since antiquity (Archimedes, ca. 225 BCE), Fermat’s spiral grows more slowly because the radius increases with the square root of angle, rather than proportionally, making it the slowest-growing spiral of the classical family r ∼ θn. The curve appeared in Fermat’s work on tangents and quadratures, part of his early contributions to the calculus of variations, predating Newton and Leibniz.

Real-life applications exist in the natural sciences and engineering. In botany, the Fermat spiral models phyllotaxis—the arrangement of florets in composite flowers such as sunflowers and daisies, where seeds pack efficiently at angles near the golden ratio4. When seeds are placed along a Fermat spiral at equal angular increments—especially when the angle is the golden angle (~137.5°)—the packing achieves nearly uniform density without radial gaps. This optimizes sunlight capture and seed packing, a principle often cited as a natural instance of mathematical optimization. In computer graphics, Fermat spirals are used in generating sunflower patterns, radial layouts, and even distribution algorithms. They provide an efficient way to scatter points inside a disk with uniform density—useful in rendering, sampling, and visual effects. In electrical engineering, the constant-area property enables construction of variable capacitors with linear capacitance change during rotation. Concentrated solar power plants use Fermat spiral layouts for heliostat mirrors to improve light collection efficiency while reducing land footprint3. Antenna arrays based on the Fermat spiral achieve lower side-lobe levels for better performance in radar and imaging systems5. Additional engineering uses include smooth curvature-continuous path planning for autonomous marine vehicles and continuous toolpaths in additive manufacturing (3D printing) for efficient layer filling without sharp direction changes6.

AutoLISP Code

(defun C:FERMAT (/ basept xaxisangle cd sa sb sm om os divcount cosangle sinangle x0 y0 plineents segcount baseinfo divpt xstep paramstep
                   restartflag rval id iq dcl opt kw mode dpr dct span xc yc skm a1 a2 x1 y1 x2 y2 eq_display fa)
  (setq id "Fermat Spiral"
        iq "r=a·√θ"
        dcl nil
        opt nil
        kw nil
        mode 'polar
        dpr "# of divisions around circle"
        dct 360
        dpt nil
        span 360
        kw (ca id iq dcl opt kw mode dpr dct dpt span)
        fa (get_real 2 "a (scale)" 1.0)
        eq_display (strcat "\na=" (rtos fa 2 4))
        rval '(* fa (sqrt (eval a2)))
        xc '(* (eval rval) (cos (eval a2)))
        yc '(* (eval rval) (sin (eval a2)))
        skm '(T T nil)
        a1 0
        a2 a1
        x1 (eval xc)
        y1 (eval yc)
        a2 '(+ a1 paramstep)
        x2 '(eval xc)
        y2 '(eval yc))
  (cc))
