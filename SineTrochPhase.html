Sine Trochoid with Phase

Sine trochoid with constant phase angle \(\alpha\) of the tracing arm measured from the normal.

Sine Trochoid with Phase parametric equations:
\[ \mathbf{r}(t)=\begin{pmatrix}t\\ \sin t\end{pmatrix}+a'\,\mathbf{N}(t)+d'\,\mathbf{U}(t) \]
Unit normal, phase, and rotated tracer:
\[ \mathbf{N}(t)=\pm\dfrac{(-\cos t,\,1)}{\sqrt{1+\cos^{2}t}},\qquad \phi=\dfrac{s}{a'}+\alpha\cdot\dfrac{\pi}{180},\qquad \mathbf{U}(t)=\mathrm{rot}(\phi)\,\mathbf{N}(t) \]
Arc length and input scaling:
\[ s=\int\sqrt{1+\cos^{2}t}\,dt,\qquad s_{\text{period}}\approx 7.640395578055424,\qquad k=\dfrac{s_{\text{period}}}{2\pi}\approx 1.21600672342498 \]
Scaled rolling radius and tracer distance:
\[ a'=k\cdot a,\qquad d'=k\cdot d \]

History and Applications

This is one of two modifications of the Sine Trochoid that plots the trochoid of a sine wave. This modification adds a constant phase angle \(\alpha\) (in degrees) to the rolling rotation. Geometrically, \(\alpha\) is the initial angle of the tracing arm measured from the unit normal to the base sine: \(\alpha=0\) recovers the ordinary sine trochoid (arm starts along the normal); a positive value rotates the arm away from the normal at the starting peak. The underlying construction remains the standard no-slip roulette of a circle of radius \(a\) on the base \(y=\sin x\). The circle centre travels on the parallel curve offset by \(a\) along the normal, and the tracing point is held at fixed distance \(d\) from that centre while the arm rotates by the accumulated arc length divided by radius. The phase merely shifts the starting orientation of that arm; the no-slip condition is unchanged. When the user supplies \(a\), \(d\), and \(\alpha\), the code implements the equations above (with the usual scaling so that illustration values match published rolling rates). The finished curve is reflected across the \(x\)-axis to match standard orientation. The classical theory of such roulettes dates to the seventeenth-century study of the cycloid and its variants by Roberval and others; the sinusoidal base itself is a modern illustrative case that yields families of loops and cusps.1 The same kinematic relation appears in the analysis of mechanisms with undulating guides and in the generation of certain non-circular gear and cam profiles.2 Related trochoidal paths occur in rotary pumps and engines, where rolling or sliding contact between rotor and housing produces epitrochoidal or hypotrochoidal trajectories.3

No applications specific to a nonzero phase offset on a sinusoidal base were located; the phase is a geometric parameter of the same family.
