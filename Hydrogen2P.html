Hydrogen 2p

Constant-C level curves of the hydrogen 2p orbital probability density in a planar slice arising from the exact solution of the Schrödinger equation.

Hydrogen 2p orbital wavefunction:
\[ \psi_{2p} \propto r \, e^{-r/(2a_{0})} \cos\theta \]
Probability density (basis of contour form):
\[ |\psi|^{2} \propto r^{2} e^{-(Z/a_{0})r} \cos^{2}\theta \]
Hydrogen 2p Orbital Contour Curve:
\[ y^{2} e^{-({Z/2})\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}} = C, \quad \text {where } (a_{0}=1) \]

History and Applications

The hydrogen 2p orbital arises from the exact solution of the Schrödinger equation for the hydrogen atom, first obtained by Erwin Schrödinger in 1926.1 This work marked a turning point in physics by replacing classical orbital trajectories with wavefunctions that describe probability distributions. The hydrogen atom, consisting of a single electron bound to a nucleus by Coulomb attraction, became the first system for which a full quantum-mechanical solution could be written in closed form.1,2 Schrödinger’s solution showed that electron states are characterized by discrete quantum numbers, with the 2p orbital corresponding to principal quantum number \(n=2\) and angular momentum quantum number \(l=1\).1,2

The mathematical form of the 2p orbital separates into a radial part and an angular part. The angular dependence is proportional to \(\cos \theta\), which creates two lobes aligned along an axis with a nodal plane at \(\theta=\dfrac{\pi}{2}\).2,3 This nodal plane where the wavefunction is exactly zero is one of the defining physical features of p orbitals.2,3 The radial part includes an exponential decay term \(e^{-r/(2a_{0})}\), where \(a_{0}\) is the Bohr radius, establishing the natural length scale of the atom.1,2 Together, these components produce a probability density proportional to \(r^{2} e^{-2kr} \cos^{2}\theta\), which is the origin of the contour equation used in this entry.2 The constant-C contours plotted in the code represent level sets of this probability density in a 2D slice, while textbook “orbital shapes” typically depict qualitative isosurfaces or angular distributions rather than exact level sets.2,3

In practical terms, the hydrogen 2p orbital is foundational to multiple areas of science and engineering. In atomic spectroscopy, transitions involving the 2p state (such as 1s → 2p) produce characteristic spectral lines, including the prominent Lyman and Balmer series, which are used to identify elements in laboratory plasmas and astronomical observations.4 The angular structure of p orbitals governs chemical bonding in molecules: p orbitals combine to form directional bonds (e.g., σ and π bonds), which determine molecular geometry and reactivity.3 In quantum chemistry and solid-state physics, p-like orbitals contribute to band structures in semiconductors and play a role in the electronic properties of materials.3 Beyond chemistry and spectroscopy, the mathematical structure of hydrogenic orbitals serves as a model system across physics. In quantum mechanics education, the hydrogen atom is the canonical example for introducing eigenfunctions, operators, and separation of variables.1,2 In computational physics and engineering, hydrogen-like wavefunctions are used as basis functions in methods such as Hartree–Fock and density functional theory for approximating multi-electron systems.3 Even in fields such as plasma physics and astrophysics, hydrogen orbital transitions are used to interpret emission spectra from stars and interstellar gas clouds.4

Thus, while the plotted contours in this entry represent a simplified 2D projection, they are directly rooted in one of the most important exact solutions in physics. They provide a bridge between the abstract mathematics of quantum mechanics and the observable structure of atoms, spectra, and chemical bonds.1,2,3
