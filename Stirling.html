Stirling Curve

The graph of Stirling’s approximation to \(\Gamma(x+1)\) and therefore to \(n!\) at positive integers.

Stirling Curve Cartesian equation:
\[ y \approx \sqrt{2\pi x}\cdot\left(\dfrac{x}{e}\right)^{x} \]

History and Applications

The Stirling approximation1,2, named after James Stirling (1692–1770) who published it in Methodus Differentialis (1730), is an asymptotic formula for large \(n!\) that extends directly to the gamma function as \(\Gamma(x+1)\approx\sqrt{2\cdot\pi\cdot x}\cdot(x/e)^{x}\). Abraham de Moivre had given a related but incomplete form in 1721 with the \(n^{n+1/2}\,e^{-n}\) factor and an approximate constant. The gamma function \(\Gamma(z)\) itself, the continuous extension of the factorial where \(\Gamma(n+1)=n!\) for positive integer \(n\), arose in 1729 letters between Daniel Bernoulli, Christian Goldbach, and Leonhard Euler.3 Euler supplied both the infinite product and the integral definition \(\Gamma(z)=\int_{0}^{\infty} t^{z-1}e^{-t}\,dt\) (for real part of \(z>0\)), the latter interpretable by an engineer as the area under an exponential decay curve scaled by the power \(t^{z-1}\). The plotted equation therefore approximates \(\Gamma(x+1)\), not \(\Gamma(x)\). This shift accounts for Wikipedia-style graphs showing the true gamma curve near 1 at the point labeled \(x=0\) (because \(\Gamma(1)=0!=1\) exactly), while the Stirling curve starts near 0 there; the approximation is asymptotically valid for large \(x\) and deviates markedly for small values. Deficiencies of Stirling include its poor accuracy near \(x=0\) (limit 0 versus true \(\Gamma(1)=1\) or the pole of \(\Gamma(z)\to+\infty\) as \(z\to 0^{+}\)), the fact that its full series is asymptotic (terms improve accuracy only up to an optimal point then diverge), and the need for boundary clamps or added terms (such as the \(1/(12x)\) correction) for usable plots or computations. Higher-order forms or Robbins’ bounds address this for practical engineering work.

Real-life applications of the gamma function appear throughout engineering and the natural sciences. In statistics the gamma distribution models waiting times between Poisson events such as earthquakes or service intervals in queuing systems. Quantum physics employs gamma in wave-function normalizations and integrals over exponentially decaying potentials.3 Astrophysics uses it for stellar nucleosynthesis rates and radiation transport. Fluid dynamics applies gamma and related beta functions to turbulence modeling and boundary-layer integrals. In reliability engineering and numerical analysis it supports computation of special functions for design software4; Stirling’s formula itself supplies the large-\(n!\) estimates needed for binomial probability calculations, entropy in statistical mechanics of large particle systems, and worst-case analysis of comparison-based sorting algorithms in computer engineering.

        iq "y≈√(2·π·x)·(x/e)ˣ"

        opt nil
        kw 0
        mode 'param
        dpr "# of divisions to x-span of division point"
        dct 500
        dpt '(10.0 0.0)

        eq_display "\nStirling approximation"

        yc '(if (<= (eval a2) 1e-9) 0.0 (* (sqrt (* 2.0 pi (eval a2))) (alt_expt (/ (eval a2) (exp 1.0)) (eval a2) nil)))
        paramstep (/ xspan divcount)
        skm '(T T T)
        a1 0
        a2 a1
        x1 (eval xc)
        y1 (eval yc)
        a2 '(+ a1 paramstep)
        x2 '(eval xc)
        y2 '(eval yc))
  (cc))
