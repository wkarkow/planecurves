Delaunay Roulette

The locus of a focus of a conic section (ellipse or hyperbola) that rolls without slipping along a straight line.

Delaunay Roulette differential equation:
\[ \left( \dfrac{dy}{dx} \right)^{2} = \dfrac{(y+a+c)(y+a-c)(y-a+c)(-y+a+c)}{(y^{2}-b^{2})^{2}} \]
Elliptic case:
\[ c = \sqrt{a^{2}-b^{2}} \]
Hyperbolic case:
\[ c = \sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}} \]

History and Applications

The Delaunay roulette, also known as the nodary in its hyperbolic form, was introduced by the French astronomer and mathematician Charles-Eugène Delaunay (1816–1872) in 1841.1 Delaunay, who later became a member of the Académie des Sciences and Director of the Paris Observatory, derived the curve while studying the locus traced by a focus of a conic section (ellipse or hyperbola) rolling without slipping along a straight line. The elliptic case corresponds to an ellipse rolling on its major axis, the hyperbolic case (nodary) to a hyperbola, and the parabolic limit reduces to the catenary (already covered as a separate entry). In 1861 the Finnish mathematician Ernst Lindelöf extended the analysis.1 The curve is the meridian of a surface of revolution having constant mean curvature.1 These Delaunay surfaces arise naturally in the calculus of variations and satisfy the condition that their mean curvature is everywhere equal to a constant.

Real-life applications occur in engineering and the natural sciences wherever constant-mean-curvature surfaces model physical interfaces. In capillary physics and fluid mechanics, Delaunay roulettes describe the shape of static liquid bridges (pendular bridges) between two parallel plates or coaxial cylinders, a configuration encountered in microgravity experiments, ink-jet printing, and porous-media flow.2,3 The nodary profile accurately predicts the stable shape of a liquid bridge under surface-tension forces, allowing engineers to calculate the maximum stable volume before breakup. Similar profiles appear in the design of capillary tubes and in the study of soap films and minimal surfaces. No other widespread engineering uses (e.g., gears or cams) have been documented beyond these fluid-mechanics contexts. See the Mathcurve link for how the curves can be generated, particularly the gif with “marching hyperbolas”. The Delaunay roulettes are also a natural manifestation of elliptic integrals.
