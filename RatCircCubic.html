Rational Circular Cubic

A plane algebraic curve of degree three that passes through circular points at infinity and has a unique real singularity at the origin.

Rational Circular Cubic polar equation:
\[ r = \dfrac{d}{\cos\theta} + 2a\cos\theta + 2b\sin\theta \]
Equivalent double-angle polar form:
\[ r = \dfrac{a\cos(2\theta)+b\sin(2\theta)+a+d}{\cos\theta} \]
Rational Circular Cubic Cartesian equation:
\[ x(x^{2}+y^{2})-(d+2a)x^{2}-2bxy-dy^{2}=0 \]

History and Applications

The rational circular cubic1,2 belongs to the family of circular cubics, plane algebraic curves of degree three that pass through the circular points at infinity, and is distinguished by possessing a unique real singularity at the origin. The nature of this singularity (crunodal with two distinct real tangents, cuspidal, or acnodal with an isolated point) depends on the relative values of the parameters \(a\), \(b\), and \(d\). These curves admit rational parametrizations owing to the singularity and arise geometrically as the cissoid of a circle passing through the origin with respect to a straight line, the pedal curve of a parabola with pole at the origin, the envelope of circles having diameters from the origin to points on that parabola, or the inverse (with respect to a point on the conic) of a conic. Special cases include the conchoid of de Sluze3 (a right cubic studied in 1662 by Belgian mathematician and cleric René-François de Sluze) and the Maclaurin trisectrix.

No real-life applications in engineering or the natural sciences were identified for the general rational circular cubic in available mathematical references. Its primary value lies in classical algebraic geometry as an illustrative example of singular rational cubics.
