Ampersand Curve

A quartic algebraic curve constructed by Julius Plücker in 1839, notable for possessing the maximum 28 real bitangents and for its ampersand-like shape when the appropriate mirror is applied.

Ampersand Curve implicit equation:
\[ (y^{2} - x^{2})(x - 1)(2x - 3) = 4(x^{2} + y^{2} - 2x)^{2} \]
Ampersand Curve Cartesian equation:
\[ 4y^{4} + y^{2}(6x^{2} - 11x - 3) + 6x^{4} - 21x^{3} + 19x^{2} = 0 \]
Ampersand Curve polar equation:
\[ r^{2}(2\cos^{2}(\theta) + \cos^{4}(\theta) + 9) - r(37\cos(\theta) + 5\cos^{3}(\theta)) + (22\cos^{2}(\theta) + 16) = 0 \]

History and Applications

The Ampersand curve, also known as Plücker’s quartic, was constructed by Julius Plücker in 1839 as an example of a quartic curve with the maximum 28 real bitangents1, a line that touches the curve at two distinct points, like a double tangent. Plücker (1801–1868), a German mathematician and physicist, advanced analytic geometry with innovations like Plücker coordinates for lines in space and homogeneous coordinates, while his physics work included discovering cathode rays, foundational to modern electronics like CRT displays. He served as a professor at Bonn and Halle universities, blending math and experimental physics. The curve gained its ampersand name, due to its “&” shape, from Henry Martyn Cundy and Arthur Philip Rollett in their 1989 book Mathematical Models2. In algebraic geometry, bitangents are key for studying curve tangency; for a general quartic, there are 28 complex bitangents, but the Ampersand curve is notable for all being real3. Self-intersections, called crunodes, points where the curve crosses itself like a figure-eight knot, occur at (0,0), (1,1), and (1,−1)4. For the full curve, use mirror “bottom” when prompted.

No confirmed real-life applications in engineering or natural sciences were found beyond its role in illustrating algebraic properties.
