Ellipse Glissette

The locus of a vertex of an ellipse that slides while remaining tangent to two fixed perpendicular lines.

Ellipse Glissette major-axis vertex parametric equations:
\[ x=\dfrac{\cos(t)\bigl(a^{2}+(b^{2}-a^{2})\sin(t)\bigr)}{\sqrt{b^{2}\sin^{2}(t)+a^{2}\cos^{2}(t)}},\quad y=\dfrac{ab\bigl(1-\sin(t)\bigr)}{\sqrt{b^{2}\sin^{2}(t)+a^{2}\cos^{2}(t)}} \]
Ellipse Glissette minor-axis vertex parametric equations:
\[ x=\dfrac{-\sin(t)\bigl(b^{2}(1-\cos(t))+a^{2}\cos(t)\bigr)}{\sqrt{b^{2}\sin^{2}(t)+a^{2}\cos^{2}(t)}},\quad y=\dfrac{ab\bigl(1-\cos(t)\bigr)}{\sqrt{b^{2}\sin^{2}(t)+a^{2}\cos^{2}(t)}} \]

History and Applications

The Ellipse Glissette1,2 is the locus traced by a fixed point (typically a vertex) attached to an ellipse as the ellipse slides while remaining tangent to two fixed perpendicular lines, usually the coordinate axes. In this constrained motion the center of the ellipse traces an arc of a circle, a direct consequence of the orthoptic property of the ellipse. The two code variants plot the paths of the major-axis vertex and the minor-axis vertex, respectively. This configuration is the mathematical inverse of the Trammel of Archimedes, in which a straight bar with endpoints constrained to two perpendicular lines generates an ellipse. The algebraic Cartesian equation obtained by eliminating the parameter from the parametric forms was the subject of the “Ellipse-Glissette Elimination Problem,” examined in 1899 by E. J. Nanson3 in connection with mechanical models constructed by Professor Peter Guthrie Tait. Glissettes in general were systematically studied by Catalan in 1865 and Besant in 1869.1

Real-life applications in engineering and the natural sciences are limited and primarily illustrative. The general glissette concept appears in kinematic geometry as a means of understanding constrained plane motion in linkages and mechanisms. The related trammel mechanism itself has been used in ellipsographs and drafting instruments to generate accurate ellipses.
