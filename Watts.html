Watt's Curve

Sextic traced by the midpoint of a rod of length 2c whose ends ride on two equal wheels of radius b, centers a distance 2a apart. Watt’s linkage used that geometry to turn piston motion into flywheel rotation while keeping the piston rod nearly straight. The drawing is the polar form, with a p/q multiplier on θ and a Plus or Minus branch.

Watt's Curve polar equation:
\[ r^{2} = b^{2} - \left(a \cdot \sin\theta \pm \sqrt{c^{2} - a^{2}\cos^{2}\theta}\right)^{2} \]
Generalized polar equation:
\[ r^{2} = b^{2} - \left(a \cdot \sin\left(\dfrac{p}{q}\theta\right) \pm \sqrt{c^{2} - a^{2}\cos^{2}\left(\dfrac{p}{q}\theta\right)}\right)^{2} \]

History and Applications

Watt's Curve is a sextic algebraic curve generated as the locus of the midpoint P of a rod whose ends lie on the circumferences of two equal wheels of radius b whose centers are a distance 2a apart.1 The curve takes its name from the Scottish engineer James Watt (1736–1819), who devised the underlying linkage in the 1780s while developing his steam engine. Watt required a mechanism that would convert the reciprocating motion of the piston into rotary motion of the flywheel while keeping the piston rod moving in an approximately straight line, thereby minimizing side thrust on the cylinder walls. Although Watt did not publish a mathematical treatment of the curve traced by the midpoint of the connecting rod, the geometric properties of the linkage he invented were subsequently studied by mathematicians as a classic example of a higher-degree algebraic curve produced by a simple revolute-joint mechanism.2

In the standard geometric construction, a rod of length 2c joins a point on each wheel circumference, and P is the midpoint of that rod. When the parameters satisfy the condition a = c, the curve degenerates to a circle of radius b containing an inner figure-eight loop. For other admissible values of a, b, and c, the curve is a smooth, closed sextic possessing reflection symmetry across both coordinate axes and typically consisting of a central loop or figure-eight region enclosed by an outer oval boundary.3,4

The principal engineering application of Watt's linkage was in beam and pumping engines of the late eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, where it furnished one of the earliest practical solutions for approximate straight-line motion without the use of sliding guides. The same geometric principle appears in the study of four-bar coupler curves and continues to be referenced in modern kinematics when approximate rectilinear paths are required from revolute-joint mechanisms.3
