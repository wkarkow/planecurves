Harmonograph

Damped Lissajous figures produced by the superposition of independent damped sinusoidal motions along the x- and y-axes.

Generalized Harmonograph parametric equations:
\[ \begin{align*} x &= A_1 [\sin(f_1 t + p_1)]^{k_1} e^{-d_1 t} + A_2 [\sin(f_2 t + p_2)]^{k_2} e^{-d_2 t} \\ y &= A_3 [\sin(f_3 t + p_3)]^{k_3} e^{-d_3 t} + A_4 [\sin(f_4 t + p_4)]^{k_4} e^{-d_4 t} \end{align*} \]

History and Applications

The harmonograph1,2 is a mechanical device that employs one or more pendulums swinging in perpendicular directions to trace geometric patterns on paper. It produces figures that are damped versions of Lissajous curves. The device appeared in the mid-19th century and enjoyed peak popularity during the Victorian era as both a scientific demonstration tool and a parlor entertainment. Hugh Blackburn, professor of mathematics at the University of Glasgow, is most often credited with its conceptual development following his description of the Blackburn pendulum in 1844; the term “harmonograph” itself first appears in the 1870s in connection with A. E. Donkin and instruments built by Samuel Charles Tisley. The underlying principle is the superposition of simple harmonic motions. In a basic two-pendulum lateral harmonograph, one pendulum drives motion along the x-axis while the other drives motion along the y-axis. Friction and air resistance introduce damping, so the amplitude decreases over time and the curve spirals inward to a point. The AutoLISP code generalizes this physical behavior by allowing each axis to be the sum of two damped sinusoidal terms, each with its own amplitude, frequency, phase shift, optional power (exponent) on the sine, and damping coefficient. For engineers, architects, and draftsmen the key practical insight is that frequency ratios determine the overall shape while the damping and exponents control how quickly the figure decays and how sharply the lobes are defined—concepts directly analogous to the decaying exponential factors and power-law modifications encountered in basic vibration analysis.

Real-life applications are centered in the natural sciences and engineering education. Harmonographs provide a direct, mechanical illustration of damped harmonic oscillators and wave superposition, making abstract ideas of phase relationships and frequency ratios visible without electronic equipment. The same parametric forms (with or without damping) appear on oscilloscopes when two sinusoidal signals are fed to the X and Y inputs; this technique remains a standard method for comparing frequencies and measuring phase differences in electronics, signal processing, and vibration analysis.3 No additional widespread industrial or scientific applications beyond these demonstration and measurement uses have been documented.
