GenPolar Curve

An experimental twenty-parameter generalized polar form constructed as the sum of two independent powered trigonometric terms.

GenPolar equation:
\[ r = \dfrac{(a_1 + b_1\sin(c_1\theta + d_1))^{e_1}}{(a_2 + b_2\cos(c_2\theta + d_2))^{e_2}} + \dfrac{(a_3 + b_3\sin(c_3\theta + d_3))^{e_3}}{(a_4 + b_4\cos(c_4\theta + d_4))^{e_4}} \]

History and Applications

The GenPolar equation is an experimental generalized polar form developed specifically for curve exploration in AutoCAD. It is constructed as the sum of two independent powered trigonometric terms, each with its own numerator and denominator, and provides twenty independent parameters that can be varied to produce hybrid lobe combinations and internal offsets such as the conchoid shown in the accompanying images. Generalized polar equations that are not tied to any single canonical named curve are relatively rare in the classical literature. One prominent modern example is the Superformula1 introduced by Johan Gielis in 2003, which uses a single compact expression with adjustable exponents and multipliers to generate a vast array of natural and abstract shapes. Another is the unified polar equation for all conic sections2, \( r = \frac{e\cdot d}{1-e\cdot\cos(\theta-\beta)} \). Conchoids themselves constitute a broad general class: given any base curve with polar equation \( r_0(\theta) \), the conchoid with constant modulus \( k \) has the polar form \( r = r_0(\theta) \pm \frac{k}{\cos\phi} \), where \( \phi \) is the angle from the pole to the base point.3 The images produced by GenPolar include a precise re-creation of the first conchoid figure displayed on mathcurve.com. That reference page defines conchoids in general terms but does not supply the explicit equation for the particular example image shown.3

No specific real-life applications in engineering or the natural sciences have been identified for this exact twenty-parameter GenPolar parametrization. Generalized polar equations of various kinds, however, appear in antenna radiation-pattern modeling, the approximation of certain symmetric biological forms (such as petal or shell outlines), and computer-aided geometric design when a compact parametric representation is needed.
