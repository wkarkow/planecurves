Lemniscate of Bernoulli

A plane algebraic curve of degree four resembling a figure-eight or infinity symbol (∞).

Lemniscate of Bernoulli polar equation:
\[ r = a \sqrt{\cos(2\theta)} \]

History and Applications

The Lemniscate of Bernoulli1,2,3,4, a plane algebraic curve of degree four resembling a figure-eight or infinity symbol (∞), was first studied and published in 1694 by Swiss mathematician Jacob Bernoulli (1654–1705) in Acta Eruditorum. He named it lemniscus (Latin for pendant ribbon). Bernoulli posed the problem of finding a curve whose polar radius squared is proportional to the cosine of twice the angle; this led directly to the canonical equation \( r^{2} = a^{2} \cos(2\theta) \). He presented it as the locus of points whose distances to two fixed foci satisfy a constant product, making it an analog of the ellipse (constant sum) and the hyperbola (constant difference). It is a special case of the Cassini ovals described by Giovanni Domenico Cassini in 1680. Giulio Carlo Fagnano analyzed its arc length in 1750, uncovering deep relationships between the lemniscate and elliptic integrals long before the formal development of elliptic functions by Euler, Abel, and Jacobi. Leonhard Euler extended this work in 1751, advancing the foundations of elliptic functions.

In engineering, the curve is exactly traced by the midpoint of the connecting rod in a specific crossed-parallelogram configuration of Watt’s three-bar linkage, a mechanical device originally developed by James Watt for steam engines to produce approximate straight-line motion; variants of this linkage appear in modern automobile rear-axle suspensions to control lateral axle movement during vertical travel. It also arises as a particular section of a torus. No additional direct physical realizations in the natural sciences are documented in the primary references.
