Richards Curve

A flexible S-shaped generalized logistic growth curve whose shape parameter \(\nu\) moves the inflection toward the lower or the upper horizontal asymptote.

Richards Curve Cartesian equation:
\[ y = A + \dfrac{K-A}{\bigl(C + Q\, e^{-B t}\bigr)^{1/\nu}} \]
Inflection time (when \(C\nu>0\), \(Q>0\), \(B\neq 0\)):
\[ t_{\mathrm{inf}} = \dfrac{1}{B}\ln\dfrac{Q}{C\nu} \]
Logistic special case (\(\nu=Q=C=1\), \(A=0\)):
\[ y = \dfrac{K}{1+e^{-B t}} \]

History and Applications

The Richards curve, also known as the generalized logistic function, was proposed by F. J. Richards in his 1959 paper “A Flexible Growth Function for Empirical Use” published in the Journal of Experimental Botany.1 Richards, a British plant physiologist, introduced the function to provide a more flexible S-shaped model for empirical growth data than the standard logistic or Gompertz curves. The independent variable \(t\) represents time. The asymmetry parameter \(\nu\) (often denoted as the shape parameter) allows the inflection point to lie closer to either the lower or upper horizontal asymptote, a feature absent in the symmetric logistic case (\(\nu = 1\), \(Q = 1\)) and the Gompertz limit (\(\nu \to 0^{+}\)).

Real-life applications are well documented in the natural sciences and related engineering fields. In biology and forestry it models plant height, tree diameter, weight gain, and stand-yield predictions.2 In oncology it describes tumor growth dynamics. In epidemiology the curve has been applied to cumulative disease incidence, including SARS, dengue, Ebola, and COVID-19 case trajectories.3 Additional engineering use appears in pavement deterioration modeling where sigmoidal growth accurately captures long-term performance decay under traffic loading.4 No other mathematical or physical applications outside these domains were identified in the reviewed sources.
