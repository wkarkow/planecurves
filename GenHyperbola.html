Generalized Hyperbola

The hyperbolic counterpart to the superellipse (Lamé curve); larger n produces increasingly box-like branches.

Generalized Hyperbola Cartesian equation:
\[ \left|\dfrac{x}{a}\right|^{n} - \left|\dfrac{y}{b}\right|^{n} = 1 \]
Generalized Hyperbola parametric equations:
\[ x = a\,(\sec\phi)^{2/n},\quad y = b\,(\tan\phi)^{2/n} \]

History and Applications

The generalized hyperbola is the hyperbolic counterpart to the superellipse (Lamé curve) introduced by Gabriel Lamé in 1818 to describe crystal forms.1,2 While Lamé’s work addressed the elliptic case (|x/a|n + |y/b|n = 1), the minus-sign family arises naturally as part of the broader superconic family, recognized in computational geometry literature.3 No separate discoverer is identified for the general-n hyperbolic branch beyond its inclusion alongside the superellipse in superconics discussions.

Applications in engineering and the natural sciences are limited relative to the elliptic case. The curve family (including its 3D superquadric extensions) is employed in computer-aided design (CAD) and computer graphics for flexible parametric modeling of non-standard shapes, allowing engineers and draftsmen to generate smooth transitions between hyperbolic and asymptotic behaviors without piecewise definitions.3 No additional documented uses in structural mechanics, optics, or natural-science contexts were located in the primary curve-reference sources.
