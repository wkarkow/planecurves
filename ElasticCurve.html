Elastic Curve

The elastica, the deformed shape of a thin flexible rod or beam under axial compression or pure bending that minimizes bending energy at fixed length; solutions involve elliptic integrals.

Elastic Curve integral equations:
\[ x = \dfrac{a}{2} \int_{0}^{t} \dfrac{\cos(u)}{\sqrt{\cos(u)-k}}\, du,\quad y = -a\sqrt{\cos(t)-k},\quad -\arccos(k)\le t\le\arccos(k) \]

History and Applications

The elastic curve, or elastica, describes the deformed shape of a thin, flexible rod or beam under axial compression or bending, minimizing bending energy while maintaining fixed length. Proposed by Jakob Bernoulli in 1691 as a problem in elasticity, it was solved by Leonhard Euler in 1744 using the calculus of variations, yielding solutions involving elliptic integrals.1 Euler’s work, detailed in his appendix to Methodus Inveniendi Lineas Curvas, distinguished between stretching and bending elasticity and laid foundations for beam theory.2 Later contributions by Lagrange and others extended it to spatial cases with torsion (Kirchhoff rod model, 1859).3

Real-life applications are well-established in engineering and the natural sciences. In structural engineering the elastica models column buckling under axial compression, giving rise to Euler’s critical-load formula that governs the design of slender columns in buildings, bridges, and aircraft frames to prevent failure.4 In mechanical engineering it describes large-deflection behavior of flexible beams, springs, and robotic arms. In materials science and biology it explains the supercoiling of DNA filaments under torsional stress, where the molecule adopts plectonemic or toroidal shapes to relieve strain. In civil engineering the same principles appear in the design of transition curves for highways and railways, ensuring smooth curvature changes that minimize vehicle sway.4

To use the code to create a continuous polyline where k ≠ 1, accept only the default number of divisions to draw one wave or loop, then specify how many additional strips (mirrored segments) are desired. See also Lintearia, which uses the same equations to draw single sections.
