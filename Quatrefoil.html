Quatrefoil

The four-petaled member of the rhodonea (rose) family, also called the quadrifolium or four-leaved rose; five related polar forms thicken or square the same four-fold outline. It is the pedal and the orthoptic of the astroid, a hypotrochoid, and the plan view of a Clelia on the sphere.

Quatrefoil Curve polar equation 1:
\[ r = a \cdot \sin(2\theta) \]
Quatrefoil Curve polar equation 2:
\[ r = a \cdot \sqrt{\lvert\sin(2\theta)\rvert} \]
Quatrefoil Curve polar equation 3:
\[ r = a \cdot \bigl(\sin(2\theta) + \tfrac{\sin(6\theta)}{4}\bigr) \]
Quatrefoil Curve polar equation 4:
\[ r = a \cdot \bigl(\lvert\sin(2\theta)\rvert + \tfrac{\sin^{2}(4\theta)}{4}\bigr) \]
Quatrefoil Curve polar equation 5:
\[ r = a \cdot \bigl(\sqrt{\lvert\sin(2\theta)\rvert} + \tfrac{\sin^{2}(4\theta)}{4}\bigr) \]
Quatrefoil Curve polar equation 6:
\[ r = a \cdot \bigl(\sin^{2}(2\theta) + \tfrac{\sin^{2}(4\theta)}{2}\bigr) \]

History and Applications

The quatrefoil1,2, also known as the quadrifolium or four-leaved rose, is the four-petaled member of the rhodonea (rose) curves. Italian mathematician Guido Grandi studied and named the rhodonea curves between 1723 and 1728 because they resemble roses.3 The quatrefoil corresponds to the case that produces exactly four petals and can be generated geometrically as the pedal curve of the astroid with respect to the origin or as a hypotrochoid with a circle of radius \(a/3\) rolling inside a circle of radius \(a\).1 It is also the orthoptic of the astroid and the view from above of a particular Clelia spherical curve.

Real-life applications in engineering and the natural sciences include its symmetrical form in the design of compact quatrefoil-shaped radiators for dual-band circularly polarized antennas operating at millimeter-wave frequencies used in 5G and beyond systems.4
