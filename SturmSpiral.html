Sturm Spiral

Plane curves whose radius of curvature at every point is proportional to the distance from a fixed point (the origin).

Sturm Spiral parametric equations:
\[ \begin{aligned} x &= a\Bigl(\cos\bigl(\tfrac{m}{n}t-\sqrt{1-(\tfrac{m}{n})^{2}}\bigr)\cos t+\tfrac{m}{n}\sin\bigl(\tfrac{m}{n}t\bigr)\sin t\Bigr)\\ y &= a\Bigl(\cos\bigl(\tfrac{m}{n}t-\sqrt{1-(\tfrac{m}{n})^{2}}\bigr)\sin t-\tfrac{m}{n}\sin\bigl(\tfrac{m}{n}t\bigr)\cos t\Bigr) \end{aligned} \]
Sturm Spiral parametric Norwich spiral equations:
\[ r=\dfrac{a}{\cos^{2}t},\qquad \theta=\tan t-2t \]
Sturm Spiral parametric hyperbolic equations:
\[ \begin{aligned} x &= a\bigl(\cosh(t\sqrt{e^{2}-1}+e)\cos t-\sqrt{e^{2}-1}\sin(t\sqrt{e^{2}-1})\sin t\bigr)\\ y &= a\bigl(\cosh(t\sqrt{e^{2}-1}+e)\sin t+\sqrt{e^{2}-1}\sin(t\sqrt{e^{2}-1})\cos t\bigr) \end{aligned} \]

History and Applications

The Sturm spirals1,2 (also called Sturmian spirals) are the plane curves defined by the property that the radius of curvature \(R\) at every point is proportional to the distance \(\rho\) from a fixed point, conventionally taken as the origin so that \(R=e\rho\) where \(e\) is a constant. The special case \(e=1\) is known as the Norwich spiral. The ranges \(0<e<1\) and \(e>1\) are conventionally termed the elliptic and hyperbolic members of the family, respectively. The defining curvature–distance relation was placed in a systematic differential-geometric framework in the late twentieth and early twenty-first centuries. Mladenov and collaborators embedded the classical Sturm spirals into a one-parameter family, derived their intrinsic equations, and obtained parametric representations for the three cases.3 Earlier contributions by Duporcq (1902), Mannheim (1902), Balitrand (1914), and Egan (1919) established important connections between Sturmian spirals and Duporcq curves, including the fact that the roulette of the pole of a Sturmian spiral on a straight line is a Duporcq curve.4

The parametric representations used here follow the conventional forms for the three cases. The sign in the phase term inside the trigonometric or hyperbolic function (\(\pm e\)) is an arbitrary conventional choice that arises from integration of the curvature condition. Both the minus and plus signs are mathematically legitimate and produce variants of the curve. The elliptic implementation retains the minus sign, while the hyperbolic implementation uses the plus sign to align with common published diagrams. In the hyperbolic case the curve exhibits a characteristic inflectional transition (a local change in the sign or stationary behavior of curvature) as the hyperbolic stretching term becomes dominant; this feature is intrinsic to the family when \(e>1\) and becomes more pronounced with larger values of \(e\). It turns out that the elliptic member of the Sturm family is the special hypotrochoid whose parameters are fixed by the curvature condition \(R=e\rho\) with \(e<1\). In the Norwich spiral case due to exponentially growing distances between vertices, curve-fitting becomes increasingly distorted with 173 vertices in image 18, out of 180 vertices per π and is much smoother in image 19 with 3600 vertices per π.

Real applications in engineering and the natural sciences are primarily theoretical. The family provides explicit examples of curves whose curvature is a prescribed function of position and appears in studies of generalized elastic rods and in the classification of plane curves with curvature depending only on distance from a fixed point.5 Concrete practical uses remain modest compared with the clothoid or logarithmic spiral, although the curvature-distance relation has conceptual value in problems involving variable-stiffness beams and in certain trajectory or optical-design contexts where curvature must scale with radial distance.
